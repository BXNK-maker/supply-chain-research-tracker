Abstract
National freight transport models usually treat prices and service provision as exogenous. This paper models both as outcomes of strategic interaction among operators of scheduled, loop-based freight services. It compares oligopolistic competition, monopoly, Ramsey pricing with service-level or system-wide cost recovery, and welfare-maximising planning. Shippers across multiple segments choose between direct cyclic liner services and an outside option, using logit demand. Operators select from candidate port rotations, vessel classes and speeds, and set origin–destination prices subject to leg capacities.
Under competition, each operator offers one service. Equilibrium requires that no operator can profitably enter, exit or replace its service after prices re-equilibrate. The solution method computes global pricing best responses and tests all unilateral service deviations, pruning only those excluded by an analytical profit bound. All regimes use the same demand and cost structure.
In a synthetic Baltic–North Sea application, multinomial logit competition sustains more overlapping services and lower prices than monopoly. System-wide Ramsey pricing captures most of the planner’s welfare gain without a deficit. Fuel-price and demand shocks affect competitive prices through changes in the service set. When maritime services are close substitutes, entrants avoid overlapping markets and competition can reproduce monopoly outcomes. The results illustrate mechanisms rather than forecast actual markets, showing how market organisation shapes service provision, prices, modal shares and welfare.

1
Introduction
Freight transport models usually take transport prices and service supply as given. Operating costs determine which services are feasible, but market prices also reflect demand elasticity, service quality, capacity scarcity and competitive conduct, and the set of services offered is itself the outcome of operators’ decisions. This paper develops a strategic freight-market model for loop-based scheduled transport services in which both prices and service provision are endogenous. The work is part of the HERMES project (High Resolution Freight Network Assignment for Swedish Conditions).
In the model, shippers allocate exogenous origin–destination (OD) demand across differentiated liner services and an outside option. Operators set OD-specific prices based on leg capacities and may enter, exit, or replace their service with another candidate service. The same demand, cost, and capacity system is solved under competition, monopoly, Ramsey pricing, and a social planner, making the regimes directly comparable.
The model is applied to a synthetic Baltic–North Sea network. The synthetic setting isolates the economic mechanisms of interest and allows transparent comparisons across regimes and scenarios. The numerical results are model experiments, not estimates of observed freight rates, modal shares or operator behaviour.
1.1
Background
Freight transport is frequently organised in loops, rotations, and round trips rather than as independent point-to-point movements. A vehicle or vessel may serve several loading and unloading locations within a single continuous cycle, while different freight flows share capacity along different parts of the same route. Consolidation, backhaul, and repeated service patterns can increase vehicle utilisation and create economies of scale. These structures occur in both local distribution systems and international liner shipping. Empirical work on vessel movements in Swedish waters, for example, documents recurring multi-port patterns in which the same vessel loads and unloads at several calls
(
Johansson et al., 2021
)
.
Representing such movements explicitly is important because the profitability and capacity use of one freight relation depend on the other flows carried by the same service. Loop-based network representations can therefore describe consolidation, return movements, and shared capacity more naturally than models in which each relation is treated as a separate direct shipment
(
Asplund and Westin, 2017
;
Westin et al., 2024
;
Westin et al., 2026
)
. At the same time, the number of feasible loops grows rapidly with the number of locations, vessel classes, speeds, and schedules. This creates a direct connection between the economic problem of service provision and the computational literature on liner-shipping network design
(
Brouer et al., 2014a
;
Brouer et al., 2014b
;
Koza et al., 2020
;
Christiansen et al., 2020
)
.
National freight models provide detailed representations of commodity flows, shipment size, transport chains, terminal use, mode choice, and network assignment. The Swedish Samgods system, for example, follows an aggregate–disaggregate–aggregate structure in which aggregate production–consumption flows are disaggregated into firm-to-firm relations and assigned to logistics alternatives based on transport and inventory costs
(
Ben-Akiva and de Jong, 2013
;
de Jong and Baak, 2024
;
Trafikverket, 2026
)
. Stochastic extensions improve the behavioural representation by allocating demand probabilistically across logistics alternatives rather than assigning all flow to the minimum-cost option
(
Abate et al., 2019
;
Lindgren et al., 2019
)
.
These advances primarily concern the demand and logistics-choice side of the model. Transport-service supply, freight rates, and strategic interaction among operators are usually represented more simply. This is a consequential limitation when operators serve overlapping markets. Operating costs determine whether a service is technically and commercially feasible, but market prices also reflect demand elasticity, service quality, capacity scarcity, ownership, and competitive conduct. A change in the price, speed, capacity, or route of one operator alters the demand and profitability of competing services. Market outcomes therefore depend not only on demand, geography, and engineering costs, but also on the strategic market decisions through which services and prices are formed.
Scheduled liner shipping provides a useful setting for studying these mechanisms. Liner services combine large fixed operating costs, discrete route choices, heterogeneous transit times, shared leg capacity, and overlapping origin–destination (OD) markets. Advanced network-design models represent cyclic services, vessel deployment, speed, cargo routing, timetables, and transhipment in substantial operational detail
(
Agarwal and Ergun, 2008
;
Brouer et al., 2014a
;
Meng et al., 2014
;
Koza et al., 2020
)
. A separate line of literature examines strategic competition in freight rates, capacity, and service
(
Wang et al., 2014
;
Shah and Brueckner, 2012
;
Song et al., 2023
;
Lyu et al., 2023
)
. Yet national freight-demand models, strategic pricing models, and detailed service-network models have largely developed as separate research traditions. The joint representation of shipper choice, OD-specific strategic pricing, leg capacity, service entry and exit, route replacement, and welfare benchmarks remains comparatively uncommon.
1.2
Research questions and contributions
The purpose of the paper is to develop a service-based model of strategic interaction among liner-shipping operators and to assess how operator pricing and service choices affect freight allocation, market structure, profitability, and social welfare.
The paper addresses three main questions:
1.
How can a loop-based representation of scheduled freight services be combined with discrete-choice demand and strategic operator pricing?
2.
How do endogenous entry, exit, and route replacement affect service provision, prices, freight flows, capacity use, and operator profitability?
3.
How do the resulting market outcomes compare with monopoly, Ramsey pricing under cost recovery, and a social-planner benchmark, and how robust are those outcomes to alternative market conditions, substitution patterns and solution procedures?
The paper makes three contributions.
(i)
It states a two-stage game in which single-service operators choose a loop-based service from a candidate pool and then set OD-specific prices under shared leg capacities, while shippers respond through segmented logit demand with an outside option. A nested-logit variant lets maritime services be closer substitutes for each other than for the outside option (
Section
3
).
(ii)
It defines a candidate-set equilibrium and a solve–verify–repair procedure that certifies it. Best responses are computed globally, and verification tests every unilateral exit, entry and route replacement in the candidate set against the post-deviation price equilibrium. A deviation is skipped only when a closed-form bound shows that it cannot be profitable, so the certificate does not depend on a screening heuristic (
Sections
4
and
5
).
(iii)
It compares competition with monopoly, Ramsey and planner outcomes on a common demand and cost system. It shows which differences follow from the model structure and which arise through endogenous service choice, and how the advantage of competition over monopoly depends on how closely the services substitute for each other (
Sections
4
and
6
).
The equilibrium claim is deliberately conditional. It applies to the generated candidate set and the stated numerical tolerances, not to every conceivable route in the unrestricted network-design space.
1.3
Scope
The scope is narrower than a complete national freight-market model. Total demand by OD relation and commodity group is exogenous, although its allocation across services is endogenous. Shippers choose among direct scheduled maritime services and an outside option; transfers between services are excluded. In the competitive regime, each operator owns one service, and there is no global constraint on the fleet by vessel class. The outside option is represented through utility rather than through an explicit supply model and resource-cost account. Environmental externalities and the marginal cost of public funds are not included in the welfare measure. The monopoly, Ramsey and planner service sets are obtained by best-improvement search over the full add–remove–swap neighbourhood from several starting sets, not by a proof of global optimality; on reduced instances, full enumeration confirms the search.
The numerical application uses a synthetic test network. This makes it possible to isolate the principal economic mechanisms and validate the algorithms in a controlled setting, and it means that the results are methodological illustrations. Empirical application requires observed freight rates, schedules, vessel and port costs, service capacities, shipment choices and calibrated demand parameters.

2
Related literature
The model draws on four literatures that have developed largely in parallel: national freight-demand models, discrete-choice models of price competition, models of competition and service design in freight and liner shipping, and models of entry and welfare under fixed costs. Each contains building blocks of the present framework, but they are rarely combined into a single model.
2.1
Freight-demand models
Freight forecasting has moved from aggregate commodity-flow models towards behaviourally explicit logistics systems that represent shipment size, transport chains, mode and terminal choice, and network assignment
(
Chow et al., 2010
;
de Jong et al., 2016
)
.
de Jong and Ben-Akiva (2007)
model shipment size and transport-chain choice jointly. The Swedish Samgods system implements this aggregate–disaggregate–aggregate structure
(
Ben-Akiva and de Jong, 2013
;
de Jong and Baak, 2024
;
Trafikverket, 2026
)
, and stochastic versions allocate demand probabilistically across chains rather than assigning all flow to the cheapest one
(
Abate et al., 2019
;
Lindgren et al., 2019
)
. Disaggregate chain-choice and joint mode–shipment-size models show heterogeneous and non-linear responses across commodities
(
Jensen et al., 2019
;
Keya et al., 2019
)
.
These models motivate the segmented logit demand and the outside option used here. On the supply side, however, prices, capacities and service networks enter as given tariffs or cost functions, not as the choices of competing firms. The models explain how shippers choose among a given set of alternatives, not how the prices and services in that set arise.

Logit demand and Bertrand pricing
Random-utility models link service attributes and prices to choice probabilities
(

;
Train, 2009
)
and provide a theory of differentiated-product competition
(
Anderson et al., 1992
)
.
Berry et al. (1995)
combine such demand with supply-side first-order conditions, the structure used here for operators’ pricing. Computing Bertrand–Nash prices under logit-type demand is well studied
(
Morrow and Skerlos, 2011
;
Gallego and Wang, 2014
)
, and the optimal prices of a multiproduct firm under multinomial and nested logit have closed forms in terms of the Lambert
W

function
(

)
, which the bounds in
Section

use. Existence and uniqueness of price equilibria require conditions on demand, shares, price bounds or costs
(

, and network pricing with logit demand is non-convex even when the network is fixed

Three limitations carry over to the present model. Multinomial logit imposes independence of irrelevant alternatives within each segment;

relaxes this with a nested logit in which maritime services compete more closely with each other than with the outside option. Single-service operators internalise only their own demand response, unlike multiproduct firms. Moreover, because entry and route replacement change the product set, a unique price equilibrium for a given set of services does not imply a unique market structure.

The existing literature leaves several important gaps.
Freight-demand models take supply as given; liner network-design models optimise one carrier or a central system; strategic freight models fix the product set; and entry models abstract from transport networks. The present model sits at an intermediate level of detail. It is more strategically explicit than a network design model and less operationally complete than the leading liner network formulations. Because the service set is discrete, it also needs an explicit equilibrium check: a converged price solution or a local-search endpoint does not establish that no profitable unilateral deviation remains.
3
Model framework
This section presents the model framework, beginning with the economic environment and the representation of scheduled liner services, including routes, operating costs, transit times and capacity constraints. It then specifies shipper demand, operator pricing and the two-stage market game governing entry, exit and service replacement. Finally, it defines the welfare measure and the alternative market regimes used to compare oligopolistic competition, monopoly, Ramsey pricing and social planning.
3.1
Economic environment
The model represents a market for scheduled freight services operated on cyclic transport loops. A service combines a geographical rotation, a vessel class, a sailing speed, a weekly operating pattern, and finite capacity on each leg. Shippers choose among the services that can carry their freight directly between an origin and a destination, together with an outside transport alternative. Operators set prices strategically and may enter the market, leave the market, or replace their current service by another candidate service.
The model therefore contains two interdependent choice layers. At the demand layer, shippers allocate a fixed amount of OD demand across differentiated transport products. At the supply layer, operators determine prices and the set of services offered in the market. Their interaction determines freight volumes, capacity utilisation, operator profits, market structure, and welfare.

4.4
Existence, uniqueness, and multiplicity
For single-service operators with one logit segment and slack capacity, profit separates across OD markets, and a price equilibrium exists and is unique
(

First, when segments with different price sensitivities face a common price, demand for a service is a mixture of logits and profit need not be quasi-concave in the service’s own price: an operator may prefer to serve both segments at a low price or only the time-sensitive segment at a high price.
Aksoy-Pierson et al. (2013)
give conditions for existence and uniqueness under mixed logit demand. Second, binding leg capacities make each operator’s feasible prices depend on rivals’ prices, which turns the price stage into a generalised Nash equilibrium problem that may have many equilibria
(
Facchinei and Kanzow, 2007
)
. The solution procedure therefore computes best responses globally and uses several starting prices to detect multiple price equilibria (

).
The service stage raises separate issues. Discrete entry games with fixed costs can have several equilibria
(
Ciliberto and Tamer, 2009
)
, and because the first-stage game is not in general a potential game, best-improvement dynamics over entry and replacement moves can cycle. Finally, the exclusion of clone entry in
Section
3.7
is a modelling choice. Under multinomial logit, a copy of an active service attracts positive demand, so allowing clones would add one entry deviation per active service.
Appendix

reports whether any such clone would be profitable in the reported equilibria.
Existence of a pure-strategy equilibrium is therefore not guaranteed. At the price stage it can fail when several segments face a common price or when capacities bind, and at the service stage a cyclic pattern of profitable entries and replacements can rule out any stable service set; the finite first stage then has only mixed-strategy equilibria. The solution procedure does not assume existence: it reports a cycle when the search returns to a set it has visited and counts every price stage that fails to converge. 5.8
Numerical validity and robustness
Numerical validity is assessed separately for competitive and centrally optimised market regimes. A valid competitive equilibrium must satisfy all active-service capacity constraints and meet the price-equilibrium tolerances in (
5.5
) on the certificate grid. In addition, no active operator may profitably exit, no inactive service may profitably enter, and no incumbent may profitably replace its service. All entry and replacement deviations must either be evaluated explicitly or excluded by the analytical profit bound.
For monopoly, Ramsey pricing and social planning, numerical validity requires feasible prices and capacities, satisfaction of any applicable cost-recovery constraints, and convergence of the continuous optimisation within the stated tolerances. The selected service set must also be locally optimal with respect to the full add–remove–swap neighbourhood.
Robustness is assessed through sensitivity to numerical tolerances, candidate set construction, and initial service configurations. These checks distinguish numerical reliability from the underlying economic assumptions, which is particularly important when combining nonlinear continuous optimisation with discrete market-structure decisions.

6.1
Sample network
The sample network is modelled on the Baltic instance of the LINER-LIB benchmark suite
(

It keeps the main features of that instance, namely cyclic services, two vessel classes of similar size and directed origin–destination demand, but it is reduced to six ports and ten markets. The ports are Stockholm, Helsinki, Tallinn, Riga, Lübeck and Rotterdam, so the network combines short Baltic routes with longer routes to the German and Dutch gateway ports. Unlike LINER-LIB, the network includes shipper choice. Freight belongs to one of two groups, time-sensitive higher-value goods and less time-sensitive goods, each with its own logit parameters, and every market has an outside option representing road, rail or other alternatives. Demand differs by direction, and the outside option is more attractive in markets where land alternatives are assumed to be stronger. The network is therefore not a solution to the original benchmark but a reduced variant, designed so that every equilibrium can be verified and the regimes can be compared under controlled conditions.
Two vessel classes and three sailing speeds are available. Candidate services are formed by listing every loop that calls at two to four ports, in either direction, which gives 145 loops. Combining each loop with the two vessel classes and three speeds gives 870 services. Of these, 758 can carry at least one origin–destination pair within its maximum transit time, and they form the candidate set

The parameter values are synthetic but chosen to make the mechanisms of interest visible: freight groups that value price and time differently, fixed service costs, strategic pricing, entry and exit, the trade-off between speed and fuel cost, and limited vessel capacity. Prices, welfare levels and modal shares are therefore not estimates for any real market. What the study can show is whether the model responds sensibly to changes in market conditions and whether the regimes produce outcomes that can be interpreted.

7
Discussion, limitations, and future research
This section discusses the results of
Section
6
, relates them to the literature and to national freight models, and sets out the main limitations of the model and the next steps in its development.
7.1
Market structure matters beyond operating cost
The numerical experiments show why the distinction between transport cost and market price matters. With the same demand, costs and candidate services, competition and monopoly select different networks: competitors enter each other’s markets, while the monopolist keeps its services apart to avoid cannibalising its own traffic. Overlap is what lowers competitive prices, and it is also what the nested-logit analysis removes. Ramsey pricing generates a much larger maritime share than either commercial regime while covering costs, and the planner expands maritime transport further only by accepting a large deficit.
These differences would not be visible in a model that assigns freight by minimum logistics cost. Strategic pricing creates feedback between service attractiveness, market share, capacity use, and profitability, and endogenous entry and route choice mean that a cost or demand shock can change the set of services offered, not only the flows over a fixed network.
Section
4
sharpens this point: for a given set of services, the fuel price and uniform demand growth leave commercial prices unchanged, so their price effects in the experiments run entirely through the service set. The fuel scenario shows that this channel can dominate: the service-set response more than doubles the welfare loss that the fuel bill alone would cause.
7.2
Entry, fixed costs, and network coverage
The scenarios show several sides of the same fixed-cost mechanism. Higher demand covers the fixed cost of an additional service and increases competitive coverage and overlap. Higher fuel costs push operators towards slower vessels and make the marginal service unprofitable. Both are consistent with the free-entry literature: the privately sustainable number and configuration of services need not coincide with the welfare-maximising network
(
Mankiw and Whinston, 1986
)
. In the fuel scenario the market can also settle in either of two equilibria, so the outcome of a shock can depend on how operators coordinate, not only on fundamentals.
Capacity scarcity operates in two ways. A binding leg raises the shadow value of capacity and enters prices through (
3.23
). Lower capacity can also change which routes cover their fixed costs, and so change the market structure before the remaining routes are full.
Under multinomial logit, neither happens under competition in the test instance because competitive loads stay below half of the original capacity. Under nested logit with
ρ
=
0.6
\rho=0.6
, competition reproduces the monopoly outcome, and with halved capacity, it fills one leg.
7.3
Substitution between services
The nested-logit results qualify the main comparison. Under multinomial logit, every additional service expands the maritime market, so entry into served markets pays and competition lowers prices. When shippers regard maritime services as close substitutes for each other, entrants avoid each other’s markets, and from a nest parameter of 0.7 down the competitive outcome reached from the
empty market is identical to monopoly in the test instance.
The same substitution pattern leads the planner and Ramsey regimes to choose fewer, less overlapping services. The welfare gain from competition is therefore not a robust feature of the model structure; it depends on a demand parameter that would have to be estimated, and on the equilibrium that the market reaches, since nesting also multiplies the equilibria.
7.4
Interpretation of the welfare benchmarks
The social optimum is an upper benchmark within the model, not an implementable policy. It treats subsidies as costless transfers, excludes the marginal cost of public funds, and represents the outside option through utility rather than an explicit resource-cost model. Its large maritime share should therefore not be read as a desirable modal shift. The Ramsey solutions are more institutionally plausible because they impose cost recovery, but they still presume coordinated welfare-maximising pricing and service selection.
The service-level Ramsey solution uses the competitive service set, so in the base case its advantage over competition is purely a matter of pricing. The system-wide variant gains more by changing the network and letting one profitable service cross-subsidise three others. The widely different budget multipliers under service-level recovery show where the break-even requirement is most costly, and the gap between the two variants grows when capacity is scarce.
7.5
Relationship to national freight models
The model is best viewed as a strategic supply-side complement to stochastic freight-demand modules. A Samgods-style logistics model can generate transport-chain attributes and market demand, while the present framework represents how operators convert costs and quality into prices and service provision. A full integration would require consistent zones, commodity classifications, outside alternatives, capacity feedback, and empirical calibration. The current results do not show that the framework is ready for national forecasting. They show that the economic mechanisms can be represented in a computational model and that the resulting equilibria can be subjected to explicit verification.

7.7
Future research
The model can be extended in several directions. The first is to apply it to larger networks, where the candidate services can no longer be listed in full. This calls for more efficient solution methods, such as heuristic and metaheuristic searches over market structures, faster equilibrium algorithms and commercial solvers such as Gurobi. Candidate services could be generated as needed, for example, through mixed-integer or prize-collecting cycle formulations combined with decomposition and column generation. The bound (
4.7
) reduces the search space by excluding every route that cannot earn a positive profit even when operated alone. Explicit verification of the equilibrium would remain necessary, so that no profitable deviation is overlooked.
The second direction is a more realistic representation of transport supply, with fleet constraints, multiproduct operators and transhipment between services. Calibration to observed freight rates, services and freight flows would also be needed to assess the model under realistic market conditions.
The third direction concerns the existence and selection of equilibria. Pure-strategy equilibria are not guaranteed, and it remains open under which conditions on demand, costs and capacities a stable service set exists. Small constructed examples could show when cycles of entry and replacement arise, and mixed-strategy or dynamic formulations could describe the market when no pure equilibrium exists. Where several equilibria coexist, as in some of the scenarios here, a dynamic model of entry and investment would give a better basis for choosing among them than sequential entry from an empty market.
In the longer term, the framework could be integrated with national freight transport models. The model has been developed within the HERMES project, which also studies other extensions of national freight modelling, including loop-based representations of maritime transport and network assignment. Combining these components would allow transport prices, service networks and market structure to respond endogenously to changes in freight demand, infrastructure and transport policy, thereby supporting more comprehensive analyses of freight markets under alternative market and regulatory regimes.
8
Conclusions
This paper presents a freight-market model that connects shipper choice, strategic pricing, endogenous entry, and liner-service choice. Shippers choose among differentiated maritime services and an outside option; operators set OD prices and can enter, exit, or replace their service; and competition is compared with monopoly, Ramsey pricing, and a planner on a common demand and cost system.
Four findings stand out in the synthetic experiments. First, under multinomial logit, competition sustains more services than monopoly, with overlapping routes, prices 12.0 per cent lower and higher welfare, while cost-recovering Ramsey prices raise the maritime share from 58.7 to 73.7 per cent at zero deficit. Second, cost and demand shocks change competitive prices only through the service set, as the model structure implies, and this channel can be large: a fuel-price increase leads to exit and slow steaming that more than double its welfare cost, and a demand increase attracts an additional competitor.
Third, under multinomial logit, halving capacity does not change the competitive
outcome, because competitive loads are low, but capacity then binds under
monopoly, both Ramsey regimes and the planner. Fourth, when maritime services are
close substitutes for each other, entrants avoid each other’s markets and the
equilibrium reached by sequential entry reproduces the monopoly outcome, so the
gains from competition rest on a substitution parameter that must be estimated.
The methodological lesson is that convergence of a heuristic search is not evidence of equilibrium. Best responses must be global, and every unilateral entry, exit and route replacement must be tested against the post-deviation price equilibrium, or excluded by a valid bound. The solve–verify–repair procedure makes this operational, and multistart shows that several equilibria can coexist. The paper is a framework and computational study rather than a calibrated policy model. Targeted route generation, fleet constraints, multiproduct operators, transshipment, and empirical calibration are the steps towards applied use.
