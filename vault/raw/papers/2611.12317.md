Abstract
This paper focuses on the stochastic and real-time constraints of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) path planning in Search and Rescue (SAR) missions. We introduce a configurable data-driven and intelligent routing architecture that combines a closed-loop probability update with the Random-Key Optimizers (RKO). Our problem is mathematically modeled as a dynamic probabilistic variant of the Open Vehicle Routing Problem (OVRP), enabling the solver to switch adaptively between two objectives, thereby balancing probability maximization and resource minimization. The framework degrades elegantly under noisy priors but not under biased ones: a systematically biased map performs about half as well as flying with no prior at all. Computational tests show that the proposed scheme is scalable and competitive with single-path scenarios in terms of solution performance and serves as a strong decision guide for intelligent planning in SAR operations in unstable environments.

Introduction
Climate volatility is making Search and Rescue (SAR) missions more frequent and harder to plan. In floods, landslides and similar events the scenario changes during the search itself, so the routing problem is stochastic by nature and current search practices struggle with it. There have been recent large-scale occurrences of this type, such as floods in Asia that resulted in over 1,700 deaths, as well as the dam collapse and floods in Brazil. Events on this scale put a premium on how the search itself is run: the techniques and strategies behind it exist to keep people alive while the danger is still immediate
Smart et al. (2024)
.
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have earned their place in this research because they reach difficult ground at low cost
Dantas et al. (2022)
; what holds them back is energy, and the route planning that has to live within it
Han et al. (2024)
. In hydrological disasters time is the resource that matters most
Narayan et al. (2023)
, so our interest is in automating the search: missions that run faster, and victims that wait less, whatever the conditions on the ground.
What the planner produces is an ordered sequence of waypoints for each UAV. Guidance, stabilization and waypoint following stay with the onboard autopilot, so the method does not depend on the airframe: any platform that flies waypoints can execute it.
The flight path planning problem for UAVs in SAR operations involves searching for one or more static targets with unknown positions, the probable locations of which are provided by a heat map (probability density). The mathematical modeling of this scenario depends on the relationship between mission objectives and operational limitations, differing from the classic Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP)
Dantzig and Ramser (1959)
. In one formulation, when the aim is to minimize travel distance subject to the requirement of collecting a minimum amount of probabilities. Conversely, when the objective is to maximize the total probabilities collected subject to a maximum cost or time limit (such as battery autonomy). In both approaches, the probability of a region containing a target is treated as the node value, allowing the system to focus on the most favorable areas.
We adopt the Random-Key Optimizers (RKO)
Chaves et al. (2025)
framework

Since the decoder is the only problem-specific piece, the same solver can run several metaheuristics side by side, like Simulated Annealing (SA), Iterated Local Search (ILS), Variable Neighborhood Search (VNS), Large Neighborhood Search (LNS), Biased Random-Key Genetic Algorithms (BRKGA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and the Greedy Randomized Adaptive Search Procedure (GRASP), all of them reading from, and writing to, a single elite pool.
In this context, this study proposes an intelligent system that repeatedly optimizes UAV routes on a discretized probabilistic search map. This results in a close-loop optimization where the routing plan and mission intelligence evolve simultaneously. The core assumption of this method is that it shortens the time to localize target. Autonomous flight control and onboard perception are outside the scope of this work, we address the mission-level decision of which cells to observe, in which order, and with which vehicle. Related Work
UAVs have also been increasingly utilized in SAR operations due to their capacity to rapidly analyze large volumes of imagery, thereby assisting in the identification of patterns that may indicate the presence of individuals
Dantas et al. (2022)
. Research into SAR operations can increase efficiency and aid in the rapid location of lost or missing persons in emergency situations, even in hazardous areas, reducing the risk of injury to rescuers and decreasing operational costs
Lyu et al. (2023)
.
The work in
Mersheeva and Friedrich (2012)
initiated an investigation into continuous monitoring using the VNS metaheuristic. That study ran into two walls: its solutions stayed up to 26.9% away from the optimum even in small scenarios, and the exact methods would not finish in acceptable time on the large ones. The authors left two directions open, adapting the approach to dynamic environments and allowing base stations with no UAV assigned to them. The work in
Mersheeva and Friedrich (2015)
took up this line and added service priorities and limited energy through the IDIH-Reserve heuristic.
The Drone Swarm Routing Problem (DSRP) approaches the problem in a fully decentralized and on-line manner, also on the basis of multi-agent systems, in which UAVs collaborate through "V" formation flights to save energy and increase the number of victims detected, proving the effectiveness of the method in the Beirut explosion case
Haddad et al. (2023)
. This study represents an evolution of the fundamental model subsequently published in
Almeida Coco et al. (2024)
, where the authors introduced the Probabilistic Drone Routing Problem (PDRP), focused on maximizing identification expectation via the Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS) metaheuristic. Although it laid theoretical foundations, the work
Almeida Coco et al. (2024)
presents some operational limitations by relying on a static heatmap that ignores real-time terrain updates and by lacking exact methods to assess the absolute quality of heuristic solutions. Furthermore, the approach requires an expected number of targets at each node and applies a deterministic offline planning algorithm to solve a problem within an inherently stochastic and probabilistic environment, generating fixed routes that lack the dynamic adaptability essential in disasters a gap that the proposal in
Haddad et al. (2023)
sought to bridge.
In
Pedersen et al. (2021)
, the authors demonstrated that GRASP outperforms greedy and local search heuristics in terms of quality and consistency within short time windows, the work possesses operational limitations by assuming static, two-dimensional maps, ignoring both topography and obstacles as well as collision dynamics between aircraft. Additionally, the model oversimplifies reality by presupposing infallible target detection in visited areas and disregarding the kinematic maneuverability constraints of the UAVs, in addition to relying on fixed parameter calibration that may not adapt to the variability of different disaster scenarios.
Existing literature on UAV routing often focuses on complete coverage strategies, prioritizing the exhaustive scanning of the entire area of interest. Motivated by the successes in solving highly complex problems and the ability of the RKO framework to handle different decoders, this work also focuses on the application of RKO using the decoding strategy proposed by
Festa et al. (2024)
. Further, the majority of these studies rely on static maps, operating under the assumption that the environment and the target probability distribution remain invariant throughout the mission execution.
Recent robotics work addresses UAV search through multi-agent reinforcement learning
Cao et al. (2024)
and evolutionary cooperative area search
Zheng et al. (2023)
, while in-flight re-planning has been demonstrated and field-validated for multi-UAV coverage
Luna et al. (2024)
. These contributions establish that adaptive search is feasible, but may not isolate the adaptation itself, since the iterative system is not compared against the same configuration without feedback under a controlled budget. This work supplies both missing pieces: an exact formulation paired with the heuristic, so solution quality is reported as a gap rather than a ranking, and a controlled measurement of the value of iteration.
3
Problem Description and Mathematical Formulation
The central problem addressed in this work consists of the efficient coordination of a UAV fleet to locate victims in a disaster-affected geographic region within a critical time horizon. Unlike conventional cargo transportation, where destinations are known deterministically, in SAR operations the final destination is latent. This fundamental uncertainty transforms the optimization task into a Probability-Driven Routing challenge, merging the complexities of probabilistic search with region coverage requirements.
Associated with the region is a probability of target presence, derived from a probability map (or heat map). This map is constructed from preliminary data, such as the Last Known Position (LKP), terrain topography, satellite imagery, and historical data of similar occurrences. The difficulty lies in the fact that these probabilities are not uniform; some areas are much more promising than others but may be geographically distant or difficult to access
International Maritime Organization and International Civil Aviation Organization (2025)
.
The region of interest must possess information regarding the probability of victim identification in specific areas. In the example shown in Fig.

Each cell represents an area that can be scanned by a UAV in a single pass or observation, if selected, it is then determined to be a Point of Interest (POI). The dimension of these cells is determined by the operational flight altitude and the viewing angle of the onboard camera or sensor, ensuring that the covered area corresponds to the equipment’s detection capacity. This discretizing transforms the physical map into a structure containing all possible actuation routes (E). Prior to the optimization phase, a network of feasible and safe flight corridors is generated to connect the identified POIs. The connections-graph is formed by the computer vision-based ground risk analysis algorithm introduced by
de Andrade et al. (2025)
. Fundamentally, this paradigm allows the mission operator to explicitly tune of the cost function, setting a tunable tradeoff between maximizing path safety (risk avoidance) and minimizing travel distance (operational efficiency).
The UAV fleet is assumed to be homogeneous, with no provisions of energy harvesting or recharging. Energy is not only spent in displacing between points, but also during observations of the cells. The problem dictates that the energy consumed in a route cannot exceed the aircraft’s maximum capacity.
A distinctive feature of this problem, in contrast to static approaches, is its random characteristic. The search environment changes as the operation progresses. When a UAV visits a cell and does not find the target, the probability of presence in that cell drops to near zero, which alters the global probability distribution of the map for the next iteration. This requires that route planning is not fixed from the start to the end of the mission, but rather adaptive.
Besides, the problem is treated with the flexibility of open routes. Operationally, this means UAVs are not required to spend energy returning to the original launch base at the end of each scanning cycle. They can terminate their current route at a strategic point in the field, maximizing useful search time in high-probability areas and minimizing unproductive displacements. Additional UAVs can also be easily added to the search at each iteration.
Route revisitation (overlap) becomes relevant whenever POI with high probability justify being visited more than once. These recurring visits can be performed by distinct UAVs, depending on routing optimization, imposed restrictions, and the determination of the operation manager, allowing for scanning by different search operators.
Therefore, the problem is summarized as deciding which sequence of cells each UAV must visit to maximize the accumulated probability of finding the victim, minimizing time and energy expenditure, while respecting fleet autonomy and the need for rapid coverage, which is vital for the targets’ survival.

The RKO-based Solution Framework
Our implementation runs several metaheuristics in parallel, coordinated by the RKO. When one of them stalls in a local optimum, as single-trajectory methods often do, it restarts from a solution another method has placed in the shared elite pool. The probabilistic landscape of a SAR mission is full of such optima; every cluster of high-probability cells is one. A trajectory method tends to settle in the nearest cluster. A population keeps enough diversity to work on several distant clusters at the same time, which RKO achieves by mapping continuous random keys to valid routes.

4.2
Dynamic Search Area Characterization
Unlike static routing methods that assume a stationary environment, the proposed framework dynamically adapts search trajectories. Each revision reshapes the goal, balancing what is known in a probability map against the limits in minutes left in the air against ground that must be seen. Figure

The loop starts on the ground: the region is cut into grid cells, the POIs are picked out and the feasible flight corridors are built between them. The planner then reads the mission parameters and chooses the formulation. Model 1 is used when the UAVs fly under a hard time limit or when no prior map is available; Model 2 when a prior map exists and the binding requirement is a coverage floor.
These mathematical formulations serve strictly to define the objectives and operational constraints; they do not participate directly in the execution of the search architecture. Instead, the proposed RKO acts as the core solver tool, providing a unified framework capable of solving both Model 1 and Model 2 seamlessly. By encoding route decisions into continuous random key vectors, RKO decouples solution representation from problem-specific constraints. As updated probability maps or operational telemetry are received during the mission, RKO rapidly re-optimizes the trajectories, ensuring agile adaptation within the dynamic search loop.

Replanning Frequency Analysis
This section asks how often the route should be rebuilt, and what each rebuild does to plan quality and to the detection achieved. Every cadence was measured under two budget regimes, because the two answer different questions. Under a fixed total budget the whole mission gets one allowance of solver time, shared out among the replans; this tells us whether iteration pays at equal computational effort. Under a full budget per replan every planning step gets the entire allowance; this is the regime closest to operations, because between two decisions the aircraft is already in the air and that time passes whether the solver uses it or not.

6
Conclusion
We presented a route-planning framework for search and rescue that turns a probability grid over a disaster area into flyable routes for a UAV fleet. The problem was formulated as a probabilistic model of the multi-depot OVRP problem, in two complementary versions, maximising collected probability under a travel limit, and minimising travel under a coverage floor, and solved with a RKO whose decoder maps key vectors into multi-visit routing plans. An exact formulation solved with a commercial solver provides the reference against which the heuristic is measured.
On small instances the exact method proves optimality and the RKO reaches those optima; as the instances grow the solver stops converging within the time limit while the heuristic keeps producing usable plans within the 120-second limit. This is the regime that matters operationally, where a prompt workable plan outweighs a provably best one.
Under a fixed total allowance, slicing time among rebuilds costs more than the new information is worth, and iteration is close to neutral, however could assist with operations defined by the specialist when setting the search parameters. Under the allowance a real mission actually offers, planning time that elapses while the aircraft flies, replanning gains 5 to 7 percentage points. The benefit is not monotonic in frequency: a moderate cadence is best, and rebuilding at every observation performs no better than never rebuilding at all.
With one aircraft, switching from probability maximisation to travel minimisation midway through the mission gains twelve points over the best fixed setting. With two aircraft the advantage vanishes, and with three the best plan of the entire grid is a fixed one. Extra aircraft appear to supply the same flexibility the schedule was supplying, since several routes in the air can pursue different aims simultaneously rather than alternating between them in time. The level of the constraint, by contrast, matters everywhere: within a single objective, moving only the limit carries the score across almost the whole range the instance allows. Calibration effort belongs on the limit before it goes on the schedule.
Noisy probability maps are absorbed; biased ones are not. Independent noise on every cell still leaves a map worth flying with, whereas a map that is wrong in a consistent direction does far worse than no map at all. Operationally, an unreliable heat map is better discarded than trusted.
To the next steps: The planner does not revise its belief about where the victims are, so a Bayesian update of the map after each empty observation is the natural extension. The reward is linear in the number of visits, which overstates repeated observations, and detection in a visited cell is assumed certain; both should be relaxed, together with the two-dimensional, obstacle-free corridors, before field trials. The strategy grid was flown once per cell and some configurations hit the replan ceiling, so that ranking needs replication. Letting the system choose objective and constraint from the state of the mission, and validating the framework on established benchmarks and in real operations, complete the agenda.
