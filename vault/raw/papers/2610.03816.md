Abstract
The Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) remains a central concern in logistics research, as route optimisation under rigid capacity and fleet restrictions directly affects operational performance. Genetic Algorithms are widely used for this problem, yet their effectiveness depends heavily on the choice of solution encoding and on the mechanisms used to preserve feasibility during the search. This paper proposes a sequential framework that separates the evolutionary process from route construction. We adapt Node Shift Encoding (NSE) to the CVRP giant-tour setting and benchmark it against Path Representation and Double Chromosome. We further propose an unsupervised machine-learning repair heuristic that restores fleet feasibility after an unconstrained linear split, and compare this pipeline against fleet-constrained split taken from the literature. Computational tests on standard benchmark instances show that NSE consistently outperforms the other encodings in solution quality and feasibility. The repair heuristic restores feasibility in approximately 72% of cases, and the Linear-Split-ML-Heuristic pipeline proves more robust than the constrained split, delivering lower costs and higher feasibility rates.

Introduction
Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) underpin a wide range of physical distribution systems, from last-mile delivery to industrial supply chains
Toth and Vigo (2014)
;
Abdoune and Boulif (2025)
. Because routing decisions directly affect fuel consumption, fleet utilization, and service times, even modest improvements in tour planning can translate into substantial operational savings and reduced environmental impact
Toth and Vigo (2014)
. Structurally, the VRP generalizes the classical Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP): whereas the TSP asks for a single minimum-cost circuit visiting every city exactly once before returning to the depot
Applegate et al. (2011)
, the VRP must coordinate multiple routes that depart from a central depot, serve disjoint customer subsets, and respect capacity, time, or fleet-size limits
Dantzig and Ramser (1959)
. This added layer of combinatorial complexity places the VRP in the NP-hard class
Lenstra and Kan (1981)
, and its capacitated variant–the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP)–has become a standard benchmark for routing methodologies
Laporte (2009)
.
Given this computational hardness, exact methods such as branch-and-cut or mathematical programming guarantee optimality for relatively small or modest instances
Ralphs et al. (2003)
;
Baldacci et al. (2008)
. For the larger problem sizes encountered in practice, meta-heuristics–including Genetic Algorithms (GAs)–are widely used because they can deliver high-quality solutions within strict time budgets
Vidal et al. (2013)
.
A prominent and effective strategy for solving the CVRP is the
giant tour
paradigm
Beasley (1983)
. In this two-phase approach, the algorithm first treats the instance as a single-vehicle TSP and evolves an unconstrained Hamiltonian cycle visiting all customers. A splitting procedure then partitions this global sequence into capacity-feasible vehicle routes
Prins (2004)
;
Vidal (2016)
. This separation of sequencing from partitioning allows the search to exploit strong TSP operators while delegating route construction to an efficient routine
Prins (2004)
;
Vidal (2016)
.
Yet this sequential decomposition introduces two practical difficulties for evolutionary approaches. First, the final route quality depends strongly on how the giant tour is encoded. Traditional Path Representation (PR) is simple, but standard crossover operators can disrupt useful sub-sequences, which in turn may reduce the effectiveness of the subsequent split
Larranaga et al. (1999)
. alternative encoding schemes are introduced to solve the problem such as: The Double Chromosome (DC)
Riazi (2019)
and Node-Shift Encoding
Boulif and Gharbi (2024)
. Second, when the splitting phase is applied without explicitly enforcing a fleet limit, the resulting number of routes may exceed the available fleet size

. Existing approaches address this either by incorporating fleet-size constraints directly in the split procedure
Vidal (2016)
or by embedding penalty terms within the evolutionary loop
Vidal et al. (2013)
, both of which add constraint-management overhead to the GA.
To address these limitations, we propose a sequential meta-heuristic framework and conduct two complementary comparative studies:
1.
Encoding comparison.
We use the Node Shift Encoding (NSE), originally developed for the TSP

Related works
The Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem has motivated extensive research on two-phase solution frameworks that separate route sequencing from route partitioning. This section reviews the evolution of the giant-tour paradigm, the genetic algorithms employed to construct these tours, the splitting procedures that partition them, and the fleet-size enforcement strategies that remain problematic. We conclude by identifying the specific gaps that our work addresses.
2.1
Giant-Tour paradigm
The
giant tour
paradigm offers a conceptually elegant reduction of the multi-vehicle CVRP to a single-vehicle sequencing problem. Rather than manipulating multiple routes simultaneously, the approach first constructs one comprehensive Hamiltonian cycle visiting all customers, then partitions this global sequence into capacity-feasible vehicle routes. This
Order-first, Split-second
philosophy was originally formalized by Beasley
Beasley (1983)
under the label
Route-first, Cluster-second
. Beasley modeled the partitioning phase as a shortest-path problem on an auxiliary directed acyclic graph (DAG), where arc weights encode sub-route costs and dynamic programming yields the optimal partition in polynomial time.
The paradigm gained substantial traction when Prins
Prins (2004)
integrated it with a Genetic Algorithm, demonstrating that a GA could evolve high-quality giant tours while a dedicated splitter handled the capacity consideration. This separation of concerns proved highly scalable and stimulated numerous hybrid evolutionary metaheuristics, surveyed comprehensively by Prins et al.
Prins et al. (2014)
. The core insight is that the GA can exploit powerful TSP methods and simple permutation-based encodings, while the splitter guarantees feasibility.
2.2
Genetic Algorithms for Giant-Tour construction
Genetic Algorithms have become the metaheuristic of choice for giant-tour construction due to their population-based exploration and straightforward adaptation to permutation spaces

;
Holland (1992)

The dominant encoding is the Path Representation (PR), which represents a giant tour as a direct permutation of customer indices. However, standard crossover and mutation operators applied to PR frequently produce invalid offspring: duplicate customer visits and omitted nodes violate the Hamiltonian cycle constraint.
Multiple corrective strategies have been proposed including: death penalties, reinitialization, or complex restoration method are introduced to restore feasibility.
Two important research directions emerged to resolve this problem. The first direction focused on designing specialized genetic operators tailored to permutation spaces. Operators such as those of

Contribution

giant tour

Giant Tour construction
We employ a Genetic Algorithm to construct and optimize the giant tour. The GA’s inherent ability to balance exploration and exploitation makes it well suited for traversing the vast search space of the CVRP.

Population representation
In routing optimization, the representation scheme dictates the design and efficiency of genetic operators. In this work, we introduce the application of Node Shift Encoding to the CVRP context and conduct a rigorous comparative analysis against the classic Path and Double Chromosome representations.

This widely adopted encoding represents a tour as a permutation vector of length

By applying these swap instructions, the reference tour is modified, leading to the generation

5.2
Experimental protocol
To determine the optimal configuration for the execution layers, comprehensive experimental combinations were tested for each encoding scheme. The operational parameters selected for the comparative analysis correspond to those exhibiting the best overall trade-off between final solution convergence speed and absolute solution quality.
To secure a statistically valid evaluation, each representation model—namely Path Representation, Double Chromosome, and Node Shift Encoding—was executed independently 30 times per benchmark instance, mitigating the stochastic variation inherent to Genetic Algorithms.
The individual runs yielded a distribution of both feasible and infeasible routing structures. Infraction of solution feasibility typically arose when the total number of physical routes obtained during the splitting phase strictly exceeded the vehicle availability fleet size (
K
K
) specified by the instance data. To address this structural issue, the Machine Learning post-processing repair heuristic detailed in Section
4.3
was triggered to restore solution compliance by reallocating customers from randomly selected
m
m
routes into active clusters based on proximity and capacity. When spatial containment constraints make repair impossible, an experimentally derived upper bound was employed to penalize the solution.
5.3
Implementation environment
The metaheuristic framework was developed and compiled in C# within the .NET framework environment. All experimental evaluations were executed on a dedicated Intel Core i3 processor operating at 2.00 GHz with 4 GB of RAM. The numerical findings obtained under these controlled operating conditions are presented in the section that follows.

7
Discussion
The results point to a clear conclusion about what works in this sequential framework. The most obvious finding is that Node Shift Encoding consistently outperforms both Path Representation and Double Chromosome. Across the benchmark set, NSE returned better tour costs and produced sequences that were easier to split into a valid number of routes. The reason is straightforward: because NSE manipulates relative shifts rather than absolute positions, the genetic operators tend to preserve useful adjacency patterns between customers. Standard crossover on a direct permutation often tears apart promising sub-sequences, and while Double Chromosome improves on this, it still does not keep the same level of relative ordering intact during decoding. The difference shows up clearly in the

Turning to the repair heuristic, the Machine Learning post-procedure seems to fill the gap we identified in the classical linear split. When unconstrained splitter returns more than K routes, the ML Heuristic steps in and manages to restore feasibility in approximately 72% of the evaluated cases. In machine learning heuristic, the centroids are updated continuously after every insertion, so the cluster model learns the new spatial layout of each route on the fly. This dynamic reassignment is essentially an online clustering process, and it works better than a static nearest-centroid rule because it adapts to the changing shape of partial routes.
The comparison with the constrained split is revealing. One might expect that enforcing the fleet limit directly inside the dynamic program (DP) would be the safer approach, but our experiments show otherwise. The constrained split is valid in theory, yet it becomes fragile when the giant tour simply does not contain enough good breakpoints to produce exactly K feasible segments. When that happens, the DP exhausts its state space and falls back on penalty-dominated solutions, which pushes the average cost up dramatically. Our approach takes a different path: we let the split optimize freely for distance, except that we might get too many routes, and then try to fix the fleet size afterward. This pays off. The Linear Split plus ML Heuristic pipeline delivers lower best and average costs and higher feasibility counts on most instances because it does not throw away high-quality partitions that happen to use one or two vehicles too many.
That said, we should be honest about where the method struggles. On a handful of instances, particularly those with very tight capacities and large individual demands, the ML Heuristic fails to recover any feasible solution. In these cases, spatial proximity is simply not enough. The capacity constraints are so strict that the problem resembles a bin-packing task more than a routing task, and no amount of centroid-guided reallocation can fit the customers into K routes without breaking the load limits. This difficulty is also tied to the ordering of customers in the giant tour. When high-demand customers are poorly sequenced, the routes fill up too quickly, leaving the repair heuristic with little room to maneuver. We also noticed that the decoding time for NSE grows on the largest instances. On P-n101-k4, for example, the framework takes roughly 66 seconds compared to about 28 seconds under Path Representation. However, this increase is modest in absolute terms and, more importantly, it is time well spent. On several medium-to-large instances, GLPK failed to return any feasible solution within a four-hour limit, whereas our pipeline consistently produced valid tours with reasonable costs. When we weigh an extra thirty or forty seconds against the ability to obtain a workable routing plan for problems that exact methods cannot even touch, the trade-off is entirely acceptable. This makes the framework particularly relevant for operational settings where planners need a workable route plan within minutes rather than hours, and where the ability to obtain a reliable solution quickly outweighs the need to prove optimality.
From a practical standpoint, the main advantage of our pipeline is its modularity. The GA can focus on what it does best, which is evolving short Hamiltonian cycles, while the splitting and repair layers handle the operational constraints. This separation makes the code easier to maintain and adapt. If a logistics company later needs to add time windows or heterogeneous fleets, the evolutionary core stays the same; only the insertion rules and capacity checks in the repair phase need adjustment.
Despite these encouraging results, there is still ample room to improve the proposed architecture. First, to address instances with poor recovery rates, replacing the random selection of routes to dismantle during repair is an obvious candidate for refinement. Such an improvement must be achieved without adding complexity to the fitness function. Second, adopting adaptive GA parameters could be beneficial. Indeed, crossover and mutation rates could be dynamically adjusted during the search rather than kept static. Finally, the framework currently optimizes total distance alone, but in practice planners often care about route balance. Extending the objective to include driver workload equity would make the approach more attractive for real-world deployment.
8
Conclusion and future directions
This paper proposes a sequential framework for the CVRP that combines Node Shift Encoding (NSE) with a post-split machine learning repair heuristic within a Giant Tour approach. Computational experiments on well-known benchmarks, supported by statistical evidence, demonstrate that NSE outperforms both Path Representation and Double Chromosome in solution quality and fleet feasibility. The proposed ML heuristic strengthens NSE’s superiority by restoring feasibility in approximately 72% of infeasible cases, while the combined Linear Split and ML heuristic pipeline proves more robust than state-of-the-art literature methods in terms of solution cost and feasibility rate (72.4% vs 44.6%). By separating route sequencing from constraint handling, the framework offers a modular and practical approach that is easy to adapt to richer operational settings. Overall, these results suggest that the proposed pipeline is a viable alternative to embedding hard fleet constraints directly within the genetic algorithm. Future work may explore smarter dismantling strategies during repair and multi-objective extensions that balance total distance with route equity.
