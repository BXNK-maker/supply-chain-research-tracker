Abstract
This work focuses on planning daily routes for a group of employees of a condominium company.
Given a set of employees and a set of customers with pre-scheduled visits, we want to build doubly-open routes to guarantee visits to all customers, ensuring that each customer is visited by an employee with the appropriate skills to carry out the required activity. The travel times between customers and the service time in each customer are known. The problem includes time windows for visiting customers, each employee has a maximum working time, and the daily working period is between 9 AM and 6:30 PM. The routes respect the lunch break period if they extend from morning to afternoon, which means that lunch break can be avoided if the route is fully completed in the morning or in the afternoon. The aim is to minimize the distance covered by all routes with a penalty for including lunch breaks.
The distances and times between customers are collected via an API to Microsoft Azure; and the solutions are then displayed on Bing Maps.
The results obtained show the operational advantages of an efficient and effective vehicle routing planning in a specific condominium management company. The model can be extended to other service provider companies.
Keywords:
condominium daily routes planning, vehicle routing problem, field service routing, field service operations, routes with lunch break

Introduction
The construction of vehicle routes in pre-scheduled field service operations has long benefited from developments in mathematical optimization. This study addresses the problem of planning daily routes, with or without lunch breaks, for a team of collaborators working for a condominium management company.
In the context of condominium services, efficient routes planning is essential to ensure timely and consistent building maintenance, as well as to preserve a high level of residents’ satisfaction. Ineffective planning can lead to unnecessary travel, unbalanced workloads, and increased operational costs. In contrast, routes optimization contributes to cost reduction, enhance collaborators productivity, and improve service quality, making the company more efficient and competitive in the long term.
Condominium management encompasses a wide range of service operations that are essential for the proper functioning of residential buildings. To meet service expectations, maintenance activities must be carried out efficiently and with minimum delay, ensuring a swift response to residents’ needs. A recurring operational challenge in this sector involves organizing daily vehicle routes to fulfill scheduled visits and maintenance tasks. This is a regular challenge as companies need to serve multiple clients each day, each with specific service requirements and time constraints.
These routes are previously planned and do not include emergency services. When an emergency service is requested, the company uses collaborators exclusively for this purpose or triggers a re-optimization process of the routes, with possible damage to the service that has not yet been provided. Therefore, the problem under study in this paper will not consider the inclusion of emergency services. Instead, it focuses just on planning pre-scheduled requests.
Despite the operational importance of routes planning, many companies still rely on manual scheduling or, in some cases, do not plan them at all, addressing service requests as time and availability allows. Such practices undermine service consistency and organizational efficiency, which can compromise scalability and sustainable growth.
The problem considers:
•
one route per collaborator;
•
each collaborator can cover a predefined set of clients, according to the characteristics of the service required;
•
all routes must be carried out during working hours, between 9 AM and 6:30 PM;
•
each route has a time limit in accordance with the collaborator’s availability;
•
each route starts on the first client and ends after serving the last, so it can be considered a doubly-open route;
•
each client has a time window for the start of the visit; and
•
a lunch break is considered if the route crosses the window between 12:30 AM and 2 PM.
The objective of the problem is to find feasible routes for the collaborators, minimizing the sum of the total distance traveled, with a penalty on the inclusion of a lunch break in each route.
It is important to justify the relevance of the conditions previously stated for companies providing condominium services. In effect, given the technical specificity of certain operations requested by the clients and the collaborators’ skills, it is important to previously classify the clients that each collaborator is able to serve. This way, each collaborator will be able to cover only a subset of the entire set of clients in the instance under analysis. In addition, we are not interested in the vehicles’ capacities but on the capability of each collaborator to make his/her scheduled route, within the company’s time working period and the collaborators’ time restrictions. Furthermore, the daily working period of these companies is limited, excluding emergency services. At the same time, the working period of each collaborator is also fixed, depending on his/her own availability or contract restrictions. In addition, the routes are doubly open, which means that the collaborators use their own vehicle or a company vehicle that is also used for private life. In these cases, each collaborator starts its journey at the first client and ends at the last one, that is, the collaborator can come directly from home to the first client and go directly home from the last client, with no need to pass through the office. This use of a vehicle for work and personal life purposes is very common in service companies. This doubly open feature of the routes allows us to see the problem as a partition of the set of clients into Hamiltonian paths (ignoring lunch breaks).
Another aspect to consider involves the use of time windows on each client. In effect, the possibility of clients being able to set time windows allows them to better organize their personal lives; but greatly limits the scheduling of visits by the company when building routes for its collaborators. This means that the use of time windows can be very beneficial for clients, but very damaging to the company collaborators’ working time, making the process of building vehicle routes even more challenging. Despite these limitations, the use of time windows is an important aspect that many condominium companies seek to adopt, doing so to provide a better service and gain a competitive advantage over competing companies.
Finally, the inclusion of a lunch break is also unavoidable when the routes extend over long periods, covering morning and afternoon. This is an aspect protected by labor laws and is also present in the problem under study.
Furthermore, in our case, the routes must respect a specific lunch break period that is not counted in the collaborator’s total route time. Similarly, the distance traveled between the two clients that precede and follow lunch break is also not counted. For these reasons, preference will be given to routes that can avoid the use of lunch breaks, which is modeled by penalizing, in the objective function, the inclusion of lunch breaks in the routes under construction.
The required data in each instance includes the distances and travel times between clients, the expected service duration at each client, the time windows within which each client must be visited, the sets of clients that can be covered by each collaborator, and the daily working period of each collaborator. Distances and travel times between clients are collected using a Microsoft Azure API, and solutions are visualized through Bing Maps.
The results obtained show the operational benefits of implementing efficient and effective vehicle routing strategies for field service operations within a condominium management company.
The main contributions of this paper involve the discussion of condominium daily routing planning for field service operations from an applied point of view. It also discusses the inclusion of doubly-open routes construction and further contributes to the modeling discussion on the inclusion of lunch breaks in the routes, bringing the possibility of not including a lunch break if the route is carried out entirely in the morning or entirely in the afternoon. To the best of our knowledge, these aspects have rarely been discussed in the literature. In addition, our model uses fewer binary variables than other models in the literature that address vehicle routing problems with lunch breaks in the routes.

Literature review
The study of Vehicle Routing Problems (VRP) has sought to address numerous practical aspects arising from the diverse operational environments in which the VRP has been applied. Some of these aspects are also brought to the problem under study, while others are less frequently encountered. This review begins by focusing on the most commonly studied characteristics (see, e.g.,
[
Zhang et al. 2022
]
,
[
Konstantakopoulos et al. 2022
]
).
Routes of services, namely field service operations involve performing maintenance, installation, or support activities directly at customer locations. Efficient planning of service routes is critical to reduce travel costs, minimize response times, and maximize resource utilization, including specialized technicians and vehicles. Given the complexity and variability of tasks, these operations are often modeled as variants of the VRP, enabling the optimization of both routes and resource allocation. Therefore, efficient organization of these routes is a central aspect of field service management, reflecting the need to balance logistic efficiency with the quality of service provided to customers (
[
Blakeley et al. 2003
]
,
[
Frits and Bertok 2021
]
).
In many practical vehicle routing applications, operations are constrained not only by the limited number of available vehicles but also by fleet heterogeneity and the individual working times of operators. These conditions correspond to a variant of the VRP known as the Fixed Fleet Heterogeneous VRP, where each vehicle has distinct capacities and characteristics and is associated with workers with varying service times (
[
Ahmed and Yousefikhoshbakht 2023
]
,
[
Lahyani et al. 2018
]
). The limitation in fleet size and the diversity of the fleet significantly increase planning complexity, requiring optimization strategies that balance operational efficiency, adherence to time windows, and human resource constraints. Recent studies show that hybrid or metaheuristic approaches can be particularly effective in addressing these constraints, providing feasible and near-optimal solutions in scenarios with fixed and heterogeneous fleets (
[
Eshragh et al. 2020
]
).
The Open Vehicle Routing Problem (OVRP) is a variant of the classic VRP that offers greater flexibility in route definition, allowing vehicles to start and finish their journeys in ways that differ from closed routes, in which the vehicle must start and return to the depot after serving all customers. In the OVRP, the vehicle may start its route at the first customer and return to the depot after completing all deliveries, or it may start at the depot and finish its journey after serving the last customer, without the need to return to the starting point. This feature makes the problem particularly relevant in contexts where logistics involve private vehicles or shared resources, such as deliveries carried out by employees using their own vehicles or when company vehicles are also used for personal purposes.
The main advantage of the OVRP over the traditional closed route model lies in the flexibility in route planning, which allows for a significant reduction in total costs and a more efficient use of resources. This increase in flexibility translates into better adaptation to transportation contexts in which returning to the depot is not always required, allowing route optimization based on the specific needs of each company (
[
Brandão 2004
]
,
[
Letchford et al. 2007
]
,
[
Li et al. 2007
]
).
[
Cabrera et al. 2022
]
developed a metaheuristic for the Doubly Open Park-and-Loop Routing Problem, a variant in which routes may start and end at different locations, without the need to begin or finish at the depot (doubly open routes), and which incorporates walking sub routes performed after the vehicle is parked. This study represents one of the most recent contributions and, to date, the only one identified being dedicated to doubly open routing models.
In most real-world applications of the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), the inclusion of temporal constraints is essential, as these directly influence the real adequacy of the routes. Among these constraints, the limitations associated with drivers’ working hours are particularly relevant, often imposed by legal regulations aimed at ensuring road safety and compliance with labour laws. Such regulations define, for example, the maximum allowable daily and weekly driving time, as well as mandatory rest periods.
These requirements are modeled in specific VRP variants, such as the Vehicle Routing Problem with Drivers’ Working Hours (VRPDWH), which extends the classical formulation by incorporating limits on driving time and mandatory breaks
[
Goel and Gruhn 2006
]
. In addition to labor-related restrictions, it is also common to impose a limit on the total route duration, including both travel time and service time, to ensure that each route does not exceed a predefined maximum value
[
Cokyasar et al. 2023
]
. Although necessary, these constraints increase the complexity of the problem, as the integration of breaks and temporal limits cannot compromise service levels or the fulfilment of delivery deadlines. They also bring complexity because these conditions act like knapsack constraints in the VRP model, making its resolution harder to reach.
The Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) is also a well-known extension of the VRP in which each customer must be served within a fixed time window, and any violation renders the solution infeasible. Variants such as the Vehicle Routing Problem with Soft Time Windows (VRPSTW) and the Vehicle Routing Problem with Hard Time Windows (VRPHTW) introduce flexibility: in the VRPSTW, serving a customer outside the time window incurs a financial penalty, while in the VRPHTW, service must occur within the time window, although the vehicle is allowed to arrive early and wait until the start of the service
[
Elshaer and Awad 2020
]
,
[
Dumas et al. 1991
]
. In many cases, vehicle routing problems with time windows are enriched with other attributes to better answer and capture real world characterisitcs
[
de Armas et al. 2015
]
.
Some papers in the literature discuss the inclusion of a lunch break in each route, being used if the worker’s labor period encompasses morning and afternoon, namely in (
[
Coelho et al. 2016
]
,
[
Bazirha 2025
]
,
[
Khanna et al. 2025
]
). The work of Bazirha (
[
Bazirha 2025
]
) proposes a new characterization of lunch breaks within a VRPTW formulation that minimizes the total travel time, comparing their model characterization with those proposed in
[
Buhrkal et al. 2012
]
and
[
Coelho et al. 2016
]
. In
[
Xiao et al. 2018
]
, the authors consider flexible lunch break requirements, allowing the resolution to decide whether to include or not a lunch break in each route. Our model follows a similar flexible methodology. The modeling characterization proposed in
[
Coelho et al. 2016
]
considers lunch breaks as dummy nodes and sets the lunch period break as a time window as for the other nodes, which means that they apply time windows to all nodes, including lunch break dummy nodes. This methodology is also folloed in our paper.
The need of a lunch break is also discussed in routing problems that address home healthcare workers scheduling (see, e.g.,
[
Liu et al. 2017
]
). However, a less common aspect involves the possibility of deactivating the lunch break if the route operates exclusively in the morning or exclusively in the afternoon. This feature is also discussed in the present paper.
In all the previously mentioned approaches addressing lunch breaks in the routes, lunch pause is compulsory, regardless of the duration of the routes. This is a natural feature when collaborators take a full-day work period only performing service operations in the field. In our case, the collaborators may carry out other activities, becoming more productive if having an entire half-day for those other duties.
Heuristic methodologies for routing problems with lunch breaks are countless. Many of the works reported above and below resort to those techniques to answer routing problems, especially when exact methodologies fail to provide a response in realistic amount of time. The present paper only discusses modeling approaches to the condominium field service routing problem here considered, with no insights on heuristic methodologies.
From an applied point of view, routes planning is becoming increasingly common and important for companies that rely on road transportation. There are countless practical applications in this area, such as planning routes for school buses, delivering and collecting parcels, delivering and collecting money from banks or automated teller machines (ATM), collecting garbage, providing home services, home clinical services
(nursing care, post-surgery care, outpatient care), maintenance operations, among others (see, e.g.,
[
Buhrkal et al. 2012
]
,
[
Liu et al. 2017
]
,
[
Farzadnia and Lysgaard 2021
]
).

The proposed model is a Mixed Integer Linear Programming formulation that incorporates a set of additional flow variables in order to construct flow conservation constraints and flow limits. These constraints allow to prevent the existence of subcircuits in the solutions. The use of subcircuit elimination conditions by means of flow variables is a well-known strategy to build a formulation with a polynomial number of variables and constraints for the vehicle routing problem being addressed (
[
Gavish and Graves 1978
]
). Furthermore, additional constraints will be considered in order to strengthen the associated linear programming relaxation, whose results are reflected in the computational tests discussed in Chapter
4
.

Computational tests
The tests were carried out on a laptop with the following specifications: an Intel Core i5-12500H processor, 8 GB of RAM, and the Windows 11 operating system. The experiments were developed in Python, using the Gurobi solver, version 12.0.2. To obtain the geographical coordinates, as well as the distances and travel times between different clients, a Microsoft Azure API was used.
In all test cases, the default parameters settings of Gurobi were maintained when running the branch-and-bound. In addition, the maximum time was set to 3600 seconds and the branch-and-bound duality gap was set to 0.0001, ensuring consistent stopping criteria across all instances.
In Subsection
4.1
, we start discussing the computational performance of the formulation, considering the additional constraints that strengthen the associated linear programming relaxation, and also discuss the influence of the main parameters that characterize the problem. Then, in Subsection
4.2
, the real applicability of the problem and the model to support decision making is discussed, comparing the solutions produced by the formulation with those built by the company.
4.1
Computational performance of the formulation
Initially, tests were carried out to evaluate the performance of the proposed formulation. For this purpose, widely used benchmark instances from the literature were employed, namely the Solomon instances (1987) (see, e.g.,
[
Dumas et al. 1991
]
), designed for the Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW). These instances are divided into three groups: C (clustered customers), R (randomly distributed customers) and RC (a combination of both). The suffix 1 indicates tighter time windows and lower vehicle capacity, while suffix 2 corresponds to wider time windows and higher capacity. The planning horizon also varies, being shorter in the instances with suffix 1.
However, these instances do not take into account specific characteristics of the context under study, such as company working hours, maximum daily working time per employee, and lunch breaks. In the present work, vehicles are interpreted as employees, and capacity constraints (in the sense of volume or weight) are not considered. On the other hand, the lunch break is explicitly included in the model, which required adapting the original instances.
In all cases, 25 employees were considered, corresponding to the number of vehicles available in the Solomon cases. The lunch break interval was proportionally adjusted, considering the high variability of the planning horizon, which ranges from 230 to 3390 time units and the demanding time windows. To ensure feasibility, the lunch break was placed approximately halfway through the planning horizon.
In total, the 56 Solomon instances were tested, with only 4 reaching the optimal solution or a feasible solution within the given time limit. These adaptations made it possible to assess the robustness of the proposed formulation in a more realistic context, while respecting the operational constraints of the problem under study.

5
Conclusions
This paper proposes a mathematical formulation for planning employees’ routes in a condominium management company, subject to operational constraints that include restrictions on the routes and labor issues, focused on the minimization of total distance traveled and penalizing long duration routes that force lunch breaks. The formulation can also be adapted to other companies that also provide field service operations. The approach proved effective by automatically creating coherent and adequate routes, making better use of available time and reducing unnecessary travel, confirming its applicability to the company’s operational reality.
The computational tests showed the efficiency and effectivity of the proposed formulation for moderate sized instances. It also helped to analyze the model performance and the quality of the solutions under different parameters’ scenarios, from an applied perspective.
The solutions brought by the model also revealed important advantages compared to those usually built by the company. In this way, the model stands for its ability to automatically provide good solutions to support the decision-making process involved in daily route planning for field service operations in a condominium company.
Several directions are suggested for future work: expanding the model to allow multiple employees to serve the same client simultaneously; allowing non-coverage of all customers, with a penalty; integrating daily planning into a weekly horizon; or testing alternative objective functions. The implementation of approximate methods would also allow the model to be applied to larger-scale companies.
In summary, the proposed formulation contributes to the continuous improvement of logistical processes, providing effective and efficient solutions while establishing a solid foundation for future extensions and applications.
