Abstract
Online, same-day delivery operations require operators to constantly revise their delivery routes.
As the routing cost increases with higher customer numbers, out-of-home (OOH) delivery options can be an effective method to allow companies to increase or maintain customer service numbers and satisfaction while decreasing delivery cost.
In this work, we develop a dynamic pricing-and-routing framework for such an online ordering scenario.
To achieve this, we first develop a Graph-Edge-Attention-Network-based (GREAT-based) neural combinatorial optimization framework to solve the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) of varying instance sizes and starting conditions.
This allows us to create an effective model to reoptimize partially executed routes under dynamically changing conditions during operations.
We demonstrate that our GREAT-based solver is fast and achieves competitive solution quality compared to traditional baselines.
Building on the capabilities of our neural solver which can quickly solve several CVRP instances in parallel in batches, we then create a pricing-and-routing framework.
We dynamically solve several candidate routing problems that arise based on an incoming customer’s decision for either home or one of the OOH delivery options.
This serves two purposes:
First, it allows us to obtain an estimate of the delivery cost increase based on the customer choice.
Since our model is fast, it allows us to utilize this estimate to dynamically provide the customer with prices for the different delivery options.
Second, we can directly obtain the new route the delivery vehicle should follow once the customer makes a final decision.
By selecting the dynamic prices in a way that trades off delivery fees and routing costs, we are able to steer customers towards OOH delivery if home delivery would result in a high delivery cost increase.
For effective steering, an estimate of the customer preferences for delivery options is required.
We show that we can learn those preferences online with Thompson sampling, a Bayesian bandit approach, using a Laplace-approximated posterior to recover the missing parameters in order to adjust our pricing strategy.
We call the resulting framework GREAT-ESDD (GREAT for Effectively Steered Delivery Decisions).
Compared to a traditional routing solver, we can increase profit by around 11% under a similar decision time budget.
Further, we show that we can successfully estimate the customer preferences online, allowing us to achieve a higher profit than a static pricing strategy after only 5 days of simulated operations.

Introduction
Last-mile parcel delivery logistics are comparatively expensive
(
Boysen et al., 2021
;
Pahwa and Jaller, 2022
)
.
Each additional stop lengthens a delivery tour, and delivery visits that lie far from the current route are disproportionately cost-intensive.
An effective way to reduce delivery costs is out-of-home (OOH) delivery, where, in contrast to attended home delivery, parcels are brought to a number of fixed delivery locations such as parcel shops or locker boxes.
OOH deliveries ideally permit the operator to deliver several parcels for multiple customers at once in a single stop
(
Mancini and Gansterer, 2021
)
.
However, instead of simply depriving the customer from the home-delivery option and forcing them to use shared OOH delivery,
pricing
can be a more elegant way to steer customer choices towards OOH delivery.
By offering a variety of delivery options at different price points, the operator can nudge customers to select delivery options that are cheap to serve
(
Campbell and Savelsbergh, 2006
;
Yang et al., 2016
)
.
Making an informed, beneficial pricing decision that steers the customer towards cheap-to-serve delivery options requires that we can estimate how expensive a certain delivery option is for the operating company.
This cost is the marginal increase in routing cost from inserting a possible delivery stop based on the vehicle’s current position and its already planned tour.
Computing this cost corresponds to solving a vehicle routing problem (VRP).
In an online setting, where customers can order continuously during ongoing operation, this means solving a VRP for every delivery option for each arriving customer while the customer waits at a checkout page for the delivery options at different price points.
Consequently, we need to solve the VRPs quickly, resulting in a setting where classical exact or metaheuristic routing solvers are too slow.
Moreover, once the customer has made a decision, we can update the delivery vehicle’s route in real time using the computed solution.
While many works exist that use machine learning to solve VRPs (e.g.,
Kool et al., 2019
;
Joshi et al., 2019
;
Nazari et al., 2018
), most of those papers focus on settings with a fixed number of nodes and “empty” starting conditions (i.e., the vehicle starts in the depot fully loaded).
A route is computed once, and the vehicle executes it without any new customers entering or stochastic events happening in the system.
A dynamic setting, on the other hand, where new, additional customers come into the system over time is comparatively underexplored.
This is a limitation in settings such as the previously described pricing context where we need a solver that can support pricing decisions for partially executed routes and a vehicle that is already en route and (partially) loaded.
In this work, we propose a deep-reinforcement-learning-based framework to solve a mixed-size version of the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) with arbitrary starting state.
We use this neural solver, which is based on the GRaph Edge ATtention network (GREAT), inside an online pricing-and-routing framework.
The trained solver serves two roles at inference: it acts as a routing cost estimator during pricing to steer customer decisions and, moreover, it produces the route the delivery vehicle follows after each state change.
GPU-based batching enables us to solve the VRPs for all possible delivery options efficiently in parallel in real time.
The resulting framework is called Graph Edge Attention Network for Effectively Steered Delivery Decisions (GREAT-ESDD).

In particular, our contributions are:
•
A fast neural solver for partially executed routes.
We introduce a GREAT-based CVRP model that can solve CVRP instances of mixed sizes.
The proposed model further accommodates routes that start at any node, not only the depot, with a partially loaded vehicle, thereby enabling the representation of partial solutions that must be extended and finalized. Through its robustness to different sizes and starting points, the trained model can be used to solve dynamic CVRPs during inference by encoding the partial solution and new customers as a new graph and iterative reoptimization. We show that the resulting trained model is fast and accurate enough to be called inside an online decision loop. It is competitive with a state-of-the-art heuristic solver under matched decision-time budgets. Further, it generalizes to instance sizes beyond those encountered during training, which allows us to solve problems with fluctuating numbers of customers over an operating day.
•
A pricing-and-routing framework.
We build a pricing-and-routing framework named GREAT-ESDD where customers iteratively enter the system and are offered prices for different home and out-of-home delivery options. For every arriving customer, our trained CVRP model solves the candidate routing problems of all delivery options in parallel, which yields the routing cost increase of each option. We use these increases to dynamically compute how to select the offered prices. After the customer makes a choice, we can directly obtain the new route the delivery vehicle should follow from the already solved candidate routing problem. Under similar decision-time budgets, GREAT-ESDD increases profit by around 11% compared to a traditional routing solver.
•
Online learning of customer preferences.
We apply a multinomial logit (MNL) model to model the customer utilities and learn their parameters online with Thompson sampling in a bandit setting in order to maximize profit. We show that the parameters are approximately recovered and that the learned dynamic pricing outperforms the best flat pricing strategy within 5 days of simulated operations.

Related work
2.1
Machine learning for static routing problems
A pioneering work using supervised machine learning to solve the Traveling Salesman Problem using pointer networks was
Vinyals et al. (2015)
. Subsequently, papers started using reinforcement learning to solve TSP to overcome the need for labeled data
(
Bello et al., 2017
;
Khalil et al., 2017
)
.
Early works that started to solve the CVRP with such learning based methods are
Nazari et al. (2018)
;
Kool et al. (2019)
.
Their work laid the foundation for the rapid growth of machine-learning-based VRP heuristics.
Besides improving performance
(
Kwon et al., 2020
)
, recent developments focus on solving larger and larger instances
(
Drakulic et al., 2023
;
Ye et al., 2024
)
, creating routing foundation models that can solve several VRP variants
(
Zhou et al., 2024
;
Drakulic et al., 2025
)
and routing on more complicated and realistic distributions
(
Kwon et al., 2021
;
Lischka et al., 2025
;
Son et al., 2026
;
Yi et al., 2026
)
.
A detailed overview of developments for learning for routing can be found in
Zhou et al. (2025)
.
Our solver builds directly on this line of work, however, it has two adaptations to fit our setting: first, it is trained on a range of instance sizes (instead of a single fixed instance size like it is common for other learning-based routing solvers) to allow us to tackle different numbers of active customers over the course of a day. Second, it is trained to start from arbitrary nodes with arbitrary vehicle loads to capture the essence of partially solved instances that need to be re-solved. This is in contrast to existing work that typically assumes an empty vehicle starting at the depot.
2.2
Machine learning for dynamic routing problems
While the vast majority of machine-learning-based work focuses on solving static VRPs, there are some papers tackling settings with dynamic components.
An early and pioneering work is
Yu et al. (2019)
. Using an actor-critic reinforcement learning framework, the authors train a structure2vec model
(
Dai et al., 2016
)
jointly with a pointer network to generate solutions for an electric-vehicle-based CVRP variant with dynamic customer arrival. In their setting, several vehicles are routed simultaneously and independently, by creating a separate graph representation of the current VRP state for each vehicle. This distributed approach is fast but does not optimize the overall setting holistically. Another key distinction compared to static papers like e.g.
Kool et al. (2019)
;
Kwon et al. (2020)
is the handling of constraints. While those papers enforce side constraints (such as vehicle capacity) by masking operations during solution generation (preventing the vehicle from making invalid moves),
Yu et al. (2019)
use soft constraints whose violation is penalized in the objective function.
Joe and Lau (2020)
also tackle a dynamic VRP setting. They formulate the problem as an MDP and train a simple neural network in the form of a multilayer perceptron for value function approximation. Those values are used in a simulated annealing framework to evaluate different re-routing options whenever a new customer enters the system. Consequently, machine learning is used as guidance in this setting only and not in an end-to-end fashion that directly constructs routes.
Ma et al. (2021)
introduce a two-stage hierarchical framework to tackle their dynamic setting consisting of 144 timeslots. At each timeslot, the upper-level agent decides if potential new customers that have arrived during the previous timeslot should be released into the system and a new route should be constructed. Alternatively, the new customers are cached and only released during a later timeslot. Once the new customers are released, a new route is constructed using a simple greedy heuristic and, subsequently, the route is improved with learned improvement operators (such as swapping nodes in the solution tour).
Baty et al. (2024)
interpret their dynamic problem at each timestep as a prize collecting TSP with time windows. They train a neural network to predict the prizes (corresponding to future demand) of the TSP variant and consequently solve the problem with a solver.
Kadyrov et al. (2025)
consider a dynamic setting with stochastic node demands and travel costs. The customers themselves, however, are known from the beginning and arranged in a synthetic grid-shaped environment which is processed by a GNN-based model trained with RL.
Finally,
Sultana et al. (2021)
also considers dynamic customer arrivals and uses Q-learning to train a multilayer perceptron to predict scores for feasible (i.e. non-VRP-constraint violating) vehicle-customer pairs.
A vehicle is assigned one customer at a time only, with a new assignment round being triggered each time a vehicle becomes free. A soft assignment mechanism is used to ensure conflicts (such as assigning several vehicles to the same customer) are avoided.
In contrast to these works, we do not try to learn the dynamic aspect of our problem. Training a dynamic model would require us to model a time component and perfectly match the customer arrival rates during training and inference. Since the exact customer count in different instances can quickly diverge over the day, batching several instances is not possible, complicating processing during both training and inference. Instead, we train a size and start-condition robust model and achieve dynamic behavior through iterative re-optimization during inference. This sacrifices the ability to anticipate future arrivals which we will discuss in Section
7
but makes the solver cheap to train and transferable between distributions during inference.
2.3
Pricing and routing
Combining pricing and routing decisions is an active area of research, especially in the context of attended home delivery and time slot booking
(
Waßmuth et al., 2023
)
.
An early work that investigates how to steer customers to make decisions that are inexpensive to serve is
Campbell and Savelsbergh (2006)
.
In
Yang et al. (2016)
, a multinomial logit customer choice model is estimated from historical booking data to determine the required incentive to influence a customer decision.
At the tactical level,
Klein et al. (2019)
integrate differentiated time slot pricing with customer choice and routing considerations.
Strauss et al. (2021)
extend dynamic pricing to regular and flexible delivery time slots, accounting for the routing benefits of customers accepting greater delivery-time flexibility.
To support pricing decisions, opportunity cost approximations have been used to account for both incremental delivery costs and the impact of current bookings on future demand acceptance
(
Klein et al., 2018
;
Koch and Klein, 2020
)
.
Yang and Strauss (2017)
and
Vinsensius et al. (2020)
develop approximate dynamic programming approaches that account for future orders when determining delivery slot prices or incentives.
Fleckenstein et al. (2025)
provide a general foundation for opportunity costs in integrated demand management and vehicle routing, clarifying the distinction between displacement costs and marginal service costs.
Using an explicit routing approach,
Abdollahi et al. (2023)
maintain routes containing committed orders and forecast orders without assigned time windows, and reoptimize these routes during the booking horizon to guide dynamic pricing.
Ulmer (2020)
uses a value function approximation in a pricing-and-routing framework for same day delivery to price different delivery deadlines and
Klein and Steinhardt (2023)
use dynamic demand management and online tour planning.
More similar to our research direction,
Mancini and Gansterer (2021)
combine private (home) and shared (OOH) delivery options to trade off quality of service for the customers against the company’s operational cost.
Galiullina et al. (2024)
formulate a two-stage stochastic program that selects customers to incentivize toward pickup point delivery and determines routes after their uncertain acceptance decisions are revealed.
Janinhoff et al. (2024)
develop a multitrip routing framework with delivery location options and evaluate how OOH product design and home-delivery surcharges affect operational costs and profitability.
Akkerman et al. (2025)
use a convolutional neural network to predict routing costs and use those in a pricing framework for home and OOH deliveries.
Zhou et al. (2026)
propose a framework that considers both time slots and OOH delivery options and develop a hybrid adaptive large neighborhood search to solve the resulting static mixed-integer linear program.
In a dynamic extension with sequential customer arrivals,
Zhou and Kulcsár (2026)
jointly optimize delivery-option assortments and dynamic prices for home and OOH delivery, using approximate dynamic programming to estimate opportunity costs.
Our work differs from this literature in how we compute the marginal routing cost increase and perform the online routing decision. Opportunity cost models
(
Klein et al., 2018
;
Koch and Klein, 2020
)
and value functions
(
Ulmer, 2020
)
approximate the cost of accepting an order including its impact on future demand, i.e., they account for displacement costs in addition to marginal service costs
(
Fleckenstein et al., 2025
)
, while learned cost predictors
(
Akkerman et al., 2025
)
approximate routing costs from the current state. Instead, we compute the actual routing cost increase by solving the candidate routing problems. As discussed in Section
2.2
, our solver sacrifices the ability to anticipate future customer arrivals, so the resulting estimate is myopic. We only partially compensate for this with a rectifier (Section
4.3
). In return, the marginal service cost is obtained from a heuristic solution of the current routing problem rather than from an approximation. Solving several problems, one for each delivery option, at decision time is only practical within sub-second budgets with a fast, batched solver. Computing actual VRP solutions also allows us to directly update our vehicle’s route after a customer has made a decision. Notably, this would not be possible when using an opportunity cost, value function or learned cost predictor alone.

4.1
A neural solver for CVRP of mixed sizes and arbitrary start states
The centerpiece of successful operation of our delivery company is efficient and effective routing.
Since decisions must be made in real time, we require a routing algorithm that is comparatively fast.
Speed is one of the major advantages of neural routing solvers, which is why we aim for such an approach as our routing heuristic.
In our company’s CVRP setting, we consider asymmetric distance distributions found in time-based routing problems (arising due to one-way streets and one-way speed limits).
As a consequence, we choose the graph edge attention network (GREAT;
Lischka et al., 2025
), which was developed for asymmetric routing problems, as our encoder.
For the decoding, we use the multi-pointer network from
Jin et al. (2023)
.
Similar to those works, we train the model using the reinforcement-learning-based POMO framework
(
Kwon et al., 2020
)
to build a solution tour by adding one node at a time until a complete tour has been constructed.
We refer the reader to those works for the details of the architecture and briefly describe the construction of a solution as follows.
We model the CVRP as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state
s
t
s_{t}
consists of the instance graph, the partial tour constructed so far, the current node of the vehicle and its remaining load.
An action
a

selects the next node to visit, i.e., an unserved customer or the depot, and the transition appends this node to the partial tour and reduces the remaining load by the node’s demand, or resets it to
Q

at the depot.
The reward of a complete tour is its negative cost, i.e., the negative sum of
c
u
​
v

over all traversed arcs.
Validity of the CVRP, such as respecting vehicle capacity constraints, is ensured by masking actions that lead to invalid moves, i.e., already served customers and customers whose demand exceeds the remaining load.
The policy is a neural network, in our case the aforementioned GREAT encoder plus a multi-pointer network, which produces probabilities representing which node to visit next.
However, we make two fundamental changes compared to existing work that are essential for our setting:
1.
Unlike existing works that typically focus on training and solving VRPs of a specific size (e.g., CVRP of exactly 100 customers), we train our model on a whole range of sizes (10 to 100 customers). By this, our model is able to accurately solve an emerging CVRP instance during inference, no matter the current number of customers that are present in the system.
2.
Since existing works assume “empty” initial solutions, the model is only required to encode and solve instances starting from the depot node with a fully loaded vehicle. To tackle the dynamic nature of our problem, we also allow different start nodes and non-zero vehicle loads during training, allowing the model to learn a more robust strategy for different starting conditions.
The start state enters the model as follows.
In addition to the node demand, we introduce a further node feature that indicates, via a binary encoding, the node where the vehicle is currently located.
The node features are transformed into edge features following
Lischka et al. (2025)
and concatenated with the original edge feature which represents the travel time.
The purely edge-based graph is then passed to the GREAT encoder, so that the start node is identified only through this feature in the encoder.
During decoding, the query of the multi-pointer network is composed of the embedding of the node the vehicle is currently located at, the embedding of the depot, the mean of all node embeddings, an aggregate of the embeddings of already visited nodes, and an embedding of the remaining vehicle load.
The start node itself is marked as visited and therefore enters the query only through this aggregate.
The remaining load of the vehicle additionally initializes the capacity mask, so that only customers whose demand fits into the remaining load can be selected.
We train our model on deterministic instances of different sizes and a variety of starting conditions (vehicle load and starting node).
By this, we train a state-conditioned neural routing policy. It does not solve the dynamic problem directly. Instead, at each decision epoch, i.e., whenever a customer orders or a realized demand deviates from the ordered demand, it solves the residual static CVRP of all accepted but unserved customers, starting from the vehicle’s current position and load. The dynamic behavior of GREAT-ESDD results from this repeated reoptimization.
We show this idea in Figure
2
. The figure shows a solution to a current routing problem.
In the stochastic inference setting, it is possible for unexpected events to arise while the vehicle is already en route.
The two different examples we consider in this work are 1) dynamic, new customers (upper branch in the figure) or 2) unexpected lower customer demand.
In both cases, we generate the residual CVRP instance that only consists of all currently active customers (already served customers are no longer considered) and the depot.
We encode this as a complete graph (note that CVRP graphs are complete graphs) where we additionally indicate the current vehicle load, depot node and current vehicle position.
We pass this graph to our robustly trained GREAT model which outputs a new complete solution for the changed setting.
This new solution can then be followed until the whole CVRP is solved or the next stochastic event occurs.
Our approach of deterministic training decreases the amount of resources required during training considerably.
Stochastic settings during training would require iterative reconstruction of the changing CVRP graph and subsequent calls to the GREAT encoder.
This is computationally expensive compared to a single CVRP graph construction and a single GREAT encoder call.
Furthermore, we perform the graph construction on a CPU while the handling of all neural components is run on GPUs, effectively slowing down the overall pipeline considerably (when done multiple times) due to repeated data transfer between CPU and GPU.
Most importantly, however, we train our model on batches of CVRP instances which only works effectively due to a shared instance size of all CVRP instances in a batch.
When considering stochasticity during training, the different instances would naturally be of different sizes after partial solution construction, making it much harder to process in existing pipelines.
As a drawback, however, our model cannot learn any hidden patterns of stochasticity that could increase performance during inference such as not visiting certain isolated customers in the beginning due to further expected customers in the same area later on.

7
Discussion of limitations
As is, GREAT-ESDD has the following limitations.
Myopic
Δ
​
C
\Delta C
estimate.
As outlined in Section
4.2
, the marginal routing cost estimate is myopic, since our solver is trained on deterministic data and cannot anticipate future customer arrivals. As a consequence, early or isolated orders tend to be overpriced, which distorts the steering of customer decisions. A model that anticipates future arrivals could produce more strategic routes (e.g., visit isolated customers only later when more customers in the same neighborhood are expected) and better estimates. However, it would require modeling time and customer arrivals during training, lose the training efficiency discussed in Sections
2.2
and
4.1
, and be conditioned on a specific city and customer distribution. Nevertheless, when a specific customer distribution and city is fixed, such a distribution-aware model would potentially result in better performance of GREAT-ESDD.
Heuristic marginal cost.
Δ
​
C
j
​
(
τ
)
\Delta C_{j}(\tau)
is the difference between two approximate route costs. It is therefore not the optimal cost increment, and the errors of both solutions carry over to the estimate. Our results indicate that solution quality matters, as backends with smaller gaps generally achieve higher profits (Section
6.2
), but we do not quantify the estimation error directly.
Static rectifier
ω
\omega
.
GREAT-ESDD uses a fixed rectifier
ω
\omega
that is tuned offline per routing backend in a simple grid search. While this works (including the rectified marginal routing cost in the optimal price search produces better results than both non-rectified
Δ
​
C
\Delta C
and no routing cost at all), it is suboptimal. Since
ω
\omega
absorbs the expected cost savings from future customers until the end of the day, its value depends on the city, the demand pattern and the operating horizon, and it may not transfer to other settings without re-tuning. Our results suggest that
ω
\omega
should be tuned per backend on a few simulated days of the target setting and that noisier cost estimates call for smaller values (Appendix
A
). We hypothesize that an
ω
\omega
that changes as a function of time, or that is tuned online, could result in better performance, since the
Δ
​
C
\Delta C
estimates become more accurate over the day. In fact, the estimate of the very last customer who orders is no longer affected by future arrivals at all.
Single vehicle CVRP.
Our current framework assumes a single delivery vehicle that executes deliveries in several subsequent subtours. This is because the MDP in the reinforcement learning pipelines of neural combinatorial optimization papers such as
Kool et al. (2019)
;
Kwon et al. (2020)
;
Lischka et al. (2025)
builds solutions sequentially by adding one node at a time to a partial solution. Generalizing to several vehicles routed in parallel requires a different modeling approach, and whether the advantage of GREAT over PyVRP that we observe for larger instances carries over to such a setting remains an open empirical question. However, using only a single vehicle, the number of customers we can serve in a day is limited.
Interchangeable goods.
We assume that all goods are interchangeable, which allows the vehicle to serve a newly accepted customer with goods it already carries. This holds for pick-up operations such as parcel returns, but not for typical parcel delivery by large e-commerce platforms, where every customer receives specific goods. In such a setting, a dynamically arriving customer can only be served after the vehicle has reloaded at the depot. Within our framework, this can be incorporated by masking newly accepted customers in the decoder until the next depot visit, while the pricing logic remains unchanged. The model would, however, have to be trained with this additional masking.
Simulation and model specification.
While our Seattle-based road network results in realistic routing data, the customer data is mostly artificial. Customer locations reflect population density, however, demands, arrival times and choices are simulated. In particular, customer choices are generated by the same MNL model family that the bandit learns, with preferences shared by all customers. The learning problem is therefore well specified, which favors parameter recovery. With heterogeneous customers or a misspecified choice model, the learned parameters would only approximate the true behavior and learning might be slower. Further, dynamic pricing charges customers in sparsely served areas more for home delivery, which raises fairness questions we do not address. For a more realistic evaluation, real customer choice data is required. However, we believe the current evaluation can serve as a representative assessment of the potential of GREAT-ESDD.
8
Conclusion
OOH delivery options can be an effective way to make delivery operations more efficient, provided that customers can be steered towards them. In this work, we studied how the operator of an online, same-day delivery service can use dynamic pricing to this end while customers order continuously and the delivery vehicle is already en route.
To achieve this, we build a framework that dynamically selects a price menu that aims to steer customer decisions and maximizes the expected profit. At the center of our work is a neural CVRP solver using GREAT as its encoder architecture, which we train to solve CVRP of different sizes and starting states. By this, we can call the solver any time a customer orders and solve the candidate CVRP instances of all home and OOH delivery options in parallel. We use the resulting increase in routing cost to select prices that trade off revenue and routing cost and, after the customer has made a decision, directly use the computed routing solution as the future plan of our delivery vehicle. Additionally, we learn unknown customer preferences online with Thompson sampling and a Laplace-approximated posterior.
Our experiments on the Seattle road network show that our neural solver is fast enough that customers are not kept waiting for a prolonged period of time, and that it outperforms a state-of-the-art metaheuristic (PyVRP) under a similar time budget, especially for larger instances. Within GREAT-ESDD, this results in an approximately 11% higher daily profit than a PyVRP-based framework with the same decision time. Compared to a flat pricing strategy where every delivery option has the same price, GREAT-ESDD increases the OOH share from 18.6% to 29.2%, reduces the routing cost by approximately 6% and lowers the average price paid by customers by 3.2%, while increasing profit by approximately $14 per day. When the customer preferences are unknown, the learned dynamic pricing outperforms flat pricing within 5 days of simulated operations. We conclude that fast neural routing makes it possible to price delivery options based on solved candidate routing problems at checkout, and that this cost-aware pricing pays off even when customer preferences have to be learned first.
GREAT-ESDD has limitations that we discuss in Section
7
: the cost estimates are myopic and heuristic, the rectifier
ω
\omega
is static, and we consider a single vehicle with interchangeable goods and simulated customer behavior.
In future extensions, we plan to investigate how the value
ω
\omega
can be learned instead of being set to a static, predefined value.
Potentially, an adaptive
ω
\omega
that changes with the (approximate) fraction of customers that have already ordered can improve the results further, since marginal routing cost estimations later in the day are more exact than earlier ones.
Further directions include multiple vehicles, non-interchangeable goods, anticipatory (non-myopic) cost estimates, and validation with real customer choice data.

Appendix C
Local search postprocessing
We apply an optional, local-search-based postprocessing to the solutions generated by our trained GREAT model.
The procedure works as follows:
First, we segment a solution into its subtours between depot visits.
Every such subtour is optimized independently, which leads to a very fast runtime, since a subtour usually contains approximately 10 nodes only, resulting in a greatly reduced search space (compared to optimizing a sequence over many more nodes).
We note that this is a deliberate choice since we are not interested in applying a sophisticated, extensive search, but only want to improve local inefficiencies in the solution that possibly arise during the decoding procedure of the neural model.
The aim of the search is not to re-optimize more complex, global patterns.
After the subtours have been split, we alternate between 2-opt
(
Croes, 1958
)
and Or-opt
(
Or, 1976
)
moves.
We run this alternation up to 10 passes or until a local minimum is found (i.e., no further improvement is possible).
Both operators ensure the first and last node of the subtour stay the same to ensure the vehicle’s current position (for a partially solved instance) and depot visits stay the same.
Since we optimize the subtours individually and do not move customer visits between them, we can ensure that vehicle capacity constraints stay satisfied.
