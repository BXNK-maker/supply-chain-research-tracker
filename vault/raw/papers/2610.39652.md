Abstract
Long-haul truck electrification requires rapid energy replenishment near freight demand, although dispersed hubs may lack sufficient grid capacity. We study a “Swap-Locally, Charge-Centrally” architecture embedded in a relay freight network: swapping remains close to freight flows, charging can be shifted to grid-suitable hubs, and co-location is retained where economical. A multi-period, two-stage stochastic location–inventory–fleet model coordinates station deployment, replenishment assignments and grid-bounded charging capacity with scenario-dependent battery stocks, batch sizes and incremental fleet requirements. Corridor electrification competes with temporary diesel operation. Building on submodularity-based constraint generation for location–inventory systems, we give an alternative scalar partial-minimization proof that the battery value function is submodular, show that the fleet recourse is affine in the binary swap-station decisions, and develop a Lovász-cut Benders decomposition with hub–period block cuts and a convex battery oracle with analytic gradients and memoization. On a seven-state southeastern U.S. freight network, the method reaches gaps below 0.01% on all five instances, the largest in about four minutes, whereas a strengthened extensive formulation reaches this tolerance only on the two smallest instances within three hours. Stochastic planning avoids the capacity shortfalls of a mean-value plan, and joint planning avoids the battery and fleet costs that sequential siting shifts downstream. The optimized network uses remote charging selectively, keeps most replenishment local, and expands geographic coverage before intensifying capacity within its committed deployment plan.

1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Decarbonizing long-haul road freight is an important part of the transition toward low-emission transportation. Heavy-duty trucks are particularly challenging to electrify because they travel long distances, consume large amounts of energy, and operate on demanding schedules with limited time for charging. Nevertheless, the potential environmental benefits are substantial. Under its Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario, the International Energy Agency estimates that electric heavy-duty vehicles, including trucks and buses, could avoid more than 2 billion metric tonnes of cumulative greenhouse-gas emissions between 2025 and 2035 compared with continued reliance on conventional vehicles
(
International Energy Agency, 2026
)
. Realizing these benefits will require reliable and time-efficient energy-replenishment infrastructure that can support long-distance freight operations without significantly reducing vehicle availability.
Conventional plug-in charging remains a major barrier to long-haul truck electrification. Heavy-duty trucks carry large batteries and operate under tight schedules, so restoring sufficient range within limited dwell times requires very high charging power. When multiple trucks charge simultaneously, logistics hubs can generate substantial peak electricity demand and may require costly grid connections, transformers, and charging equipment
(
International Council on Clean Transportation, 2025
)
. Consequently, widespread deployment of high-power charging may be economically prohibitive, particularly because locations that are convenient for freight operations often lack sufficient grid capacity.
Battery swapping offers an alternative by allowing depleted batteries to be replaced within minutes and recharged separately from truck operations, thereby reducing vehicle downtime and enabling more flexible charging schedules. CATL’s QIJI Energy system demonstrates the technical feasibility of this approach through standardized 171-kWh battery blocks that can be combined according to route, load, and operating requirements and exchanged using an automated chassis-level system
(
Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Limited, 2023
)
. Swapping alone does not remove the grid problem, however: if every swapping station also charges its own packs, high-power equipment and electrical capacity must still be spread across dispersed sites. Swapping is best located close to freight demand, whereas large-scale charging is better concentrated where grid capacity is available.
Research on electric freight transportation has examined fleet electrification, charging-infrastructure deployment, and the integrated routing and charging of electric trucks
(
Schiffer et al., 2021
;
Alp et al., 2022
;
Bragin et al., 2024
)
, but it remains centered on plug-in charging and does not represent modular batteries as circulating assets that are exchanged at one hub and recharged at another. Studies of heavy-duty battery swapping address vehicle economics, battery management, and station-level operations
(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Deng et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
rather than the integrated design of a long-haul swapping, charging, battery-inventory, and fleet system.
1.2
Problem Statement
To address this spatial mismatch, we consider a two-tier energy-replenishment architecture for heavy-duty electric trucks based on the “Swap-Locally, Charge-Centrally” (SLCC) concept
(
Qi et al., 2023
)
. Battery Swapping Stations (BSSs) at logistics hubs along freight corridors provide rapid battery replacement close to freight demand, while Centralized Charging Stations (CCSs) at grid-suitable hubs perform large-scale charging. Each active BSS is assigned to one CCS, which recharges its depleted batteries and returns them as charged inventory, by intra-facility handling when the two are co-located and by batched inter-hub transport otherwise. At hubs without swapping, trucks recharge their onboard batteries on site, which lengthens dwell and may require additional trucks.
We embed SLCC in a hyperconnected logistics network, whose hub-based structure supports shared freight and energy services and the pooling of resources and modular capacity across hubs
(
Montreuil, 2011
;
Crainic et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2025b
;
Xu et al., 2026
)
. Freight follows predetermined paths through logistics hubs under relay-based truck operations, so trucks already stop at intermediate hubs for freight transfer
(
Liu et al., 2025a
)
. We take this relay structure, which is decided in hub-network design, as an input and study how energy infrastructure should be added to it; because loads, trailers, or drivers are exchanged at these stops whatever energy technology is used, the model does not optimize route deviations toward equipped hubs. The coverage and capacity findings are therefore conditional on the given relay structure.
We interpret the system as an Electrification-as-a-Service (EaaS) platform, in which an operator bundles vehicles, batteries, and charging infrastructure so that carriers adopt electric operations without owning the underlying assets, a model already deployed commercially in fleet electrification
(
McKinsey & Company, 2024
)
. We take the perspective of an integrated EaaS operator or stakeholder consortium that minimizes the system cost of electric operation, measured relative to a common freight-service baseline; the allocation of costs among individual stakeholders lies outside the scope of the study. The operator’s decisions are coupled through two mechanisms. First, the facility configuration determines whether trucks swap batteries or recharge on site, and hence hub dwell times and fleet requirements. Second, BSS-to-CCS assignments determine replenishment lead times, which shape battery turnaround, stock requirements, batch sizes, and transshipment costs; concentrating charging at grid-suitable hubs reduces infrastructure duplication but lengthens these replenishment cycles.
Uncertainty in long-haul freight demand amplifies this coordination problem. Facility activation, BSS-to-CCS assignments, charging capacity, and corridor electrification levels must be committed before demand is observed, whereas battery stocks, replenishment batch sizes, and fleet capacity depend on the realized demand. Planning for expected demand may leave battery or fleet capacity short under high demand, while planning for peak demand over-invests. We therefore formulate a two-stage stochastic location, inventory, and fleet-planning model that minimizes the expected incremental system cost subject to approximate per-location battery cycle-service targets, offered-load fleet margins, and charging-energy limits in every demand scenario. Corridor electrification is treated as an economic decision rather than a mandate: demand on corridors not yet equipped for electric service is met by diesel operation at a substitution premium, so the model weighs early infrastructure commitment against temporary diesel continuation under each adoption trajectory.
The closest location–inventory foundation is the joint location and repairable-inventory model of
Qi et al. (2023)
for a static urban SLCC network, which takes the swapping stations as given and represents centralized charging through charging-time parameters rather than the explicit hub-level power budgets considered here. In a long-haul freight network, freight-path demand drives both battery requirements at the hubs and truck workload, and the swap-or-charge choice at each hub ties infrastructure to truck dwell and hence to fleet size, which turns the location–inventory problem into a location–inventory–fleet problem.
Xu et al. (2024)
study station deployment, freight consolidation and routing, battery inventory, and transshipment within hyperconnected hub networks.
The gap addressed here concerns planning BSS and CCS activation, BSS-to-CCS assignment, and grid-bounded charging capacity jointly with circulating battery stocks, replenishment batch sizes, and path-level freight-fleet capacity when demand is unknown at the time of commitment and corridor electrification competes economically with diesel operation, and evaluating the combined effects of those decisions on long-haul freight infrastructure.
On the methodological side, square-root safety-stock pooling is a classical submodular structure, with extended-polymatroid descriptions of the associated binary epigraphs
(
Atamtürk and Narayanan, 2008
;
Atamtürk and Gómez, 2020
)
. More directly,
Qi et al. (2023)
already establish submodularity with optimized replenishment quantities (Section
2.3
); our approach builds on this foundation. Section
5.1
provides a scalar partial-minimization proof for the battery value function, while the computational development combines this structure with the present freight, fleet and grid-capacity model.
1.3
Contributions
This paper makes three main contributions:
•
We introduce an integrated planning problem for long-haul freight electrification under uncertainty, which coordinates BSS and CCS deployment, BSS-to-CCS assignments, and grid-bounded charging capacity with scenario-dependent battery inventory, replenishment batches, and freight-fleet capacity. We formulate it as a multi-period, two-stage stochastic program in which assignments determine battery turnaround through a repairable-inventory model, the swap-or-charge choice determines incremental fleet requirements through dwell time and Little’s law, and corridor electrification is phased against diesel substitution under node-level grid limits.
•
We develop a Lovász-cut Benders decomposition for the joint energy-infrastructure, battery-inventory and fleet planning problem on a given relay freight network, building on the submodularity-based location–inventory framework of
Qi et al. (2023)
. A scalar partial-minimization argument provides an alternative derivation of the submodularity of the battery value function and supports hub–period block cuts that dominate cuts aggregated over hubs and periods. The fleet recourse has an exact affine representation. A convex battery oracle with certified error bounds and memoization accelerates recourse evaluation. On five nested instances drawn from the seven-state network, the method reaches the
0.01
%
0.01\%
target gap on all of them, whereas a strengthened extensive formulation reaches it only on the two smallest within the three-hour limit.
•
We provide computational evidence and four planning insights. First, mean-value planning provides a useful siting baseline but underestimates capacity needs and misses assignments required at grid-constrained hubs. Second, sequential siting reduces infrastructure costs at the expense of higher battery and fleet costs. Third, SLCC decouples rapid energy service from grid access, keeping swapping close to freight demand while shifting charging selectively to grid-suitable hubs. Requiring every swapping hub to charge locally increases total cost by 14.5% in the case study. Fourth, the cost-minimizing horizon-committed plan builds geographic coverage first and expands capacity at existing hubs as adoption matures.

Literature Review
2.1
Electric Freight Charging and Battery Swapping
Battery-electric truck adoption is constrained by limited range, long charging times, battery durability, high acquisition costs, and uncertain performance across duty cycles
(
Cheng and Lin, 2024
)
, barriers that weigh most on long-haul operations, where charging dwell reduces vehicle utilization. Because operators may invest in private infrastructure when public charging is insufficient
(
Alp et al., 2022
)
, infrastructure development and adoption must be coordinated over time; on a southeastern U.S. network derived from the Freight Analysis Framework,
Kim et al. (2026)
couple Bass diffusion with capital-constrained sequencing of hydrogen refueling stations and show that deployment pace and network position jointly shape adoption. Misaligned facilities and delayed corridor-wide deployment restrict the operability of electric freight networks
(
Otero-Palencia et al., 2026
)
, and large-scale electrification may require substantial power-system upgrades at high-demand freight locations
(
Shoman et al., 2023
)
. Optimization studies coordinate electric-truck investment and charging deployment with power-system capacity
(
Ye et al., 2024
)
and design capacity-constrained public charging networks for long-distance truck traffic
(
Speth et al., 2025
)
, but remain centered on plug-in charging, in which batteries stay attached to trucks.
Battery-swapping research optimizes charging and swapping schedules
(
Sarker et al., 2014
)
, coordinates battery inventory with vehicle routing and real-time operations
(
Ni et al., 2020
)
, and jointly determines battery procurement and charging
(
Schneider et al., 2018
)
; for heavy-duty trucks, studies address degradation-aware battery management and station configuration
(
Deng et al., 2023
)
and state-of-charge-dependent charging
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
, and show that viability depends strongly on station utilization
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
. At the network level, electric commercial-vehicle studies integrate siting with routing, charging, inventory, and capacity decisions
(
Sun et al., 2019
;
Schiffer et al., 2021
;
Bragin et al., 2024
)
, and swapping-specific studies jointly locate swapping and fast-charging facilities
(
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. These studies plan against a demand trajectory taken as known, and none treats modular batteries as circulating assets whose assignment, inventory, and replenishment arise when swapping is separated from charging; the closest antecedents,
Qi et al. (2023)
and
Xu et al. (2024)
, are discussed in Section
1.2
.
A separate body of work designs the hub networks in which such services would be embedded. Planning models for hyperconnected logistics networks
(
Montreuil, 2011
;
Crainic et al., 2023
)
size hub capacity in relay transportation
(
Liu et al., 2025a
)
, allocate containerized modular capacity across supply chain ecosystems
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
, and deploy pooled operating resources
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
. These models dimension hubs for handling and storage and treat the energy that moves freight as exogenous, whereas energy-facility models locate stations without representing the freight-transfer function that brings trucks to them. The problem studied here sits at their junction: it adds energy replenishment to hubs whose freight role, and the truck stops it already induces, are given.
2.2
Stochastic Location–Inventory Planning and Fleet Sizing
Stochastic programming distinguishes here-and-now decisions from recourse actions taken after uncertainty is revealed
(
Birge and Louveaux, 1997
;
Shapiro et al., 2021
)
and has been widely used to coordinate facility location and inventory.
Atamtürk et al. (2012)
show that joint location–inventory optimization with stochastic demand and service-level constraints, solved by conic integer programming, improves on sequential planning, and
Farahani et al. (2015)
review how uncertainty in demand, lead times, and disruptions couples facility configuration, inventory, and service. Applications include closed-loop systems with uncertain forward and return flows
(
Hu et al., 2026
)
. Relative to this literature, our model links infrastructure and assignment decisions with scenario-dependent battery-inventory and replenishment recourse driven by long-haul freight demand. Fleet sizing adds a service-time dimension: Little’s law relates the average number of entities in a stable system to their arrival rate and time in system
(
Little, 1961
)
,
Mahavir Varma et al. (2026)
jointly determine the minimum fleet size and charging capacity of electric fleets for a target service level, and
Zackrisson et al. (2026)
show that intrashift charging and joint reoptimization of fleet, routing, and charging can serve a given freight workload with fewer trucks. Charging time thus determines fleet capacity, and we use Little’s law to link the swap-or-charge choice at each hub to the dwell time and fleet requirement of each path.
2.3
Submodular Structure and Decomposition in Discrete Design Problems
A concave nondecreasing function of a nonnegative modular sum is submodular, and square-root mean-risk terms of this kind are well understood in discrete optimization: the convex hull of their binary epigraphs is described by extended-polymatroid inequalities, a consequence of
Lovász (1983)
, with conic quadratic extensions to mixed 0–1 sets
(
Atamtürk and Narayanan, 2008
;
Atamtürk and Gómez, 2020
)
, and related formulations support the maximization of concave utility functions of modular sums
(
Ahmed and Atamtürk, 2011
)
. Such inequalities also serve as cuts for submodular value functions within decomposition methods. In choice-based facility location,
Ljubić and Moreno (2018)
combine outer-approximation and submodular cuts, and
Méndez-Vogel et al. (2023)
develop cut-generation methods for nested-logit choice;
Rahmaniani et al. (2017)
survey the broader Benders literature. The closest work is
Qi et al. (2023, Section 5.2)
, whose evaluation function already optimizes batch quantities inside a pooled square-root safety stock: their Lemma 8 establishes submodularity through a feasible-solution exchange, Lemma 9 gives the Lovász representation used in a constraint-generation algorithm, and a parametric search over a scalar multiplier evaluates the subproblems. We use the same representation. Our setting differs in three respects: the value functions are indexed by scenario and period inside a two-stage stochastic program with grid-bounded charging capacity and phased electrification, and their per-charging-station constraint family becomes one family per scenario, period and charging hub, which dominates cuts aggregated over hubs and periods (Proposition
4
); the battery recourse is combined with a fleet recourse that does not depend on the assignments and is represented exactly by flip-difference cuts; and the subproblems are evaluated as convex programs under a stated variance condition. Our scalar partial-minimization argument offers an alternative derivation of submodularity that connects it to minimization over a lattice
(
Topkis, 1978
)
.

3.1
Network, Demand, and Corridor Activation
We consider an open-access long-haul freight network operated by the EaaS operator of Section
1.2
. Candidate logistics hubs are connected by predetermined freight paths; trucks operate in relay and stop at intermediate hubs for freight transfer, and routing is not optimized. Each hub may host a BSS, a CCS, or both. At a hub with an active BSS, trucks exchange depleted packs for charged ones; otherwise they recharge on board at the hub’s CCS, which takes longer and therefore raises dwell times and fleet requirements. Each active BSS is assigned to one active CCS, which recharges its depleted packs and returns them as charged inventory; co-located pairs exchange packs by intra-facility handling at no shipping cost, while remote pairs use batched shuttle transport. Replenishment is modeled through lead times, batch sizes, and transportation costs, separately from path-level freight operations, and both swap replenishment and on-site truck charging draw on the hub’s grid-limited charging capacity.

The SE-5 solution reveals how freight demand, charging access, and grid availability jointly shape the infrastructure network. We describe four features of the 50-scenario solution and then their robustness.

Robustness.
Online Supplement S4 re-solves SE-5 under heavier energy use, alternative shuttle costs and diesel premiums, spatially heterogeneous adoption, a tighter service level and irreversible investment; the deployment sequence and the predominance of local replenishment persist in every case. Heavier energy use, 1.2 and 1.5 kWh per mile, raises total cost by 49% and 93% and shifts the mix toward co-location, and the swap load at the hub of the longest assignment then outgrows every grid connection within reach, so the reach of centralized charging is bounded by the largest single connection within the assignment radius. 7
Conclusion
This paper studies an SLCC network for long-haul truck electrification, with a multi-period, two-stage stochastic model that coordinates station deployment, BSS-to-CCS assignments, and charging capacity with battery inventory, replenishment, and incremental fleet requirements under uncertain demand.
Building on the submodularity-based framework of
Qi et al. (2023)
, we give a scalar proof that the battery value function is submodular and embed the resulting hub–period block Lovász cuts, together with exact fleet cuts, in a Benders decomposition accelerated by a convex oracle with certified error bounds and memoization. The method reaches gaps below
0.01
%
0.01\%
on all five instances and solves the largest in about four minutes. The numerical study shows that mean-value planning provides a useful siting baseline but underestimates capacity needs and misses the assignments required at grid-constrained hubs, that sequential siting reduces infrastructure costs at the expense of higher battery and fleet costs, that the optimized network keeps swapping near freight demand while using remote charging selectively at grid-suitable hubs, and that its committed deployment establishes geographic coverage before expanding charging capacity.
The findings have several limits. Infrastructure is committed over the horizon rather than adapted as adoption becomes observable, and the capacity protection is specific to the training scenario generator. The fixed-demand sizing convention has a negligible cost effect in the reported plans, but an endogenous served-flow formulation would require renewed value-function and cut analysis. The pooled stock meets the service target under day-to-day rate variability except at thin remote hubs, but not at remote hubs if variability also accrues within the day.
Further extensions include multistage investment, joint sizing of the two buffers of remote links, integer batch quantities, corridor-level adoption heterogeneity, endogenous route choice, multiple charging suppliers per swapping hub, and shortfall-recourse or chance-constrained capacity models; extensions that change served battery demand or charging durations require the validity of the present cuts to be re-established.
