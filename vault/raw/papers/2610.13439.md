Abstract
Collective Autonomous Vehicle (AV) route choices in real-world traffic networks can create externalities for Human-Driven Vehicles (HDVs), as routes that benefit AVs may increase travel times for other road users.
To address this, we introduce a social component, the marginal cost, to the selfish travel time AV reward. Marginal cost captures an AV’s impact on other road users by comparing their travel times with those in a counterfactual simulation in which the AV is removed.
We study AV route choice in mixed-autonomy traffic settings using Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), where AVs learn routing policies through repeated interactions with the traffic environment.
Experiments on the real-world networks of Ingolstadt and Provins show that
incorporating marginal cost into the AV reward not only limits the increase in HDV travel times and shortens AV travel times relative to selfish AV routing, but also improves overall system performance
.
In Ingolstadt, selfish routing of 40% AVs increases the travel times of other vehicles by 1.7%, and this drops to 1.1% when the social reward is included.
Interestingly, not only does overall system performance improve, but the AVs are also better off when acting socially.
This effect is consistent across tested cities, penetration rates, and replications, yet when the weight of the social component in the reward increases, the system fails to reach the desired performance.
For a more scalable approach to social AV routing in mixed-autonomy traffic, we compute the marginal cost only for the AVs whose route choices most affect the traffic system. This reduces the number of counterfactual simulations and achieves similar performance at a significantly lower computational cost.
Our results suggest that AV routing should go beyond selfish rewards and, for the benefit of all traffic participants, include a social component in its objective.
Keywords:
Autonomous vehicles, urban route choice, multi-agent reinforcement learning, social awareness, reward shaping.

1  Introduction
Every day, human drivers commute through urban traffic networks, choosing among several possible routes the ones with the minimal expected perceived travel time
[
38
,
4
]
. The growing presence of Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) in cities may introduce new dynamics, as AVs may deviate from the shortest routes provided by real-time navigation systems and can use a variety of algorithms to learn routing strategies, potentially achieving shorter travel times than Human-Driven Vehicles (HDVs) or disadvantaging them by consistently increasing their travel times
[
16
,
28
]
.
We consider a city that initially consists entirely of HDVs who learn routing strategies by updating their utility through repeated days of commuting
[
8
]
. We assume this repeated learning process brings the traffic system close to an equilibrium state
[
40
]
. Then, a subset of HDVs switches to traveling with AVs, which use Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) to learn routing policies. As learning progresses, AVs reduce their travel times and eventually achieve shorter average travel times than HDVs. However, we find that when AVs select their routes solely to minimize their individual travel times (selfish behavior), their routing policies can increase the travel times of HDVs relative to those experienced before AVs were introduced in the system (see Figure
1
A).
We therefore ask:
How can AV routing MARL algorithms be designed to reduce AV travel times while limiting the additional costs on other road users, given that urban road networks are shared public infrastructure?
To solve this problem, we augment the selfish travel-time AV reward with a component that accounts for each AV’s impact on the system, the marginal cost, following the formulation introduced in
[
29
]
. We show that incorporating marginal cost into AV rewards can reduce the increase in HDV travel times and allow AVs to achieve shorter travel times than under selfish AV routing (see Figure
1
B). We further find that these travel-time improvements depend on the relative weighting of the two reward components: assigning a greater weight to marginal cost can further improve HDV travel times but may increase AV and overall system travel times.
Figure 1:
We study AV route choice in traffic environments shared with human drivers. (A) When AVs selfishly minimize their own travel times, their routing decisions can increase HDV travel times. (B) Incorporating our marginal cost into the AV reward allows AVs to account for the impact of their route choices on other road users, mitigating the increase in HDV travel times observed under selfish AV routing while also improving AV travel times on average. However, computing marginal cost requires additional counterfactual simulations for each AV, adding computational cost. (C) To reduce this burden, we introduce selective marginal cost reward shaping, which computes marginal cost rewards only for a subset of the most impactful AVs. This preserves some of the benefits for AVs and HDVs’ travel times while reducing the number of counterfactual simulations.
We further investigate how to make marginal cost computation scalable, since it requires an additional counterfactual simulation per AV each day of commute. In each counterfactual, we remove an AV from the traffic system and compare the remaining vehicles’ travel times with those observed when the AV is present. For example, with 400 AVs and 10,000 training iterations (equivalent to commute days), computing marginal costs for all AVs would require four million additional simulation runs. We therefore investigate whether marginal cost rewards can be computed and applied only to a fraction of the most impactful AVs on the traffic system (selective marginal cost reward shaping), rather than to all AVs (marginal cost reward shaping). We then evaluate whether selective marginal cost reward shaping can achieve system, AV, and HDV travel-time improvements comparable to those obtained with marginal cost reward shaping, relative to selfish AV routing (Figure
1
C).
The contributions of this work include:
•
We show that AV routing policies trained with MARL to minimize individual AV travel times can increase HDV travel times relative to those experienced before the introduction of AVs in the system, revealing a negative externality of selfish AV routing (see Figure
3
A, D).
•
We demonstrate that incorporating a social component, the marginal cost, into the AV reward can mitigate the increase in HDV travel times observed after AV introduction while also improving AV travel times compared to those achieved when AVs minimize their individual travel times. We further show that AV and HDV travel times depend on how much weight is assigned to the marginal cost. Higher weights can further improve HDV travel times but worsen AV and overall system performance (see Figures
3
,
4
).
•
We show that computing marginal costs only for a fraction of the most impactful AVs,
selective marginal cost reward shaping
, is sufficient to retain HDV, AV, and system travel times comparable to those achieved when all AVs receive the marginal cost reward. This approach also reduces the number of counterfactual simulations (see Table

). We identify impactful AVs from sampled joint actions that capture how total system travel time varies across alternative AV route choices.
1.1  Literature review
Route choice can be formulated as a congestion game in which travelers repeatedly select among alternative routes in a traffic network while sharing limited road capacity
[
15
]
. Assuming homogeneous, fully rational users, this game converges to the Wardrop or User Equilibrium (UE), the Nash equilibrium in the route choice context, where no traveler can improve their travel time by unilaterally switching routes
[
39
,
6
]
. The System Optimum (SO) is the traffic allocation that minimizes total system travel time across the network
[
24
]
, but achieving it may require some travelers to take routes that are less favorable for them to improve overall system performance.
Human route choice is commonly modeled using the utility-maximization decision rule
[
12
,
16
,
20
]
. The main component of the utility is the expected travel time for each route
[
7
]
, which is updated after each trip. In random utility theory, utility additionally includes a random stochastic variable that captures heterogeneity in travelers’ preferences, imperfect perception of travel time and congestion, and fluctuations in driving conditions
[
8
,
40
]
. This stochastic route choice behavior is assumed to converge to the Stochastic User Equilibrium (SUE), in which no driver
believes
they can improve their travel times by switching routes.
In a mixed-traffic environment where HDVs and AVs coexist, AV route assignment can be solved in several ways. MARL is a promising method
[
3
,
2
,
37
,
19
,
36
]
, where AVs can learn routing policies through repeated interactions with the traffic environment. These policies can be optimized using different objectives, such as minimizing AVs’ individual travel time, the system’s travel time, or even maximizing HDV travel times
[
3
,
16
,
2
]
. Among these objectives, if AVs optimize only their individual travel times, their routing choices may increase other road users’ travel times because of limited road capacity. This motivates the need to search for AV routing objectives that improve AV travel times while limiting the additional costs imposed on other road users.
In economics, marginal cost denotes the change in total cost resulting from an increase in the quantity produced
[
23
]
. In transportation research, marginal cost quantifies a vehicle’s impact on the system by measuring the additional travel time it imposes on other road users through its presence in the traffic system
[
25
,
32
]
. This idea forms the basis of marginal cost pricing, in which travelers are charged according to the increase in latency their action or presence causes to the system
[
10
,
26
]
. Marginal cost pricing has also been incorporated into reinforcement learning approaches for route choice. Ramos et al.
[
30
]
, for example, incorporated marginal cost tolls into the reward signal for commuters using reinforcement learning for route choice, aiming to align individual routing decisions with system-level efficiency and minimize congestion. Inspired by the concept of marginal cost and following
[
29
]
, we define the marginal cost of an AV as the difference in travel time for all vehicles in a system when the AV is present versus a counterfactual simulation in which that AV is removed.
Marginal cost relates to difference rewards, introduced in cooperative multi-agent settings to address credit assignment when agents optimize a shared global objective
[
37
,
11
,
27
]
. Difference rewards compare the global reward with a counterfactual in which an agent’s contribution is removed or replaced by a default action, providing a reward that reflects the agent’s individual contribution to the system. In traffic routing, Grunitzki et al.
[
13
]
studied route choice with reinforcement learning and compared agents trained with selfish travel time rewards and difference rewards, showing that the latter can achieve lower average system travel times.
Prior work introduced the marginal cost reward to help MARL routing AVs reach SO solutions faster than selfish routing AVs in a toy network where the UE and SO solutions coincided
[
29
]
. In this work, we examine whether marginal cost reward shaping can prevent selfish AV routing from reducing AV travel times at the expense of HDVs in real-world traffic networks. We additionally propose a method to decrease the counterfactual simulations needed to compute the marginal cost reward by rewarding a fraction of the most impactful AVs in the system.
In the literature, researchers have studied the identification of impactful agents to explain agents’ importance in multi-agent systems. For example, Heuillet et al.,
[
14
]
used Shapley values to quantify each agent’s contribution to the global reward. Chen et al.
[
9
]
stated that an agent is impactful if the system total reward is largely changing when its action is randomized. Following a similar intuition, we consider an AV to be impactful when alternative route choices lead to substantial differences in total system travel time.
Research gap:
To the best of our knowledge, little attention has been given to designing AV routing objectives that minimize AV travel times while mitigating travel time increases for other road users in mixed-autonomy traffic. Prior work has explored different AV routing objectives but has not explicitly investigated how to mitigate the travel time externalities these objectives may impose on HDVs or the system
[
3
,
16
,
2
]
. Similarly, existing work on difference rewards has primarily focused on cooperative settings, whereas we use the counterfactual term to account for the externalities that one agent population can impose on another while coexisting in the same environment
[
37
,
11
,
27
,
13
]
.
We address this gap by incorporating marginal cost into the AV routing reward and examining how the trade-off between individual AV travel times and their system impact during MARL training affects AV, HDV, and system travel times after training. Since AV routing objectives can shape how congestion costs are distributed among agents participating in this complex system. We further investigate selective marginal cost reward shaping, in which we apply marginal cost only to a fraction of the most impactful AVs. We examine whether we can achieve similar AV, HDV, and system travel times with fewer counterfactual simulations, providing a more scalable approach to social AV routing.
2  Preliminaries
2.1  Urban routing problem
We study the route choice problem in which, each day, a number of vehicles make one trip from an origin to a destination point in an urban traffic network at a specific departure time by selecting a route from a set of discrete alternatives. To simulate this problem, we use the
RouteRL
[
1
]
framework integrated with
SUMO
[
22
]
, a continuous microscopic traffic simulator that enables modeling complex urban traffic networks and agent interactions.
Our urban environment initially consists only of HDVs, which choose routes using random utility theory
[
8
]
. After consecutive days of commuting, HDV route choices stabilize. Then, a number of HDVs switch to using AVs, which can use various MARL algorithms to learn routing strategies. AVs keep the departure time, origin, destination points, and action space (set of routes they can travel on) of their human predecessors. Human learning is thereafter disabled, and human route choices are sampled from the fixed learned distribution. This provides behaviorally plausible human route choices that AV routing policies can be trained on before deployment. AV observations include an estimate of each route’s travel time based on travel times of earlier-departing vehicles with the same origin and destination, together with the AV’s origin, destination, and departure time. The set of routes does not change during the simulation or from one day to the next, and both departure times and demand remain constant across simulation days.

5  Conclusion
AVs will increasingly operate in complex, capacitated urban traffic networks, where road users compete for limited road capacity, and each user’s route choices can affect others’ travel times. AVs may use a range of algorithms, with MARL being a promising one, to learn routing strategies that may differ from, and be more efficient than, those typically made by HDVs, potentially resulting in shorter AV travel times. Understanding how MARL AV routing can influence traffic performance is crucial to understanding how this emerging technology, AVs, may shape the performance of future urban transportation systems.
In this work, we study social AV route choice in mixed-autonomy traffic environments shared with HDVs, where AVs use MARL to learn routing policies.
To reduce the travel time costs AV routing imposes on other road users, we incorporate marginal cost into the AV reward.
Marginal cost is a counterfactual defined as the difference in travel times for all vehicles in the system when a given AV is present versus absent from the simulation. This formulation lets us include in MARL training the trade-off between minimizing an AV’s own travel time and reducing its impact on the system.
Our results show that incorporating marginal cost into the AV reward can mitigate increases in HDV travel times while also reducing AV travel times relative to selfish AV routing, indicating that
AVs that account for their impact on the system can improve travel times for both HDVs, AVs, and the system
. However, placing greater weight on marginal cost can further improve HDV travel times at the expense of AVs and worsen total system performance. For example, at 40% AV penetration in Ingolstadt, increasing
β
\beta
from 0 to 0.1 improves HDV, AV, and system travel time relative to the travel times before AVs were introduced by 0.6, 0.1, and 0.4 percentage points, respectively. Increasing
β
\beta
to 0.2 yields an additional 0.1 improvement in travel time change for HDVs but reduces the relative travel time change for AVs and the system by 1.3 and 0.5 percentage points, respectively, relative to
β
=
0.1
\beta=0.1
.
Since computing the marginal cost requires an additional counterfactual simulation for each AV, we propose selective marginal cost reward shaping to reduce this computational burden. We find that assigning the marginal cost reward to a fraction of the most impactful AVs yields improvement in relative travel-time change for HDVs, AVs, and the system that are close to those obtained when all AVs receive the marginal cost reward. In the Ingolstadt traffic network, rewarding the 40% most impactful AVs reduces the number of additional simulations by around 40%, while the HDV relative travel time change is around 0.2 percentage points lower than when all AVs receive the marginal cost reward.
Overall, our findings show that AV routing algorithms can influence not only AV travel times but also how travel time costs are distributed across road users. AV routing policies that optimize only individual AV travel times may systematically disadvantage human drivers. By showing that marginal cost reward shaping can reduce these effects, our results highlight the importance of designing AV routing algorithms that account for AVs’ impact on shared transportation systems.
