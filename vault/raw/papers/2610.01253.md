Abstract
Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL) integrates reinforcement learning with parameterized quantum circuits and is a promising approach to combinatorial optimization. On Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices, however, decoherence, gate imperfections, and measurement errors reduce policy quality and make learning less reliable. Existing error mitigation techniques are generally applied as fixed corrections that do not adapt to changing noise conditions or to the evolving state of training. This work presents Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM) as a context-aware orchestration layer of the hybrid quantum-classical training loop that dynamically selects the most suitable mitigation strategy during QRL training. APGEM evaluates Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE), Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC), Clifford Data Regression (CDR), and Readout Error Mitigation (REM) using policy-level indicators, including quantum-state fidelity, policy entropy, cumulative reward, and approximation ratio, and integrates the selected strategy directly into the reinforcement learning loop. The framework is evaluated on the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP), a representative NP-hard problem in urban logistics, under a range of NISQ noise models and noise levels. APGEM consistently outperforms conventional static mitigation methods, reaches approximately 94% of the utility of an oracle strategy, maintains higher quantum-state fidelity as noise increases, and produces more stable learning behaviour throughout training. Ablation studies show that the framework learns context-aware mitigation policies that adapt to different noise environments and circuit execution conditions. These findings demonstrate that integrating adaptive error mitigation into the learning process substantially improves the robustness and reliability of QRL on NISQ hardware.

Introduction
Combinatorial optimization problems such as the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) represent some of the most challenging and practically relevant tasks in operations research and logistics
[
1
,
2
]
. The VRP, in its many variants, requires the design of cost-efficient routes for a fleet of vehicles to serve a set of customers under resource and operational constraints such as vehicle capacity, service time, and time windows
[
3
]
. Efficient solutions to VRPs are critical for applications in transportation networks, supply chain management, postal and parcel delivery, and distribution systems, where even marginal reductions in routing cost can yield significant economic savings, reduced fuel consumption, and lower environmental impact
[
4
,
5
]
. Given the scale and complexity of modern logistics systems, VRPs are not only of theoretical importance but also a key driver of efficiency in industry and sustainability initiatives
[
6
]
.
Classical approaches to VRPs include exact solvers based on mixed-integer programming and a wide range of heuristics and metaheuristics, such as nearest-neighbor, tabu search, simulated annealing, ant colony optimization, and genetic algorithms
[
7
]
. While these methods remain powerful and are widely applied in practice, they often struggle to scale effectively with problem size. The VRP is NP-hard, and as the number of customers, vehicles, and constraints increases, the computational burden grows exponentially
[
8
]
. Heuristic methods, though effective for moderate scales, tend to degrade in performance for large-scale, high-dimensional instances with complex temporal and capacity constraints
[
9
]
. This motivates the search for novel computational paradigms that can complement classical methods in tackling large-scale optimization.
With the emergence of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, quantum computing has been identified as a promising candidate for advancing combinatorial optimization
[
10
,
11
]
. Quantum algorithms, by leveraging superposition and entanglement, have the potential to explore vast solution spaces in ways that classical methods cannot. While large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computers are still beyond reach, hybrid quantum-classical algorithms designed for NISQ-era devices have become central to ongoing research
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Among these, Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) are especially promising due to their compatibility with near-term devices, reliance on shallow circuits, and adaptability through classical feedback loops
[
15
]
.
Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL) extends this paradigm by embedding parameterized quantum circuits within reinforcement learning agents, enabling policies to be represented as variational quantum circuits (VQCs)
[
16
]
. In this setting, the quantum policy maps environment states to action probabilities, while classical optimization updates the parameters. This hybrid structure makes QRL particularly attractive for dynamic decision-making problems such as VRP, where sequential adaptability and exploration of combinatorial action spaces are essential. However, QRL faces two central challenges in practice. First, quantum circuits executed on NISQ devices are highly susceptible to noise, which degrades fidelity, increases state entropy, and ultimately destabilizes the learning process
[
17
]
. Second, while error mitigation strategies have been developed to counteract noise, they are typically applied in a static manner. Conventional mitigation methods such as Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE), Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC), Clifford Data Regression (CDR), and Readout Error Mitigation (REM) are generally selected once and applied uniformly, without adapting to the varying characteristics of circuits or noise across different executions
[
18
]
. This static application often results in suboptimal improvements and fails to fully exploit the potential of mitigation in a reinforcement learning context.
Recent progress in error mitigation has shown that techniques such as Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE), Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC), Clifford Data Regression (CDR), and Readout Error Mitigation (REM) can substantially improve quantum execution quality when carefully applied
[
19
,
20
]
. However, existing studies generally evaluate these techniques in isolation and apply them as fixed mitigation strategies throughout execution. Such static approaches fail to account for the dynamic nature of NISQ noise, where the effectiveness of a mitigation technique may vary with circuit characteristics, noise intensity, and the current stage of learning. Consequently, there remains an open challenge of designing adaptive mitigation frameworks that can continuously identify and apply the most suitable mitigation strategy based on real-time performance signals
[
14
]
. Addressing this challenge is particularly important in Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL), where noise directly affects policy optimization and learning stability
[
21
]
.
In this work, we extend the
Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM)
framework, first proposed for the Traveling Salesman Problem by Majid et al.
[
22
]
, to Quantum Reinforcement Learning applied to capacitated VRP instances. The framework combines reinforcement learning, variational quantum circuits, and adaptive error mitigation in a closed feedback loop. Our key contributions are fourfold:
1.
Hybrid QRL Environment for VRP:
We design a reinforcement learning environment tailored to VRP, where the policy is represented by a variational quantum circuit trained under noisy simulations that mimic realistic NISQ hardware conditions. This provides a practical benchmark for evaluating quantum-enhanced learning.
2.
APGEM Controller:
We introduce an adaptive error mitigation controller that dynamically selects among multiple mitigation techniques (ZNE, PEC, CDR, REM). The controller employs a LinUCB contextual-bandit selection strategy over a context vector (depolarizing noise level, readout rate, fidelity and its degradation, entropy, normalized circuit depth, remaining shot budget, approximation ratio, and recent reward trend), scored by a cost-aware utility that balances mitigated fidelity against sampling cost and estimator variance. This transforms error mitigation from a static pre-processing step into a dynamic, shot-budget-aware runtime component of the hybrid quantum-classical loop, integrated with reinforcement feedback.
3.
Feasibility-Guaranteed Policy-Gradient Training:
We train the quantum policy with REINFORCE and exact parameter-shift gradients over a coverage-preserving decoder with cheapest-insertion repair, which guarantees complete feasible routes on every instance and provides a directed learning signal for the routing policy.
4.
Comprehensive Adaptive Mitigation Analysis:
We conduct extensive ablation and robustness studies to analyze mitigation-selection behavior across varying noise regimes, circuit characteristics, and training horizons. The results reveal that APGEM learns interpretable and context-aware mitigation policies that closely approach oracle-level mitigation performance while consistently outperforming fixed mitigation strategies.
Through extensive experiments across different noise families (depolarizing, amplitude damping, dephasing, and readout) and multiple noise levels, we demonstrate that APGEM consistently outperforms static mitigation strategies and approaches oracle-level mitigation performance. The framework learns context-dependent mitigation policies that adapt to changing operating regimes, preserves higher quantum-state fidelity under increasing noise, reduces performance degradation, and improves learning stability throughout training. These findings highlight the importance of adaptive error mitigation as a key enabling technology for reliable Quantum Reinforcement Learning on NISQ devices and demonstrate its potential for real-world combinatorial optimization applications.

Related Work
Quantum computing in logistics and supply chain management has been widely discussed in recent overviews such as
[

and

These works highlight the promise of quantum algorithms, particularly QAOA and annealing, but also note the challenges posed by NISQ-era devices, including qubit limitations, noise, and shallow depth constraints. The research gap here lies in the lack of strategies that directly address the noise-resilience of logistics-focused quantum algorithms. Our work differs by not only applying quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) to CVRP but also embedding an adaptive error mitigation layer that explicitly counters NISQ noise during learning.
In parallel, studies on quantum-inspired computing such as
[

and
[
26

demonstrate that classical algorithms inspired by quantum principles can yield scalable heuristics and practical benefits for logistics planning. While these approaches serve as useful baselines, they cannot capture the full advantages of true quantum superposition and interference. Moreover, they lack mechanisms to adapt error management dynamically, since they are executed on classical systems. In contrast, our APGEM-QRL framework leverages actual quantum circuits simulated under noise and adapts mitigation strength in real time, going beyond static quantum-inspired methods.
Research focusing on direct quantum algorithms for logistics optimization (for example,
[
27
]
) shows how logistics problems can be mapped into Ising or QUBO formulations. These studies explore penalty-based encodings, circuit depth trade-offs, and hybrid decomposition strategies, but they generally assume fixed noise levels or apply static mitigation techniques. The gap here is the absence of noise-aware learning that evolves with circuit complexity and training phases. Our approach addresses this by coupling reinforcement learning with Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM), allowing the quantum solver to adaptively regulate noise handling based on policy progress and circuit depth.
Cognitive and learning-oriented perspectives such as
[
28
]
introduce the notion of quantum-enhanced cognitive modeling for logistics, suggesting that quantum interference can improve policy exploration. While these studies provide a conceptual foundation for quantum reinforcement learning, they remain largely at the modeling stage and do not address the critical challenge of noise robustness. Our work extends this idea by not only using QRL for policy learning but also embedding mitigation strategies that respond to real-time feedback signals, thereby improving convergence reliability in noisy environments.
At the systems level,
[
29
]
and
[
30

argue for the integration of quantum methods into end-to-end logistics workflows, highlighting real-world requirements such as data acquisition, hybrid orchestration, and translation of algorithmic outcomes into operational KPIs like cost, energy efficiency, and on-time delivery. These contributions provide a systems perspective but stop short of demonstrating noise-resilient learning with statistical validation. Our work addresses this by evaluating on standard CVRPLIB benchmark instances under multiple noise models, and reporting not just raw optimization cost but also approximation ratio to best-known solutions, reward convergence, distribution fidelity, and entropy, together with formal statistical testing across instances.
Finally,
[
26
]
emphasizes the importance of data-driven and adaptive decision-making in logistics optimization. While this line of work showcases the benefits of feedback mechanisms in classical settings, it does not extend adaptivity to quantum error mitigation. Here lies another gap: existing approaches do not treat error mitigation as a controllable, feedback-driven process. APGEM fills this gap by making mitigation strength a dynamic variable guided by policy performance, noise conditions, and learning stage, transforming mitigation from a static parameter into an adaptive component of the training loop.
APGEM was first proposed for QRL on the Traveling Salesman Problem by Majid et al.

Taken together, prior research establishes the foundations for applying quantum and quantum-inspired methods to logistics but leaves several gaps unaddressed: static treatment of noise, limited focus on real-world urban networks, lack of integration between learning dynamics and error mitigation, and reliance on narrow performance metrics. This paper addresses these shortcomings by proposing APGEM-QRL, a hybrid framework that adaptively mitigates noise during QRL training on standard CVRPLIB benchmark instances, achieving improved approximation ratios, faster and more stable convergence, and enhanced robustness under varying noise conditions.

3.5
Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM)
While existing error mitigation techniques such as Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE), Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC), Clifford Data Regression (CDR), and Readout Error Mitigation (REM) can improve quantum execution quality, their effectiveness depends strongly on the underlying noise characteristics, circuit structure, and execution conditions. 6
Experimental Setup
We evaluate the proposed framework on the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) using standard benchmark instances rather than a single synthetic case. The study uses instances from the CVRPLIB library, dominated by the Augerat Set A (25 instances) together with representatives from the B, E, F, M and P families, spanning 15 to 100 customers. Each instance specifies a single depot, fixed customer demands, and a fixed vehicle capacity read directly from the instance file; inter-node distances are the Euclidean (EUC_2D) distances of the benchmark, rounded to integers as in the CVRPLIB convention. Every instance is paired with its published best-known solution (BKS), which serves as the reference for the approximation ratio. This benchmark-based setup replaces single-instance evaluation and provides the blocking factor for the statistical tests reported in Section
5
.
6.1
Classical Baselines
The implementation includes a suite of constructive and metaheuristic CVRP solvers: Nearest Neighbour, Savings (Clarke–Wright), Genetic Algorithm, Simulated Annealing, and Tabu Search, together with the Google OR-Tools routing solver as a strong reference. Each solver returns a set of vehicle routes and a total cost, and a unified wrapper runs all baselines and aggregates the results, reporting the median over random seeds for the stochastic methods. Capacity feasibility is enforced during route construction and perturbation, and the total cost is the sum of pairwise arc distances including depot returns.

6.3
Reinforcement Learning Environment
The environment encodes the CVRP as an episodic decision process. The state is a compact feature vector comprising the fraction of customers already served, the current vehicle load as a fraction of capacity, the normalized distance to the nearest unvisited customer, and the fraction of the vehicle budget used; these features are mapped to encoding angles in

The action set consists of the unvisited customers whose demand fits the residual capacity, plus a return-to-depot action; capacity feasibility is enforced as a hard constraint by an action mask. Step rewards combine the negative arc distance with a per-visit progress bonus, and a terminal penalty is applied for any unserved customers. To guarantee complete feasible solutions, the environment uses a coverage-preserving construction that opens a new route whenever no active route has capacity, followed by a cheapest-insertion repair; consequently every episode yields a solution that serves each customer exactly once within capacity. The measurement distribution of the (mitigated) circuit is aggregated over the feasible actions in a distance-ordered, contiguous mapping to form the categorical policy, and the policy parameters are trained by the REINFORCE policy-gradient estimator with exact parameter-shift gradients. During training the environment resets at the start of each episode, and per-episode metrics are recorded, including mean reward, distribution fidelity, normalized entropy, approximation ratio, total quantum route cost, and best classical route cost, with execution time logged for both the quantum policy and the classical solvers.

6.6
Evaluation Protocol
The evaluation reports: (i) total route cost; (ii) approximation ratio, computed primarily against the best-known solution and secondarily against the best classical baseline; (iii) feasibility, which is guaranteed by the coverage construction and additionally reported as a served-customer fraction; (iv) mean reward per episode; (v) distribution fidelity and normalized entropy as indicators of quantum-execution quality; and (vi) runtime for both the quantum policy and the classical solvers. Results are reported across solvers, episodes, noise conditions, and mitigation techniques, and differences are assessed with non-parametric significance tests (Friedman, Nemenyi post-hoc, and Wilcoxon signed-rank) using the instance as the blocking factor, with bootstrap confidence intervals for stability.

First, the classical baseline suite is validated on thirty CVRPLIB instances with formal significance testing, with OR-Tools and Clarke–Wright statistically strongest. 8
Conclusion
This work presented a noise-resilient Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL) framework for the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP), combining a variational quantum policy trained by REINFORCE with parameter-shift gradients, a feasibility-guaranteed decoder, explicit NISQ noise modeling, and an Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM) controller that selects error-mitigation techniques online. The framework was evaluated on a thirty-instance CVRPLIB benchmark with formal statistical testing, and every reported figure and table was produced directly by the released implementation.

Future Scope
Several directions follow naturally from these findings. The dominant limitation is the representational capacity of the compact policy circuit at larger instance sizes, which future work will address through more expressive encodings and ansätze (for example problem-informed or hardware-efficient layers with instance-dependent qubit and shot budgets), hybrid quantum-classical decoders that couple the quantum policy with a classical local-search refinement, and alternative training objectives such as actor-critic or advantage-based estimators to strengthen the learning signal beyond REINFORCE. Since all results here use simulated noise, a natural next step is to evaluate the framework under calibrated device noise models (thermal relaxation with realistic
T
1
T_{1}
and
T
2
T_{2}
, coherent and crosstalk errors, and measured readout confusion matrices) and, ultimately, on real quantum processors, to confirm that the regime-dependent mitigation behaviour observed in simulation transfers to hardware. The residual regret suggests limited headroom for the current linear contextual-bandit design, so non-linear controllers (neural contextual bandits, Thompson sampling, or deep reinforcement-learning selectors), a finer noise discretization, and a broader mitigation pool (for example symmetry verification or virtual distillation) may further narrow the gap to the oracle, particularly in the severe-noise regime where the controller currently under-reacts. The environment can also be extended beyond capacity constraints to richer routing formulations, including time windows (CVRPTW), pickup-and-delivery, and multi-depot settings, each of which requires a time-aware feasibility mechanism and appropriate benchmark instances with published references. Future evaluations will further widen the benchmark beyond the Augerat-dominated set used here to include the full B, E, F, M, P and X families and larger X-series instances, with more independent seeds per instance, to strengthen the statistical power of the comparisons and the controller ablation. Finally, the routing policy and the mitigation controller are currently trained as coupled but distinct components, and jointly co-adapting them, so that the policy learns to be robust to the mitigation it will receive, is a promising direction; on the theoretical side, deriving regret guarantees for the cost-aware contextual bandit under the proposed utility would place the empirical near-oracle behaviour on a firmer footing.
