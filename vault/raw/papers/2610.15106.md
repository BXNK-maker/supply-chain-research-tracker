Abstract
Latent recursion repeatedly processes internal
representations through the same neural network.
Recent work has shown this approach to be
effective on structured reasoning tasks such
as Sudoku and maze solving. However, whether
latent recursion can support neural routing
remains unclear.
We introduce RELAGRAM (
Re
cursive
La
tent
Gra
ph
M
odel), a novel approach that
brings latent recursion to graph combinatorial
optimization. RELAGRAM encodes a capacitated
vehicle routing problem (CVRP) instance as
edge tokens; a compact transformer recurrently
updates the latent representations of these tokens. An
output head maps the answer state to an edge
heatmap, from which an autoregressive decoder
constructs a feasible solution. On CVRP-100 instances,
the 0.53M-parameter RELAGRAM achieves gaps
to a PyVRP reference of 13.4%, 9.8%, and 8.3%
under greedy decoding, eightfold symmetry
augmentation, and 256-candidate search,
respectively. Trained only on CVRP-100, RELAGRAM
generalizes zero-shot to CVRP-500 and
CVRP-1000, achieving gaps of 15.6% and 21.7%
under 256-candidate search. Together, these
results demonstrate that latent recursion is
a promising approach to neural routing.

Introduction
Solving a combinatorial optimization problem in a single,
end-to-end pass of a neural network is inherently difficult.
A learned model must navigate a constrained and exponentially
large solution space with limited computation. Increasing
network depth provides more computation, but also raises
training and inference costs
[
1
]
.
Neural-based routing methods commonly construct routes autoregressively
or iteratively refine complete solutions
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Both approaches simplify
the task by breaking the solution process into a series of simpler
decisions. However, this simplicity comes at the cost of an
information bottleneck. If only the updated solution is carried
between iterations, information that exists only in the latent
state, such as intermediate reasoning, is lost. This creates a
form of representational ‘amnesia’ that makes it difficult to
accumulate a richer understanding of the problem.

Latent recursion avoids this bottleneck by carrying the internal state, rather
than the candidate solution, between iterations. A solution can be decoded
at any stage but is not fed back into the model: each subsequent stage
continues directly from the preceding latent state. Every depth therefore
produces both an intermediate computation and a valid solution point.
Recent latent-recursive models perform strongly on Sudoku, maze solving, and
ARC-AGI
[
5
]
.
However, whether latent recursion transfers from supervised reasoning to
reinforcement learning for graph combinatorial optimization remains unclear.
We introduce RELAGRAM, a novel application of the latent recursion idea
to graph combinatorial optimization, and demonstrate it on CVRP.
After brief training, a 0.53M model reaches a 13.4% greedy gap on
CVRP-100, reducing it to 8.3% under a
256-candidate search.
On CVRP-1000, RELAGRAM achieves gaps of 29.9% and 21.7% under greedy and
256-candidate decoding, respectively. Notably, it continues to improve
beyond its trained depth: on CVRP-1000 it achieves its best greedy gap of
28.45% at depth ten — six stages deeper than seen during training.

Background
Latent recursion.
Latent recursion applies the same network repeatedly to an internal state.
The Tiny Recursive Model (TRM) keeps two states: a solution state
y
y
and a latent
reasoning state
z
z
. One cycle refines
z
z
over repeated passes conditioned on
the input and on
y
y
, then updates
y
y
from the refined
z
z
; a supervision step
chains several such cycles, all but the last without gradients. Deep
supervision gives every step its own loss, and the states are detached
between steps, so the model learns successive corrections without
backpropagating across them. TRM achieves strong results on Sudoku,
maze solving, and ARC-AGI benchmarks
[
5
]
.
Edge heatmaps.
An edge heatmap records the model’s confidence that each transition belongs to
a high-quality solution. DIFUSCO produces such heatmaps for the travelling
salesman problem by iteratively denoising edge variables
[
6
]
.
However, DIFUSCO’s learned edge representation is
separate from the procedure that converts it into a discrete solution.
Feasible decoding.
A heatmap is not itself a route. For CVRP, each decision must account for the
visited customers and remaining vehicle capacity. The Attention Model, a
neural routing model, constructs a route autoregressively, selecting one node
at a time while masking infeasible actions
[
2
]
. DeepACO
instead learns a heuristic measure for an ant colony optimization
system: a population of simulated ants builds routes in parallel, each choosing
only feasible transitions
[
7
]
.
Learning from sampled routes.
REINFORCE allows a routing policy to learn without optimal labels: complete
routes are sampled, scored by their cost, and used to improve the
policy
[
8
,
2
]
. The signal is
sparse, however, because one route-level reward must guide every edge decision
made during construction. Self-imitation adds denser feedback: the lowest-cost
sampled route serves as a temporary teacher, and a cross-entropy loss trains
the heatmap to favour its transitions
[
9
]
.

Experimental design.
We train on CVRP-100 in the RL4CO

The model has 0.53M parameters and is trained at four depths.
Runs use a batch size of 16 with 16 sampled routes per instance, which
across the four recursion depths yields 1,024 routes per batch.
We evaluate RELAGRAM under two training budgets: 40,000 optimizer
updates with a training time of 2.98 hours to compare RELAGRAM with
DeepACO; and 160,000 optimizer updates with a training time of
11.94 hours to assess performance and its scaling under a larger
training budget.
Training uses a 10 GB A100 GPU slice.
DeepACO is chosen as our baseline because it is a competitive neural
combinatorial optimizer with a similar design: a learned edge heatmap guides
a separate solution search. DeepACO iterates through pheromone updates,
whereas RELAGRAM updates latent states.

twice as many base graphs and takes 1.87 times longer to train.

Solution quality across graph sizes.
We evaluate RELAGRAM trained for 160,000 updates on CVRP-100.
The model reaches an 8.30% gap on CVRP-100 under 256-candidate decoding
(Table

15.64% and 21.65% on CVRP-500 and CVRP-1000 under 256-candidate decoding.
On CVRP-1000,

to 21.65%. The decoder therefore offers test-time scaling that strengthens
with instance size, worth 5.10 points on CVRP-100 and 8.26 on CVRP-1000.
Comparison with DeepACO.
We compare RELAGRAM with DeepACO after training both for 40,000 optimizer
updates. DeepACO is stronger in eight of nine size–decoder regimes: it
achieves 3.94-point and 3.49-point lower gaps on CVRP-100 under
greedy and 256-candidate decoding, and a 9.33-point lower greedy gap on
CVRP-1000. RELAGRAM does not yet achieve solution
quality competitive with DeepACO. Under 256 candidates, the two converge as
instances grow: ants sample every step, so DeepACO’s candidate scaling weakens
with size while RELAGRAM’s does not.

Discussion and Conclusion
RELAGRAM shows that a small latent-recursive model successfully learns
to route. It transfers without retraining to graphs up to ten times larger
and can improve beyond its trained depth, suggesting promising inference-time
depth scaling.
The model does not reach state-of-the-art results, and the matched greedy
comparison finds no significant advantage from recursion. We therefore
see RELAGRAM as a proof of concept for applying latent recursion
to graph combinatorial optimization.
We hypothesize that an important limitation is the sparse RL signal, which
needs further engineering for latent recursion. Importantly,
y
y
and
z
z
receive only indirect RL signal, making learning harder.
Lastly, RELAGRAM’s recursive nature may be very useful for dynamic problems.
The loop already runs zero-shot past the conditions it was trained under; if it
handles an updated instance as well as extra depth, a single model with a decoder
could solve such problems end-to-end, by recurrently processing the last latent
state and the updated problem.

Appendix A
Further Model Evaluation
The main experiments compare RELAGRAM and DeepACO on solution quality. Here,
we look more closely at how the models work and provide a matched
comparison with a single-pass control.
Both models learn edge scores, but iteration happens in different places.
RELAGRAM repeatedly refines a latent state using the same network before decoding
the final heatmap. DeepACO instead uses an anisotropic GNN to produce a heuristic
for Ant Colony Optimization, where completed solutions update the pheromone state
and guide subsequent search. DeepACO performs better overall, although
RELAGRAM is competitive in two Combined256 settings: 17.11% versus 17.38% on
CVRP-500 and 24.32% versus 23.31% on CVRP-1000.
We compare RELAGRAM with a direct model that maps the
input straight to one heatmap. Both use the same 0.526M-parameter transformer
and are matched in optimizer updates, unique training instances, and sampled
routes.
