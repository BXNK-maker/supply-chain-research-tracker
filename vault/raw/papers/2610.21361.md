Abstract
Autonomous delivery in large-scale buildings requires coordinated planning across multiple spatial levels, including building, floor, and room, while ensuring scheduling efficiency. Existing methods often focus solely on algorithmic development, neglecting integration with physical robotic platforms, and rarely validate performance in real-world scenarios. Many approaches also overlook hierarchical building structures and room-level privacy constraints, limiting practical applicability. To address these challenges, this paper presents a hierarchical delivery framework based on a Building–Floor–Room abstraction, which decomposes the problem across multiple levels to significantly reduce optimization complexity. We formulate a building-aware extension of the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) that explicitly models inter-building and inter-floor connectivity, room-level access constraints, and privacy-sensitive areas, enabling globally feasible and privacy-compliant task assignment and routing. Based on this hierarchical model, the framework can evaluate delivery efficiency across different building layouts and optimize depot placement for specific configurations. At the execution level, we introduce a novel hardware design for non-interactive, privacy-preserving delivery, supported by reachability-aware distances and a closed-loop mapping, localization, and navigation system, ensuring consistency between planned routes and physical navigation. The proposed framework is validated on a real multi-sensor robotic platform, demonstrating reliable multi-floor delivery with improved path rationality and overall efficiency.

Introduction
The increasing demand for last-mile delivery in building complexes, campuses, and large residential communities necessitates mobile robots capable of executing multi-goal delivery tasks in real-world indoor environments
[
34
]
. Unlike conventional indoor navigation, which often focuses on a single floor or building, real-world facilities exhibit a
three-level hierarchical structure
—
building, floor, and room
—each posing distinct operational challenges
[
24
]
:
(1)
Building-level
, where physical separation and sparse inter-building connections make inter-building travel costly and planning-sensitive;
(2)
Floor-level
, where vertical mobility relies on elevators with vertical transfer cost and nontrivial waiting times, creating significant scheduling constraints;
(3)
Room-level
, where each floor features complex 2D topologies, branched corridors, diverse room layouts, and privacy-restricted zones, complicating local path planning and reachability.
Such hierarchical structures pose fundamental challenges to classical Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) formulations. VRP typically relies on ideal Euclidean distances and metric properties (e.g., symmetry and triangle inequality), assumptions fundamentally violated by non-Euclidean path distances, elevator access costs, and time-varying vertical transfers. Furthermore, while the base Capacitated VRP (CVRP) is already known to be NP-hard
[
1
]
, constructing a unified graph spanning all building, floor, and room levels exacerbates this complexity. This unified modeling leads to a combinatorial explosion of the state space, rendering the global hierarchical routing problem intractable for real-time operation and preventing timely responses to dynamic delivery requests
[
7
]
.
Beyond structural complexity, reliable multi-goal delivery in real-world deployments is further hindered by three key practical challenges.
(1) Lack of effective large-scale cross-building optimization models.
Most existing approaches focus on single-building or small-scale routing problems, and do not scale to delivery scenarios involving multiple buildings and heterogeneous inter-building distances. As a result, cross-building delivery decisions are often handled by ad hoc heuristics, leading to suboptimal capacity utilization and excessive depot returns.
(2) Dynamic environments and sensing limitations.
Real-world indoor environments are highly dynamic, with moving pedestrians and time-varying layouts that disrupt stable perception and localization. These factors introduce uncertainty into navigation and execution, necessitating robust odometry and sensor fusion methods to maintain reliable operation
[
40
]
.
(3) Plan–execution mismatch in decoupled systems.
Global VRP solutions are typically computed on idealized maps and assume perfect execution. In practice, however, discrepancies between planned routes and feasible robot trajectories frequently arise. Moreover, mapping, localization, scheduling, and navigation modules are often designed and optimized independently, preventing closed-loop coordination and resulting in inconsistent end-to-end performance.
Moreover, with the increasing adoption of autonomous delivery systems and the rising cost of manual labor, delivery efficiency has become an important consideration in residential building design. In many communities, building layout and depot placement directly affect the feasibility and performance of unmanned delivery. Therefore, it is also essential to use quantitative models to guide building layout design toward better support for autonomous delivery.
To address these challenges, a
hierarchical autonomous delivery system
tailored to the Building–Floor–Room structure is proposed. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
1.
A
hierarchical Building–Floor–Room model
and a
building-aware CVRP formulation
are introduced, accounting for inter-building and inter-floor connectivity, room-level access, and privacy-sensitive areas, which reduces optimization complexity while enabling globally feasible and privacy-compliant routing.
2.
A
unified mapping and autonomous navigation system
integrated with a
non-interactive, privacy-preserving robot design
is developed, ensuring consistency between hierarchical planning and real-world execution.
3.
A
layout evaluation and depot optimization method
is proposed, allowing quantitative assessment of delivery efficiency across different building configurations and optimal depot placement.
2
Related Work
Establishing reliable autonomous delivery in complex building complexes requires novel solutions across environmental modeling, operational research, and robotic perception. This section reviews the state-of-the-art in these three interconnected domains, highlighting the existing limitations that motivate our proposed hierarchical autonomous delivery system.
2.1
Hierarchical and Semantic Environmental Modeling for Navigation
Autonomous navigation fundamentally relies on accurate environment representation.
Pioneering works established metric-based grid maps and early topological graphs
[
33
]
to represent planar environments.
Modern facilities, however, demand multi-level abstractions that can explicitly capture building-scale structure and semantics. Han et al.
[
9
]
and Palacín et al.
[
20
]
model robots navigating multi-floor buildings, explicitly considering elevator and stair nodes.
To this end, hierarchical environment models and graph-based representations have been proposed to manage spatial complexity,
ranging from extended hierarchical building graphs for design and engineering
[
30
]
,
to hierarchical attributed graphs for facade and building component modeling
[
36
]
,
and indoor navigation graphs that explicitly encode multi-storey visibility and connectivity
[
44
]
.
Such hierarchical mapping structures often segment the environment into connected regions or levels
[
12
,
37
]
,
enabling scalable reasoning across floors and functional spaces.
As environment scale increases, optimizing large unified graph structures becomes computationally expensive.
Hierarchical or multi-level graph representations can significantly improve efficiency; however, designing effective hierarchical models that balance geometric fidelity, semantic richness, and computational tractability remains an open research problem.
With the prevalence of 3D sensing, research has moved towards dense 3D reconstruction using methods like TSDF
[
13
,
5
]
, and scalable volumetric maps
[
41
]
. Concurrently, semantic mapping has gained traction, enriching geometric models with object and place labels. Recent systems, including Kimera
[
23
]
and Hydra
[
11
]
, represent the environment using 3D Scene Graphs, unifying metric and semantic information. Gu et al. proposed ConceptGraphs, an open-vocabulary graph-structured representation for 3D scenes
[
8
]
. While these semantic approaches provide rich contextual representations, they mainly focus on scene understanding or geometric reconstruction. Meanwhile, with the growing emphasis on user privacy, explicitly modeling privacy-sensitive areas (e.g., restricted offices or sensitive zones) has become increasingly important. Existing privacy-preserving efforts, such as the multi-factor authentication framework proposed by Yang et al.
[
42
]
, primarily protect identities and communication security in robotic delivery systems rather than spatial semantic representations. As a result, few semantic environment models explicitly incorporate privacy constraints into their spatial abstractions. Our work addresses this gap by integrating semantic connectivity, privacy-sensitive regions, and cost modeling into a unified Building–Floor–Room hierarchical graph optimized for delivery and navigation tasks.
2.2
Vehicle Routing Problems with Indoor and Dynamic Constraints
The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) is the core of multi-goal delivery, with foundational theory well-documented in classical works
[
35
]
. Standard VRP formulations rely on metric assumptions, such as symmetric and Euclidean distances, which fail dramatically in structured indoor settings
[
29
]
.
Efforts to adapt VRP to robotic systems have focused on incorporating physical constraints. The VRP with Time Windows (VRP-TW)
[
31
]
is relevant for timed deliveries, while stochastic VRP addresses uncertainty in service times. For indoor logistics, the primary challenge is the non-Euclidean topology and the asymmetric cost induced by vertical movement. Studies have attempted to model elevator waiting times
[
21
]
and integrated multi-floor scheduling with localization
[
14
,
16
]
. Furthermore, optimizing for energy (Green VRP)
[
26
]
is crucial for battery-powered AMRs. From an algorithmic perspective, both metaheuristics like Ant Colony Optimization
[
32
]
and emerging learning-based approaches
[
2
]
have been applied to VRP variants. In addition to single-robot navigation, multiple robots must be coordinated to optimize overall delivery efficiency. Multi-trip vehicle routing problems (MTVRP) are used in
[
9
]
to schedule multiple robots across multiple floors while minimizing total travel cost. However, existing VRP applications for robotics generally operate on idealized graphs. They lack a mechanism to dynamically incorporate SLAM-derived reachability and consistency information, leading to the persistent plan–execution mismatch when transitioning between different building levels. We overcome this by formulating a hierarchical CVRP whose distance matrix is derived directly from a reachability-aware map pipeline.
2.3
LiDAR SLAM, Fusion, and Closed-Loop System Integration
Reliable autonomy hinges on robust Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM). The progression from 2D methods like Cartographer
[
10
]
to 3D LiDAR SLAM, starting with LOAM
[
43
]
, has been critical. Contemporary systems like LIO-SAM
[
27
]
, Invariant-DLIO
[
4
]
, and the highly efficient FAST-LIO2
[
39
]
use tight LiDAR-Inertial fusion to maintain accuracy over long trajectories. For additional robustness, visual-inertial odometry (VIO), pioneered by systems like VINS-Mono
[
22
]
and DSO
[
38
]
, and full Lidar-Visual-Inertial fusion (LVI-SAM
[
28
]
, R3LIVE
[
18
]
) have been developed to mitigate drift in feature-poor or geometrically degenerate environments. Graph-based SLAM systems are also critical for global consistency and loop closure
[
15
,
19
]
.
Despite the sophistication of these modules, they often operate in isolation. The widely used navigation architecture consists of global planning feeding into local planners like the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA)
[
6
]
or the Time-Elastic Band (TEB)
[
25
]
. Crucially, this setup inherently suffers from decoupling: the high-level VRP scheduler (Section 2.2) is blind to real-time SLAM consistency and execution challenges (e.g., elevator localization degradation). While theoretical work has outlined the necessity of integrated systems
[
3
,
17
]
, few deployable architectures have achieved true closed-loop integration. Our work represents a significant step by implementing a closed-loop system that leverages Fast-LIO for high-fidelity state estimation and feeds real-time spatial information directly back into the hierarchical task scheduler, thereby ensuring global-local plan consistency.

3.2
Hierarchical Two-Stage Capacitated Vehicle Routing Formulation
Leveraging the hierarchical building representation introduced previously, we formulate a
building-aware
two-stage capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) to address system-level delivery objectives. Rather than solving a single monolithic routing problem across all spatial scales, we decompose the decision-making process into two distinct stages: inter-building allocation and intra-building route optimization. This hierarchical decomposition enables a top-down assignment of delivery tasks while allowing bottom-up refinement of route costs, ensuring that high-level scheduling is consistent with detailed in-building navigation.

Building Layout Typologies, Evaluation, and Depot Optimization
Residential building layouts exhibit diverse spatial organizations that directly influence delivery connectivity and travel distance for autonomous delivery systems. In this study, four representative residential layout typologies are considered:
grid
,

,

7
, further provides illustrative examples of each typology together with depot adjustment behaviors under varying demand distributions, including depot relocation and the introduction of additional depots.
Figure 7:
Representative residential building layout typologies.
Loop layouts
adopt ring-like circulation structures with internal or peripheral paths, offering balanced redundancy and compact routing. When the depot is placed near the geometric or demand-weighted center, loop layouts consistently achieve the lowest total delivery distance, particularly under symmetric or smoothly varying demand.
Tree layouts
follow a hierarchical access structure in which local branches connect to a limited number of main corridors. The lack of alternative routes makes delivery distance highly sensitive to depot placement, and terminal or poorly positioned depots often lead to increased detours and higher overall cost.
Grid layouts
feature regular, orthogonal arrangements with multiple alternative paths, providing strong robustness to depot placement and environmental variations. While centrally located depots generally perform well, clustered demand can shift the optimal depot position away from the geometric center.
Irregular layouts
, shaped by terrain constraints or historical development, exhibit non-uniform building spacing and curvilinear paths. Although such layouts tend to increase path length variability and reduce global optimality, they reflect realistic residential environments; in these cases, adaptive depot relocation and multi-depot deployment become particularly effective.
For all layout typologies, buildings are abstracted as nodes in a delivery graph, with inter-building travel costs computed using shortest-path distances along the corresponding layout. This unified abstraction enables a quantitative comparison of delivery efficiency across different layouts and supports systematic analysis of depot adjustment strategies. Results indicate that relocating depots toward high-demand regions or introducing auxiliary depots in demand-intensive areas can substantially reduce total delivery distance, especially in layouts with limited path redundancy or highly uneven demand distributions.

Delivery Strategies
Four representative strategies are evaluated.
Random Baseline:
Packages are dispatched in a random first-in-first-out (FIFO) order.
A single tour may include packages for multiple buildings and floors, leading to inefficient inter-building traversal and highly suboptimal intra-building routes.
Sequential Baseline:
Packages are delivered sequentially by building ID, and floors within each building are also served in a fixed order.
Most tours follow the pattern

Hybrid Strategy (Optimized Stage 1 + Random Stage 2):
This strategy applies an optimized solution for Stage 1 inter-building routing to improve capacity utilization across buildings, while Stage 2 intra-building delivery remains randomly ordered.
As a result, inter-building travel is reduced, but inefficient intra-building routing still dominates the overall cost.
Hierarchical CVRP Model:
The proposed approach formulates the delivery task as a hierarchical capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP).
Inter-building routing is optimized to allow partial loads across buildings, while intra-building delivery jointly optimizes floor and room visitation sequences before returning to the building entrance.
By optimizing both stages in a unified framework, the model achieves globally optimal delivery loops.

6.4
Practical Deployment
The complete autonomous delivery system was deployed and validated through multiple real-world delivery missions spanning several buildings and floors.
Figure 22:
Representative real-world deployment of the autonomous delivery system
Fig.
22
illustrates a representative deployment scenario. Each mission begins at a centralized depot, where parcels are loaded into the robot’s onboard locker. Guided by a CVRP-optimized delivery plan, the robot autonomously navigates to the target building. Upon arrival, the robot interacts with building infrastructure, including access control systems and elevators, to reach the designated floor. During operation, the robot performs continuous localization and perception, enabling safe navigation and real-time avoidance of both static obstacles and dynamic human traffic. Upon reaching the target room, the robot executes the final delivery by actuating the locker mechanism to release the parcel.
Field deployment results demonstrate the practical robustness and reliability of the proposed system, including reliable elevator interaction with accurate floor detection and consistent re-localization across vertical transitions, robust corridor navigation that enables smooth and collision-free motion in dynamic environments with human presence, and consistent task execution under hierarchical constraints, validating the seamless integration between high-level route planning and low-level motion control while respecting privacy and access constraints.
7
Conclusion
This work presents a hierarchical optimization framework for autonomous delivery in large-scale buildings based on a Building–Floor–Room abstraction. By decomposing the original routing problem across multiple spatial levels, the proposed formulation significantly reduces optimization complexity while preserving global feasibility and consistency. A building-aware extension of the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) is introduced to explicitly model inter-building connectivity, inter-floor transitions, room-level access constraints, and privacy-sensitive regions. The hierarchical model further provides a unified analytical basis for evaluating delivery efficiency under different building layouts, and enables consistent optimization of depot placement and room-level access strategies.
To demonstrate practical realizability, the proposed algorithms are instantiated in a complete autonomous delivery system that tightly integrates hierarchical planning with mapping, localization, and navigation. A non-interactive, privacy-preserving delivery robot and reachability-aware distance metrics derived from real building geometry ensure consistency between theoretical planning results and physical execution. Extensive multi-building and multi-floor experiments confirm that the algorithmic advantages translate into reliable real-world performance, effectively bridging abstract optimization models and deployable autonomous delivery systems.
Future work will explore theoretical performance bounds for the hierarchical routing formulation, extension to multi-robot cooperative optimization, and uncertainty-aware planning under dynamic demand and access constraints, further strengthening both the theoretical foundations and practical scalability of hierarchical autonomous delivery.
