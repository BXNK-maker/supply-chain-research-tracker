Abstract
Routing problems, like the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP), are NP-hard and slow to solve for large instances. Diffusion-based solvers can be trained in a supervised way using sets of optimal solutions, and then quickly solve new test instances. However,
for large instances, optimal solutions are hard to find, and diffusion models fail to generalize to instance sizes outside their training distribution.
Here we describe ResizeCO, a label-free size-generalization approach that addresses both the change in statistical properties of larger instances, and the computational challenge of solving them quickly. It uses reinforcement learning on large-scale instances to adapt a diffusion model trained on small instances. Specifically, we adapt GRPO to this problem, using route length as the training signal.
ResizeCO also formulates
k
k
-opt as a tree search, solved by a beam search on the GPU. In size-generalization experiments on TSP and CVRP instances with up to 10000 nodes, ResizeCO achieves a Pareto front over route quality and time that dominates learned solvers, and also outperforms other solvers at short run times, setting a new state of the art.

Introduction
Combinatorial optimization (CO) problems, and specifically routing problems, appear in many real-world systems, from logistics and supply-chain planning to chip design and scheduling.
Among these, probably the most well-studied and widely deployed are the
Traveling Salesman Problem
(TSP) and the
Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem
(CVRP), both NP-hard.
For small instances, heuristic approaches can efficiently find near-optimal solutions for these problems, but solving large instances could take hours. Solving within seconds would open new possibilities for real-world problems like on-the-fly routing, interactive “what-if” planning, and large-scale routing, like coordinating deliveries within a city.
Supervised learning methods offer a promising approach for fast combinatorial optimization, because the long training cost is amortized over many new instances, each solved quickly at inference
(
Joshi et al., 2019
;
Luo et al., 2023
)
. Diffusion-based generative solvers, trained by adding noise to precomputed optimal solutions, now match or beat hand-designed heuristics when tested on in-distribution instances
(
Li et al., 2025
;
Chen et al., 2026
)
. Unfortunately, supervised training on large instances may be prohibitively expensive because it requires creating a large number of near-optimal solutions as labels. This effect is quantified in
Section
D.1
for TSP and CVRP. At the same time, current supervised approaches also fail at size generalization, namely, diffusion models trained on small instances perform poorly on large instances. Together, scaling supervised training to large instances is challenging.
Here we describe ResizeCO, a label-free size-generalization approach, designed to generalize from small to large instances.
ResizeCO trains a diffusion model on small instances and then fine-tunes the model using reinforcement learning (RL) on large-scale instances.
ResizeCO tackles the two main challenges of size generalization: the fact that statistical properties of instances may change with size, and the efficiency challenge of optimizing larger instances. ResizeCO addresses these using an integrative approach that handles architecture, fine-tuning and inference.
(1) Architecture: We design a variant of Graph Transformer to generalize better across instance sizes. (2) RL fine-tuning: We adapt GRPO to provide label-free fine-tuning of a diffusion model trained on small instances. Specifically, we use a node-walk decoder, which provides a better estimate of a trajectory probability given the model. (3) Inference: We design a new and efficient
beam
k
k
-opt search
that runs in parallel on the GPU, which improves inference quality.
We evaluate ResizeCO on TSP and CVRP and in several size-generalization setups, comparing to both machine-learning and fast non-learned approaches. ResizeCO achieves a new state-of-the-art (SoTA) quality for short run times for several size-generalization setups, and dominates learning-based methods over the full Pareto front.
Our main contributions are: (1) ResizeCO is the first diffusion solver trained on small instances and adapted to large instances without labels, becoming the first diffusion solver trained for CVRP instances of size 10000. (2) Using node-walk decoding, we introduce an exact probability model over the routes sampled from a masked diffusion solver, then used to weight GRPO trajectories. (3) An efficient formulation of
k
k
-opt as a tree search and a beam-search approach that can be run on the GPU to solve it efficiently. (4) A new SoTA in size generalization on both CVRP and TSP for short durations, and Pareto-domination over learning-based methods in terms of time vs. solution quality.

Our Method: ResizeCO
To allow diffusion-based combinatorial optimization to generalize to large instances, we need to address two challenges:
(1)
Size generalization
– handling instances that come from a distribution of large instances; and (2)
computational scalability

4.1

Section
4.2
), and (C)
fast parallel inference
whose quality scales with the test-time budget (
Section
4.3
).

Simply put, in each move

Conclusion
We described ResizeCO, a diffusion-based approach for routing optimization that generalizes a model trained on small instances to large instances, using RL on large instances without labels. It combines a Graph Transformer encoder, label-free fine-tuning with GRPO and a GPU beam
k
k
-opt search, and our ablation shows that removing any one of these components increases the gap. In size-generalization experiments on TSP and CVRP instances with up to 10000 nodes, ResizeCO reaches the lowest gap of every learned solver at a comparable run time, and on CVRP its margin over the strongest learned baseline grows with the target size. From CVRP-1000 to CVRP-10000, its routes are shorter than the HGS reference solutions. ResizeCO also reaches the lowest gap among the learned solvers in five of the six groups of real-world instances from TSPLIB and CVRPLIB, although we train on synthetic instances only. Together, these results set a new SoTA at short run times, which may open new applications like on-the-fly interactive routing.

The Sequential Search of LKH
LKH searches this tree depth-first, that is, it follows one branch as deep as it can, backtracks when the gain is no longer positive, and tries the next branch. When a closed branch gives a shorter route, LKH applies this one move and starts again from the new route. When no branch gives a shorter route, LKH runs a non-sequential search for the moves that no branch contains. The search continues as long as it finds a better route. Each decision therefore depends on the result of the decision before it, and the work differs from route to route. In contrast, a GPU is efficient when many routes perform the same computation at the same moment, so this search runs poorly on a GPU.

(4) Double-bridge moves in every sweep.
Instead of a separate non-sequential search, every sweep also searches for double-bridge moves. It scores, in one pass, every splitting move that adds an edge between a node and one of the candidates of that node, and keeps the splitting moves with the largest gains. Two of them whose cuts alternate along the route form a double bridge, because the second is the fix step of the first, and the gain of the double bridge is the sum of their gains. A sweep keeps as many double-bridge moves as closures.
(5) Many moves per sweep.
Instead of applying one move at a time, a sweep applies many kept moves together, closures and double-bridge moves alike, as long as they do not overlap on the route. Specifically, each kept move spans a range of route positions, from the first to the last node it touches, and moves whose ranges are at least one position apart can be applied together, because their gains add up and the route stays a single cycle. We find the set of such ranges with the largest total gain by dynamic programming over the ranges sorted by their right end (weighted interval scheduling).
