Abstract
Column generation folklore warns that initial columns hurt: a warm start may bias the dual and slow
the root of branch-price-and-cut. We show the opposite once the columns are imperfect in the right
way. Preloading the support of the optimal root LP, the best columns one could know in advance, fails to
finish on half of our hardest instances; a pool of ordinary routes that need not belong to any optimal
solution, held outside the master and priced at the current smoothed dual, speeds the root up. Because the columns need not be optimal, producing them becomes a learning task: a
neural edge scorer guides a constructive generator that builds the pool in seconds. A second
network predicts the initial center of Wentges smoothing from the instance file, and with it the
direction of the smoothed pricing queries; the solver’s own updates run unchanged. With prefill and center
in place, exact pricing dominates root pricing time, so we rebuild bucket-graph labeling around its
dominance test for the GPU and certify on the CPU. Inside RouteOpt, the state-of-the-art exact
solver for capacitated vehicle routing, the pipeline reaches the same certified root bound in
13.2 % of vanilla RouteOpt’s root pricing time over 95 instances with
300
300
to
1000
1000
customers, prefill alone in 26.1 %, and 17.5
times faster in the best case. In an exact branch-price-and-cut solver for unrelated parallel
machine scheduling, prefill with
smoothing and the learned center certifies all 25 benchmark instances,
12 times faster than the solver’s own column generation, which certifies 20 of them.

Introduction
Branch-price-and-cut (BPC) is the leading exact approach for large-scale NP-hard
optimization problems, such as vehicle routing, crew and machine scheduling, and cutting stock
(
Barnhart et al., 1998
;
Lübbecke & Desrosiers, 2005
;
van den Akker et al., 1999
;
Costa et al., 2019
)
. Within branch-and-bound, column generation (CG) solves the
Dantzig–Wolfe relaxation by generating necessary variables on the fly through solving a pricing subproblem, and
cutting planes are typically applied to strengthen the bound. Work on accelerating BPC has therefore concentrated on
branching, pricing, and cutting
(
Fukasawa et al., 2006
;
Jepsen et al., 2008
;
Baldacci et al., 2011
;
Pecin et al., 2017a
;
Pessoa et al., 2020
;
Sadykov et al., 2021
)
, with arc elimination
(
Irnich et al., 2010
)
, route enumeration
(
Baldacci et al., 2008
)
and dual stabilization
(
du Merle et al., 1999
;
Wentges, 1997
;
Pessoa et al., 2018
)
as complementary
accelerators. All of these techniques apply across problems, but pricing is where the problem’s structure
enters. Our main test bed is RouteOpt
(
You & Yang, 2026
)
, an exact solver for the capacitated
vehicle routing problem (CVRP) with a state-of-the-art integrated implementation of all of the above.
With the CPU-side toolbox this mature, learning and GPU computing offer two further directions. Learning has
been applied to branching
(
Khalil et al., 2016
;
Gasse et al., 2019
;
Bengio et al., 2021
;
You et al., 2026
)
, to cutting plane generation and
selection
(
Tang et al., 2020
;
Paulus et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Deza & Khalil, 2023
)
, to
column generation and selection
(
Morabit et al., 2021
;
Chi et al., 2022
;
Morabit et al., 2023
;
Shen et al., 2022
;
Xu et al., 2025
;
Koutecká et al., 2025
;
Václavík et al., 2018
)
and to dual
stabilization
(
Kraul et al., 2023
;
Babaki et al., 2022
;
Shen et al., 2024
;
Sarkar et al., 2025
;
Fang et al., 2025
;
Wang & Khir, 2026
)
. On the GPU side, first-order methods have moved the linear-programming solve onto the GPU
(
Lu & Yang, 2025
)
, and GPU labeling exists for constrained shortest paths outside BPC
(
Lu et al., 2020
)
, but the exact pricer of a state-of-the-art BPC solver has stayed on the CPU. It is worth noting that gains beyond a modest research
baseline can shrink or reverse inside a state-of-the-art solver, so a method of practical value has
to be demonstrated against an efficient implementation.
Our target is
root pricing
: the column generation that solves the root relaxation before
any cutting plane is added. Its wall-clock time is our outcome, and every configuration we compare that completes ends at
the same certified root bound, so root pricing is a clean test bed and delivers a certified lower
bound of practical value on its own. Our method is a combination of three moves: a prefilled
archive supplies candidate columns, a learned initial center shapes the pricing queries, and a GPU
accelerates the exact pricing level.
We started from a piece of folklore. Textbooks warn that a warm start, even from an excellent primal solution, can
hinder column generation
(
Lübbecke & Desrosiers, 2005
;
Vanderbeck, 2005
)
: the initial
columns pull the dual toward the solution they encode, and in the root of a modern BPC, heuristic routes are elementary and need not suffice to attain or certify the ng-route
bound that pricing works over
(
Baldacci et al., 2011
)
, while a
large pool adds to the complexity of solving the restricted master problem (RMP). Our measurements confirmed the warning on deep instances: preloading a heuristic pool into the
master slowed the root down there, and preloading the support of the optimal root LP, the best
columns one could know in advance, was the worst choice of all (Section
3.1
,
Appendix
D.1
). What did help was the opposite of what
the folklore protects against. A diverse pool of ordinary, non-optimal routes, held
outside
the master as a lazy archive and queried at the current smoothed dual, shortens
root pricing by a large factor on every benchmark set and route-size class we test
(Section
4.2
). Learning for column generation has so far concentrated on the
pricing loop itself, on selecting, ranking, or generating columns during the iterations. We take a
different perspective: leave the pricer untouched and learn the prefilling, with a constructive
generator guided by a neural edge scorer, whose pool need not be optimal.
Prefilling alone is not the whole story. In an earlier development experiment, dumping a
metaheuristic pool into the initial master slowed the root down, as the folklore predicts
(Appendix
D.1
): the pricer spent many rounds searching for the direction of the
dual and accumulated a bloated LP. The
classical remedy is dual smoothing
(
du Merle et al., 1999
;
Wentges, 1997
;
Pessoa et al., 2018
)
, and the learned variants predict the optimal dual and install it as the
reference point
(
Kraul et al., 2023
;
Shen et al., 2024
)
. Where those methods learn the values of the dual, we learn its direction: the RMP has many optimal duals, so what
matters is the direction from which the query approaches the optimum, not its distance to one
optimal point. We therefore learn the
initial
center of the standard Wentges smoothing from the
instance file, calibrate its scale, and let the solver’s own rule move the center and adapt the
weight from there. On the hardest instances in particular, the learned center becomes decisive, especially in combination with prefill (Section
4.2
, Appendix
D.2
).
Prefill and center cut the run time of heuristic pricing sharply, so exact pricing, the level that certifies
optimality, becomes the dominant share of root time. Bucket-graph labeling is the exact pricer of
modern routing solvers
(
Sadykov et al., 2021
)
, and its dominance
test is the cost that structure exists to contain; parallel CPU labeling has been developed for it
(
Petersen & Spoorendonk, 2025
)
, while GPU work on BPC has addressed the linear-programming solve
(
Lu & Yang, 2025
)
. We rebuild exact bucket-graph labeling around the
dominance test for the GPU, with a final CPU certificate
(Sections
3.3
and
4.3
). The construction applies to any bucket-graph-based pricer once the
resource extension, the dominance rule, and the join are adapted to the problem. Our
contributions
are summarized as follows.
•
A general learn-to-prefill framework: a neural edge scorer and a constructive generator whose pool
is served to root pricing as a lazy archive. This opens a new direction for learning in column
generation, one that is both highly effective and broadly applicable (Section
3.1
;
Appendix
D.1
).
•
A learned dual direction under prefilled columns: the initial center of Wentges smoothing,
predicted by a neural network, together with a calibration that emphasizes the direction and
relaxes its absolute magnitude to better steer the prefilled columns (Section
3.2
,
Appendix
D.2
).
•
A GPU backend for exact bucket-graph labeling with CPU certification, which applies to
any BPC solver with bucket-graph pricing (Sections
3.3
and
4.3
).
•
A general plug-in pipeline for problems solved by BPC, which delivers strong performance
inside optimized exact BPC solvers: the state-of-the-art CVRP solver RouteOpt and an exact
solver for unrelated parallel machine scheduling (Sections
4.2
to
4.4
).

3.3
GPU exact pricing
The first two components change where root pricing spends its time. Prefill and a learned center reduce the work at the heuristic levels, and in our development runs this left exact pricing as the largest stage of the run and the next target for acceleration. The CPU profile of Appendix
C.2
identifies the
dominance test as the hotspot, and its per-level counters show that an exact call evaluates orders
of magnitude more dominance predicates than a heuristic call. Appendix
C.1
reviews
bucket-graph pricing: labels are extended bucket by bucket, each arrival is checked against the
current contents of its target bucket, and the resulting insertions and deletions create a
sequential dependence between the arrivals to a bucket. Our GPU exact pricing removes this
dependence in three steps.
1.
Batched dominance.
Labels enter a bucket in waves. Each wave passes three dominance
stages: stored labels against candidates, candidates against earlier candidates in a canonical
order, and the surviving candidates against the stored labels. The stages run in sequence and the
comparisons within a stage in parallel; every deletion keeps a direct dominator that survives the
wave, with conservative floating-point guards and a repair pass that restores any candidate left
without a surviving direct dominator.
2.
Labeling redesigned around the test.
RouteOpt’s push of one label at a time into the next bucket
becomes a level-synchronous, wave-based expansion in which the arrivals to a bucket are batched
instead of chained, the form parallel CPU labeling also takes
(
Petersen & Spoorendonk, 2025
)
: resource levels are processed in order, waves repeat within a level
until its frontier is empty, and all label state is in structure-of-arrays form. Label extension,
dominance testing, and the join of half-routes run on the GPU; route reconstruction,
deduplication, and the final column selection run on the CPU, the heuristic levels stay on the
CPU, and dispatch sits inside the exact level, so the CG loop is unchanged.
3.
Memory and filtering.
Shared-memory tiles reuse candidate prefixes across comparisons,
and the cross-bucket filter runs one warp
per frontier label and stops at a reduced-cost prefix bound.
Bucket-graph labeling is the exact pricer of state-of-the-art vehicle-routing solvers, among them
VRPSolver and RouteOpt
(
Sadykov et al., 2021
;
Pessoa et al., 2020
;
You & Yang, 2026
)
. They share the
label-comparison structure the three steps act on, whose dominance test is the cost the bucket graph
exists to contain
(
Sadykov et al., 2021
)
, so the steps carry over once the resource extension,
the dominance rule, and the join are adapted. Appendices
C.3
and
C.4
give the implementation details and the safeguards behind the optimality guarantee.

5
Conclusion
This paper breaks with the folklore that initial columns harm column generation and turns
prefilling into a general plug-in accelerator for BPC: learning to prefill the
columns. The idea at its center is that imperfect columns, routes that need not belong to any optimal
solution but together cover the customers well, are what speeds the root up, provided they are held
outside the master as a lazy archive and served at the current smoothed dual. We built a
learn-to-prefill pipeline on this idea and obtained large speed-ups on two problems inside two exact
solvers, CVRP in RouteOpt and parallel-machine scheduling in the code of
Hijazi et al. (2026)
. On the hardest instances prefill also showed its weakness: the dual it
produces is close to the optimal dual in value but deviates from it in direction, so the query
needs a correction of direction rather than of distance, and we designed a learned dual center for
that purpose. Once archive and center are in place, the remaining bottleneck of the pipeline is
exact pricing, and we rebuilt it around its dominance test for the GPU. On the state-of-the-art CVRP
solver the complete pipeline reaches the certified root bound with a
7.6-fold geometric-mean speed-up of root pricing over vanilla RouteOpt, and in
the best case in 5.7 % of vanilla RouteOpt’s time.
The main limitation is that the effect on the full BPC tree is unknown: at the
scale we test, the instances have no known exact solution time, and root pricing is the part we can
measure and certify; extending the measurement to the tree is one direction of future work. A
second direction is the co-design of the two learned components, which we designed independently,
either as a joint configuration or as a single model that predicts pool and center together.

Appendix C
GPU exact pricing in detail
C.1
Bucket-graph labeling and the dominance test
RouteOpt prices by bucket-graph labeling
(
Sadykov et al., 2021
)
. A label is a partial route
leaving the depot together with its reduced cost, its resource consumption, its ng memory and, in
the tree, its cut states. The labels at a customer are kept in buckets indexed by a resource
interval, and the algorithm processes buckets in resource order, extending each label along the
bucket arcs and the jump arcs into the buckets of its successors. In RouteOpt’s own implementation this
is a
push
: an extended label arrives at its target bucket and is checked there against the
bucket’s move-to-front list of stored labels, with early exit once a dominator is found; before a
stored label is extended, a cross-bucket check compares it with the earlier buckets of the same
customer through reduced-cost prefix bounds; buckets are sorted by reduced cost when their level is
done. The predicate compares resources, tests ng-set inclusion and, with cuts, checks a
reduced-cost gap corrected by the cut duals; the LIGHT and HEAVY levels use weaker predicates,
which is why they keep few labels and run quickly. Because every arrival is checked against the
bucket as it stands at that moment and changes it, the arrivals to a bucket form a chain; this is
the sequential dependence the backend removes. Under symmetry RouteOpt labels forward to the meet
point and joins two forward half-routes.

C.3
Removing the sequential dependence
The backend keeps the contract of the CPU pricer and changes the shape of the computation. At
each resource level it expands the current frontier and groups the candidates by target bucket;
same-level candidates form the next wave of that level, and candidates for later levels wait for
theirs. The candidates of one bucket in a wave form a segment, tested in three stages. Stored
labels against candidates: each candidate independently, stopping at its first dominator, whose
stored label is marked as a witness. Candidates against candidates in the canonical order
(reduced cost, resources, ng cardinality, generation index): the serial reference accepts
candidates in that order, and the exact GPU mode compares each candidate in parallel against all
earlier candidates that survived the first stage. Surviving candidates against the stored labels,
with marked witnesses exempt from deletion. A repair pass then restores any deleted candidate
without a surviving direct dominator. Every deleted label therefore has a dominator alive at the
end of the wave, an invariant maintained by retaining the stored labels that serve as deletion
witnesses and stronger than what RouteOpt’s own implementation offers under tolerance. Label state is
held in structure-of-arrays form; shared-memory tiles reuse candidate prefixes across the
comparisons of a block; an optional warp-per-segment path for small segments exists and is
disabled in the deployed configuration; the cross-bucket filter runs one warp per frontier label
and uses the same reduced-cost prefix bound as the CPU. Label extension, dominance testing and the
forward–forward join run on the GPU; route reconstruction, deduplication by canonical route
identity and the final selection of returned columns run on the CPU, and with stable numbering,
canonical sorting and fixed scan rules the output does not depend on scheduling. The heuristic
levels, the master and the cut loop stay on the CPU, and dispatch sits inside the exact level, so
the column-generation loop is unchanged.
