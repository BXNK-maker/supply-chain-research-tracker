Abstract
We study a stylized single-vehicle replenishment-routing problem in which the cities of a national logistics network are both demand points and supply points: a vehicle of limited capacity delivers to every city once, and when its load is insufficient for the next city it must detour to a city already served that can still supply goods. A 100-node benchmark is built from Korean population and geographic data, with the cities divided into 10 levels; the per-visit supply coefficient and the allowed number of stops decrease geometrically with the level. Building on matrix-based ant colony optimization, we keep the states of the whole colony in arrays, couple route construction with a fixed greedy replenishment-decoding policy, and evaluate the candidate moves of a local search in batches. With frozen parameters and 30 runs on seeds never used for tuning, the matrix-based ant colony search with the batch local search (MACO) reaches a mean total distance of 3081.7 km; a genetic algorithm equipped with the same local search at the same frequency reaches 3074.9 km (no significant difference), and a MAX–MIN ant system with the same local search 3142.2 km. Ablation identifies the batch local search as the main contributor to solution quality; a load-aware transition factor gives no significant gain. Within the parameter ranges tested, raising the supply coefficients of the cities below the top level shortens the route more than relaxing the stop limits, which are not binding here.

I
Introduction
Logistics planning is commonly modeled by the traveling salesman problem (TSP) and the vehicle routing problem (VRP)
[
1
,
2
]
. A national logistics system, however, has a clear urban hierarchy. The capital and the metropolitan cities have large populations and large storage facilities; they consume goods and can also supply passing vehicles. Small cities have small demands and limited storage and handling capacity. A vehicle has limited capacity: when the goods on board are not enough for the next city, it must detour to a city that it has already served and that can still supply goods. Which cities are used for reloading, and how long the detours are, depend on the order in which the cities are served, so the cost of a visiting order is not determined by its own edges alone; it is obtained by following the route and its reload detours step by step.
Routing in which a vehicle reloads on its way has been studied in several forms: inventory routing

In these models the reload points are dedicated facilities or customers that only supply; here they are the served cities themselves, each with a demand of its own and available only after it has been served, with a per-stop supply that decreases with its level and a limited number of stops. We describe this hierarchy with two geometric sequences: the per-visit supply coefficient decreases with ratio

In replenishment routing each ant also carries a load, a set of served cities and stop counts that decide its detours, so the construction must be coupled with a state-dependent decoding step. This paper makes the following contributions.
1.
We formulate a stylized single-vehicle replenishment-routing benchmark from Korean population and geographic data: 100 cities and counties in 10 levels, with level-dependent per-visit supply limits and stop allowances. The model, its fixed greedy decoding policy and the distinction between the two are stated explicitly (Section III).
2.
Building on matrix-based ACO, we develop a batched implementation that maintains the load, service and stop-count states of all ants, couples route construction with the replenishment decoding so that the cost of every ant is available when construction ends, and evaluates the candidate moves of a local search in batches (Section IV). The matrix form is a formulation, and its batch decoder is the evaluation engine shared by all algorithms compared; no speed-up over an ant-by-ant implementation is claimed.
3.
We show on the benchmark, against competitors that share the same decoder and the same local search, that the quality of the routes comes from the batch local search rather than from the ant colony construction or from a one-step load-aware transition factor, and that the supply coefficients of the lower-level cities shape the routes more than their stop limits, which are not binding here (Section V).

VI
Conclusion
We studied a stylized replenishment-routing problem in which the served cities of a national network become capacity-limited reload points, with per-visit supply and stop allowances that fall geometrically with the level of the city, and built a 100-city benchmark for it from Korean data. Because the cost of a visiting order is only known after its reload detours have been decoded, we organized a matrix-based ant colony search around this decoding: the load, service and stop-count states of the whole colony are kept in arrays, construction and decoding proceed together, and the candidate moves of the local search are decoded in batches.
On the benchmark the routes of the method do not differ significantly in length from those of a genetic algorithm with the same local search and are shorter than those of the MAX–MIN ant system with the same local search, and the batch local search, not the colony construction, is what makes them short; a one-step load-aware factor does not help and, as a penalty, hurts. Raising the supply coefficients of the lower-level cities shortens the routes more than relaxing their stop limits, which are not binding on this instance. These findings are confined to one stylized instance and to the local-search setting used (frequency and candidate number
K
K
were not varied): they establish neither general superiority of the ant colony construction, nor a speed advantage of the matrix form, nor operational validity for the real network. The natural next steps are an exact reload sub-problem to quantify the loss of the greedy policy, construction rules that anticipate the remaining route, further instances, road distances and travel times, and multiple vehicles and depots.
