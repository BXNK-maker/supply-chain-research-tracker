Distribution logistics with capacity constraints and service priorities can be modeled as a variant of the

1
Introduction
Logistics efficiency is one of the determining factors for the competitiveness of distribution companies. In a typical setting, a driver departs from a central depot with a truck loaded with orders and must visit a set of customers spread over a geographic region, returning to the starting point at the end of the journey. Two optimization problems arise naturally and interdependently: the selection of which orders to load given the vehicle’s weight limit, and the determination of the visiting order of the selected customers.
The first is an instance of the
0/1 Knapsack Problem
, in which indivisible items must be selected to maximize total value within a capacity
(
Kellerer et al., 2004
)
. The second is an instance of the
Traveling Salesman Problem
(TSP), one of the most studied NP-hard problems in Computer Science
(
Garey and Johnson, 1979
)
.
The combination of these two problems, together with the possibility of
not visiting
all customers, places this work within the family of
TSP with Profits
(TSPP).
Feillet et al. (2005)
classify TSPP into three main categories: the
Orienteering Problem
(OP), which maximizes collected profit subject to a route duration limit
(
Vansteenwegen et al., 2011
)
; the
Prize-Collecting TSP
(PCTSP), which minimizes travel cost subject to a minimum profit
(
Balas, 1989
)
; and the
Profitable Tour Problem
(PTP), which maximizes the difference between profit and cost
(
Dell’Amico et al., 1995
)
. The variant addressed here — customer selection via priority-weighted 0/1 Knapsack followed by TSP routing — is a form of
hierarchical decomposition
that approximates the OP with a capacity constraint rather than a duration constraint.
The contribution of this work is
applied and methodological
: (i) to propose a quadratic priority weighting in the Knapsack objective function, with a sensitivity analysis of the exponent; (ii) to parameterize the priority bias in the Nearest Neighbor heuristic and provide a geometric interpretation of the preference condition; (iii) to present a comparative experimental protocol that allows assessing the quality of the hierarchical decomposition against alternatives; and (iv) to explicitly discuss the limitations of this decomposition.
The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the theoretical background; Section 3 details the mathematical formulation; Section 4 describes the algorithms; Section 5 presents the methodology and experimental protocol; Section 6 discusses the results; Section 7 analyzes the parameter
α
\alpha
; and Section 8 provides conclusions and limitations.

2.3
TSP with Profits and the Orienteering Problem
The family of
TSP with Profits
(TSPP) generalizes the TSP by allowing not all vertices to be visited, associating a profit with each vertex and a cost with each edge.
Feillet et al. (2005)
provide a comprehensive classification: the

The OP is formally described by
Vansteenwegen et al. (2011)

, whereas this work imposes a

For OP instances and variants,

(
Chagas et al., 2021

2.4
Hierarchical Decomposition in Routing
The strategy of first solving selection (Knapsack) and then routing (TSP) is a form of
hierarchical decomposition
.
Laporte (1992)
discuss this type of approach in the context of location-routing problems. The advantage is simplicity and the guarantee of optimality in the selection sub-step (via exact DP). The disadvantage, often overlooked, is that the global solution may be suboptimal: a low-priority customer located very close to the route could, if included, reduce the total route cost enough to offset its low priority — but the Knapsack excludes it beforehand because it does not consider geometry.
This limitation is inherent to the decomposition and must be made explicit. An alternative would be to solve the integrated problem as an OP with a capacity constraint, using a metaheuristic that evaluates selection and routing simultaneously
(
Chagas et al., 2021
)
. The present proposal does not aim to overcome this limitation, but to offer an applied, low-computational-cost solution for scenarios where priority is the dominant criterion.

8
Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work
This work presented an applied routing variant with customer selection, combining priority-aware 0/1 Knapsack with priority-biased TSP. The contributions are: (i) quadratic priority weighting in the Knapsack objective function, with sensitivity analysis; (ii) a parameterizable priority score in the VNN-P, with geometric interpretation; (iii) a comparative experimental protocol that allows assessing the quality of the hierarchical decomposition; and (iv) an explicit discussion of the limitations.
Limitations.
The hierarchical decomposition (Knapsack
→
\to
TSP) does not guarantee global optimality: a low-priority customer close to the route may be excluded, increasing total distance. Moreover, the base scenario is single and small-scale; generalization to larger instances requires the experiments proposed in Section
5.2
. The parameter
α
\alpha
was calibrated heuristically, without systematic search.
Future work.
(i) Execute the full experimental protocol and report comparative results; (ii) extend to multiple vehicles (CVRP) and time windows (VRPTW); (iii) apply metaheuristics for PTP/OP
(
Chagas et al., 2021
)
to the TSP component on larger instances; (iv) use public OP benchmarks
(
Vansteenwegen et al., 2011
;
Gunawan et al., 2016
)
for comparison; (v) integrate real geospatial data via OpenStreetMap.
