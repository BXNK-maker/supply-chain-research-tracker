Abstract
We study the dynamic allocation of scarce medications in Liberia’s community health program. A central warehouse supplies a health facility and the Community Health Assistants (CHAs) it supervises. When a CHA runs out of medication, patients are referred to the facility, incurring travel costs if they complete the referral and remain untreated otherwise. Allocating inventory to CHAs allows more patients to receive treatment without traveling, while retaining inventory centrally preserves flexibility to respond to uncertain demand. The allocation problem must balance these considerations while accounting for fairness across locations.
We formulate this problem as a finite-horizon stochastic control problem whose objective combines the costs of referrals and untreated patients with a demand-weighted fairness utility. Because standard dynamic programming methods are computationally intractable in this setting, we develop a continuous-time approximation that involves controlling the drift rate of a reflected Brownian motion. We then adapt the deep splitting method, using deep neural networks to approximate the value function and its gradient and compute a dynamic allocation policy.
We calibrate the model using data on malaria cases among children in the Fangonda region of Lofa County, Liberia, and evaluate the proposed policy in the original discrete-time model. In the baseline setting, our policy reduces operating costs by about 6% relative to the strongest benchmark, the fairness-only policy, while retaining approximately 98% of its fairness utility. Additional experiments show that higher referral costs shift inventory toward CHAs and reduce local unmet demand, while greater demand variability increases our policy’s performance over the fairness-only benchmark. These findings illustrate the value of dynamic policies. More broadly, our model and computational approach provide a framework for studying similar last-mile allocation problems in which scarce resources must be distributed dynamically across multiple service locations.

Introduction
Liberia’s health system relies on Community Health Assistants (CHAs), who provide integrated community case management (iCCM) diagnosis and treatment during regular household visits. The widespread iCCM approach focuses on malaria, pneumonia, and diarrhea cases in children under the age of five. The CHA program is intended to extend the reach of established health facilities to serve populations that would otherwise face difficulties in accessing healthcare, and is most effective when CHAs are not only able to diagnose but also treat cases they encounter in the field. When CHAs diagnose cases but are unable to treat patients during a household visit, they must refer these patients to the nearest regional health facility. There are two principal causes for CHAs being unable to treat patients: either the patients require care that is beyond the scope of CHA training (usually when the patient is too sick or the case involves complicating factors), or the CHA does not have the necessary medications to treat a case he or she would have otherwise been able to treat in the field. The latter of these two causes results from a point-of-care stockout. While the set of referrals resulting from required care beyond the scope of CHA practice does not have a straightforward solution, the number of referrals resulting from point-of-care stockouts can be reduced through better inventory management strategies.
This aim is a point of focus for our implementing partner, The International Rescue Committee’s (IRC) Liberia country office, which identified the reduction of CHA health facility referrals as an important area for improvement within the community health system. These referrals can place a significant burden on families, often in the form of travel costs and lost wages. In most cases, a referral requires the child and caregiver to either walk or pay for transportation to the nearest health facility. In Lofa County specifically, where we source data for this project, the average distance between the communities served by CHAs and health facilities is greater than 5km.
The health facilities in Liberia involved in the CHA program each oversee a set of CHAs serving communities within its region. Community Health Services Supervisors (CHSS) attached to these health facilities are responsible for the ongoing training and supervision of the CHAs. In this role, CHSSs are the main source of communication between CHAs and the health facilities, as well as a means for transporting medications to CHAs. The health facility managers must decide how much medication to retain at the health facility and how much to distribute among CHAs. Allocating more medication to CHAs should reduce the number of referrals, but increases the risk of stockouts at the health facility. Additionally, the distribution of more medication to all CHAs without accounting for demand patterns may leave some CHAs with excess, unused supplies. We model this setting to acount for the trade-offs between holding more inventory centrally as opposed to dispersing more medication among CHAs, while also accounting for demand pattern differences between CHA locations.
Focusing on malaria cases among children under the age of five, we formulate an inventory rationing problem within a two-echelon supply network and develop a dynamic allocation policy. The system consists of a central health facility and multiple geographically dispersed CHAs. We model demand at each location as a binomial random variable, which we approximate using a Gaussian distribution. To balance illness and travel costs with geographical equity considerations across the populations served by the CHAs and the health facility, we adopt an objective function that combines these costs with an
α
\alpha
-fair utility function.
As a solution for this objective, we pursue a dynamic rationing policy for this problem. Standard dynamic programming methods are computationally intractable in this setting due to the curse of dimensionality. We therefore develop a continuous-time approximation that involves controlling the drift rate of a reflected Brownian motion (RBM) in the orthant. To solve this approximate model, we develop a computational method based on deep neural networks. Specifically, we build on the deep splitting method of
Beck et al. (2021)
to approximate the value function and its gradient, enabling us to solve the problem in high dimensions.
We calibrate our model using data from the Fangonda region of Lofa County, Liberia, and evaluate the proposed policy in the original discrete-time model. Our policy outperforms the benchmark policies considered in this paper. Relative to the fairness-only policy, the strongest benchmark, our policy reduces operating costs by about 6% while retaining approximately 98% of the benchmark’s fairness utility. These results show that dynamically adjusting allocations based on the system state can substantially improve the balance between operating costs and allocation fairness.
Our additional experiments examine how the proposed policy responds to changes in the operating environment. We find that increasing the weight placed on fairness yields higher fairness utility at the expense of somewhat higher operating costs. Higher referral costs shift inventory toward CHAs, reducing local unmet demand and supply–demand mismatches. Greater demand variability makes demand more difficult to meet but also increases our policy’s performance over the fairness-only benchmark. Higher spatial demand correlation reduces mismatches across locations, while changes in HF demand and initial inventory prompt the policy to adjust the balance of shipments between the HF and CHAs. Together, these experiments show that the proposed policy responds systematically to changes in costs, demand, and resource availability.
Overall, our results suggest that dynamic allocation can improve the use of scarce medication inventory by accounting for current inventory levels, uncertain demand, referral costs, and fairness considerations rather than relying solely on fixed allocation shares. Although grounded in the Liberian context, our model and computational approach also provide a framework for studying similar last-mile allocation problems in which limited resources must be distributed dynamically across multiple service locations.

Literature Review
At its core, the modeling approach in this paper addresses a fundamental question: how should a health facility and its community health assistants (CHAs) share a limited medication supply? Inherently, this is a problem of inventory rationing. The tension in our model is captured, in part, through competing costs: keeping stock centrally located increases flexibility, whereas dispersing it among CHAs brings treatment closer to patients. This dynamic is common in distribution systems, and we draw on existing literature to understand the tradeoffs between these inventory placement strategies. Given the global health context of our setting, we also incorporate fair allocation measures to consider when constructing and evaluating policies. Finally, our modeling structure and proposed solutions rely on stochastic control techniques. Broadly, we connect this allocation problem to literature on inventory rationing, distribution systems, fairness, global health logistics, and stochastic control.
Inventory rationing models help determine when to reserves scarce stock for demand classes with different shortage costs or service requirements.
Veinott (1965)
studies nonstationary inventory with several demand classes, and
Topkis (1968)
derives class- and time-dependent thresholds, under his model’s assumptions, for deciding when to serve demand or preserve inventory.
Nahmias and Demmy (1981)
analyze rationing under periodic and continuous review. These foundational models show how the value of reserving stock depends on demand priorities and replenishment opportunities;
Romero-Silva et al. (2026)
review the broader literature.
Later studies clarify how shortage assumptions shape allocation policies.
Deshpande et al. (2003)
coordinate replenishment and threshold rationing for two demand classes with backorders.
Arslan et al. (2007)
evaluate multiclass rationing through an equivalence with serial inventory systems under a specified threshold policy and backorder-clearing rule.
Ha (1997)
establishes structural results for production and rationing with multiple demand classes and lost sales. In a health application,
Samii et al. (2012)
study vaccine inventory rationing.
Our model also starts with a fixed supply, but links shortages across treatment locations through patient referrals. Some patients encountering a CHA stockout reach the facility; others remain untreated, and referred patients still depend on the facility having medication. Rationing therefore affects both immediate access and demand at the facility.
Our model can be seen as a one-warehouse, multiple-retailer (OWMR) model. OWMR models study how shared supply should be distributed across locations.
Eppen (1979)
quantifies pooling benefits under normal demands and common linear costs, highlighting the role of demand correlation.
Eppen and Schrage (1981)
analyze centralized ordering through a stockless depot under an allocation-balance assumption.
Federgruen and Zipkin (1984a)
;
Federgruen and Zipkin (1984b)
develop allocation policies and systematic approximations for dynamic multilocation systems.
Jackson (1988)
is particularly relevant because a warehouse allocates a shipment over a cycle before its next outside replenishment. Retaining stock allows later allocations to respond to additional demand information.
Jackson and Muckstadt (1989)
examine this risk-pooling benefit in a two-period model. Our setting shares this reserve-allocation problem, with treatment access and referral costs determining the consequences of inventory placement.
Central backup service also has precedents.
Anupindi et al. (2001)
allow some unmet retailer demand to be served from central or other retailer inventories, while
Axsäter et al. (2007)
consider direct customer demand at the warehouse. Our model distinguishes deployable warehouse reserve from facility treatment stock, even when they are co-located. The reserve can supply any treatment location; facility stock serves local patients and referrals. This distinction connects the flexibility of central reserves to the value of local treatment under seasonal demand.
Our objective combines stockout-related costs with a demand-weighted alpha-fair utility of allocation rates relative to expected local demand rates. This utility rewards both the amount allocated and its distribution. Fairness objectives depend on what is being distributed and how recipients are compared.
Mo and Walrand (2000)
develop the alpha-fair family for network resource allocation, and
Bertsimas et al. (2012)
characterize its efficiency–fairness trade-offs.
Karsu and Morton (2015)
review alternative formulations. In related applications,
McCoy and Lee (2014)
study fairness in allocating health-delivery transport capacity.
Integrated community case management extends treatment of childhood malaria, pneumonia, and diarrhea to communities with limited facility access
(
Young et al., 2012
)
. A controlled before-and-after evaluation of a precursor program in Rivercess, Liberia, found increased treatment by qualified providers
(
White et al., 2018
)
. Reliable supplies are essential to this delivery model.
Chandani et al. (2012)
examine medicine availability among community health workers, and
Yadav (2015)
identifies broader weaknesses in health-product supply chains.
While the traditional cost of centralized inventory is increased delivery response time, modern literature also views decentralization as a risk mitigation tool.
Schmitt et al. (2015)
measured the benefits of decentralized inventory in reducing cost variance and lowering the probability of a stockout during a warehouse delay or failure. Additionally, with the growth of e-commerce, the OWMR framework has expanded to include warehouses as sources of direct order fulfillment, paralleling the direct-to-patient dynamics in our model. In our specific context, the response time cost of centralization translates directly to patient burden. Patients unable to receive treatment at decentralized CHAs incur the cost of traveling to a centralized facility. Consequently, some of them will not receive treatment. We rely on literature measuring the cost of failed referrals in community health settings, such as
Morris et al. (2023)
and
Rasanathan et al. (2014a)
;
Rasanathan et al. (2014b)
. In general, the limited studies on community-based referrals in sub-Saharan Africa indicate that pregnancy and newborn-related referrals have a much higher realized referral rate than iCCM conditions, with compliance for the latter falling below 10% in some settings
(
Lal et al., 2018
)
. In contrast,
Jarolimova et al. (2018)
find substantially higher follow-through in another Ugandan program.
Nanyonjo et al. (2015)
document referral-related expenses and caregivers’ opportunity costs. These findings motivate including patient movement and access costs in inventory decisions, while recognizing that referral behavior varies across settings.
Several operations studies address closely related distribution problems.
Vledder et al. (2019)
evaluate a large supply-chain field experiment in Zambia.
Gallien et al. (2021)
use a field-informed, validated simulation to compare pharmaceutical inventory policies and examine availability and access equity.
Parvin et al. (2018)
provide a particularly close precedent: their Malawi model combines delayed shipments, clinic clustering, and dynamic transshipments under seasonal demand, with equity considerations. Our focus is on stockouts that move patients toward the supervising facility. Such referrals couple local availability with facility demand and patients’ travel burdens.
We first formulate a discrete-time stochastic dynamic optimization model for inventory allocation, following the existing literature. However, this model is computationally intractable due to the curse of dimensionality. We then use a moment-matched Gaussian approximation of the demand process and adopt a continuous-time formulation. This yields a controlled reflected Brownian motion (RBM) model coupled with an exhaustible warehouse reserve. The reflection process tracks unmet demand and generates referrals to the facility when CHAs run out of stock.
Harrison and Reiman (1981)
establish foundational results for RBM, and
Harrison (1988)
develops Brownian network models for dynamic control. Applications include make-to-stock scheduling
(
Wein, 1992
)
, joint capacity and inventory decisions
(
Bradley and Glynn, 2002
)
, and volunteer staffing in nonprofit gleaning operations
(
Ata et al., 2019
)
. Our construction in this paper is a demand-based approximation rather than a heavy-traffic limit theorem.
Ata et al. (2025)
provide a closely related computational method for drift control of RBM under infinite-horizon objectives.
We use deep neural-network based method to solve our dynamic allocation problem. Deep neural network methods approximate value functions and their marginal values from simulated paths, avoiding a full grid over inventory states. The deep backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE) methods of
E et al. (2017)
and
Han et al. (2018)
train neural networks for the value function and its gradient. They propagate approximate values along paths and train against a known terminal condition, coupling parameters across time.
Beck et al. (2021)
develop deep splitting, which replaces joint training across all time steps with successive smaller regression problems. Our adaptation works backward from the terminal condition. At each step, the next-time value approximation is fixed while current values and allocation-relevant marginal values are fitted jointly. Training smaller problems eases computation.
We adopt the deep splitting to drift control of RBM. Our adaptation accounts for referral flows, stockout costs, and the warehouse resource constraint. It learns the marginal effect of sending another unit to a CHA instead of retaining it centrally. We apply this method to solve the inventory allocation problem for the Lofa County. That corresponds to a nine dimensional control problem. While this is remarkable by historical standards, we view it only as a proof-of-concept, and expect that our method can handle problems of much higher dimensions, e.g., in hundreds if not thousands of dimensions; see
Beck et al. (2021)
and
Ata and Kaşıkaralar (2026)
.
Our contribution combines dynamic medication rationing, stockout-induced referrals, unequal access costs, and an explicit preference for equitable allocation under limited supply. Adapting deep splitting to this problem allows us to examine how central reserve retention and local treatment access should be balanced over time.

Data, Test Problems and Benchmark Policies
In this section, we calibrate our model using data from the Fangonda region of Lofa County, Liberia. As is typical of data collected in community health contexts, the dataset used for these purposes needs to be supplemented with secondary data sources. Using the information available, we attempt to capture the cost tradeoffs in the Fangonda and similar settings, while acknowledging its limitations. Furthermore, the model is intended to allow for insights that are broadly applicable to other contexts.
In what follows, we first describe the Fangonda data and explain how they inform the selected model parameters for our numerical experiments. We then define the test problem, introduce the benchmark policies used for comparison, and specify additional experiments.

Conclusion
We study the dynamic allocation of scarce medications between a health facility and the Community Health Assistants it supervises in Liberia’s community health program. The allocation problem requires balancing the flexibility of retaining inventory centrally against the benefit of placing medications closer to patients, while also accounting for geographical fairness considerations. We formulate this problem as a finite-horizon, two-echelon stochastic control problem that captures uncertain demand, referrals, and the costs associated with untreated patients and referrals.
To solve this problem, we develop a continuous-time approximation based on reflected Brownian motion and characterize the allocation decision through a high-dimensional HJB equation. We then adapt the deep splitting method to approximate the value function and the marginal values that determine the allocation policy. This approach allows us to solve the resulting high-dimensional dynamic allocation problem without discretizing the full state space.
We calibrate the model using data from the Fangonda region of Lofa County, Liberia, and evaluate the proposed policy in the original discrete-review system. Our policy outperforms benchmark policies considered in the paper. Relative to the fairness-only policy, the strongest benchmark, our policy reduces operating costs by about 6% while retaining approximately 98% of its fairness utility. These results show that dynamically adjusting allocations based on the system state can improve the balance between operating costs and allocation fairness significantly.
Our additional experiments show how the proposed policy responds to changes in the operating environment. Increasing the weight placed on fairness moves the policy toward higher fairness utility at the cost of somewhat higher operating costs. Higher referral costs shift inventory toward CHAs and reduce local unmet demand and supply–demand mismatches. Greater demand variability makes the system more difficult to serve, but also increases the improvement of our policy over the fairness-only benchmark. Higher spatial correlation reduces mismatches across locations, while changes in HF demand and initial inventory lead the policy to adjust the balance of shipments between the HF and CHAs. Together, these experiments show that the proposed policy responds systematically to changes in costs, demand, and resource availability.
Overall, our results suggest that dynamic allocation can improve the use of scarce medication inventory by accounting for current inventory states, uncertain demand, referral costs, and fairness considerations. More broadly, the model and computational approach provide a way to study similar last-mile allocation problems in which limited resources must be distributed dynamically across multiple service locations.
