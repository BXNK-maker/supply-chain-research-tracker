Abstract
This paper introduces the Location-Routing Problem with Delivery Preferences,
a last-mile delivery problem with service flexibility that integrates collection-point activation and routing decisions while accounting for customer choice. The company determines which collection points to activate, which customers to offer home delivery, and the corresponding delivery route. Customers subsequently decide whether to accept the proposed home-delivery service or collect their parcels from an activated collection point according to ranked preferences over delivery time intervals and collection points.
This hierarchical interaction is modeled through a bilevel optimization framework, which can be formulated as a mixed-integer bilevel program that incorporates compensation mechanisms for service inconvenience. Two equivalent single-level reformulations that exploit the structure of the customers’ problems are developed. In addition, a two-phase heuristic is proposed to generate feasible solutions that can be used as warm starts for the formulations. The computational experiments run to compare the performance of the formulations, show that the warm-start strategy becomes particularly effective as the instance size increases.
The comparison with a benchmark model that ignores customer preferences indicates that explicitly accounting for customer choice alters the resulting location-routing configuration while improving customer preference satisfaction at a small increase in the company’s overall cost. These findings highlight the importance of jointly considering operational efficiency, service flexibility, and customer autonomy in last-mile delivery planning.

Introduction
The rapid growth of e-commerce has profoundly reshaped urban freight distribution, placing significant pressure on last-mile logistics systems. Increasing order volumes, tighter delivery expectations, and greater consideration of customer opinions regarding service quality have made distribution planning considerably more challenging
[
24
]
. Failed home deliveries, traffic congestion, and rising operational costs further complicate last-mile logistics processes. In response, companies are progressively incorporating alternative delivery options, such as parcel collection points, to enhance flexibility and reduce costly redelivery attempts
[
20
,
22
]
. Integrating these alternatives into routing decisions is therefore of significant practical and strategic importance.
At the same time, customers have become active participants in the delivery process. Unlike in the past, they now express explicit preferences regarding delivery time windows and pickup locations
[
19
]
. The flexibility associated with these preferences can have important operational implications. For instance,
Yildiz and Savelsbergh [27]
show that additional delivery-time flexibility can generate substantial savings in distribution costs. As noted by
[
8
]
, ignoring customer behavior can lead to inefficient routing plans, service dissatisfaction, and reputational damage. Consequently, modern last-mile systems must be designed not only from an operational efficiency perspective but also with explicit consideration of customer-driven service constraints.
Motivated by these challenges, this paper introduces the Location-Routing Problem with Delivery Preferences (LRP-DP). The LRP-DP is
a business-to-customer delivery problem in which an e-commerce company operates a central depot and may activate a set of collection points. The depot serves exclusively as the origin and destination of delivery routes, whereas collection points act as intermediate facilities where customers can retrieve their parcels as an alternative to home delivery. The service route visits all activated collection points and a subset of customers who are offered home delivery. Upon purchasing a product, each customer specifies an ideal time window for home delivery. Consequently, the company must design routes that account for these preferred service times and determine which customers will be offered home delivery.
Customers respond to the company’s decisions according to their preferences as follows. Customers who are offered home delivery may accept home service even if the proposed service time differs from their ideal time. Alternatively, they may prefer to pick up their parcels at an activated collection point. On the other hand, customers who are not offered home delivery select their most preferred activated collection point for parcel pickup.
When home delivery does not take place at the customer’s ideal service time, the company incurs compensation payments reflecting its estimate of the resulting service deterioration. These compensations are determined based on the customer information available to the company. For customers ultimately receiving home delivery, compensation depends on the deviation from their ideal service time. For customers served through a collection point, compensation is based on the distance to the nearest activated collection point, regardless of the collection point ultimately selected according to their preferences. This distinction separates the compensation attributable to the network designed by the company from any additional travel resulting from the customer’s individual choice, which is independent of the service quality offered by the company. Therefore, customer reactions are incorporated into the company’s routing and collection-point activation decisions to effectively design the last-mile logistics network.
The company aims to minimize total costs, including collection-point activation costs, routing costs, and customer compensation payments associated with deviations from ideal service times or pickup at collection points. The hierarchical nature of the interaction between the company and its customers makes a bilevel optimization framework a natural modeling approach for the LRP-DP. In summary, the LRP-DP integrates routing and facility location decisions while accounting for customer preferences and compensation mechanisms.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
•
We introduce the LRP-DP, a bilevel location-routing problem that integrates collection-point activation, routing, customer choice for service mode, and compensation mechanisms for service inconvenience.
•
We exploit the structure of the customers’ problems to derive two equivalent single-level MILP reformulations. The first one is obtained from primal/dual optimality conditions, whereas the second one uses primal variables only and incorporates a system of inequalities that captures the customers preferences.
•
We develop a two-phase MIP heuristic and a warm-start strategy to improve the solution of larger instances.
•
We conduct extensive computational experiments and quantify the impact of explicitly incorporating customer preferences.
•
A comparison of the LRP-DP with a benchmark model that ignores customers’ preferences indicates that explicitly accounting for customer choice alters the resulting location-routing configuration while improving customer preference satisfaction, with only a small increase in the company’s overall cost.

Literature Review
Last-mile delivery systems increasingly combine home delivery with out-of-home alternatives, such as parcel lockers and collection-and-delivery points (CDPs). These alternatives enable the consolidation of multiple deliveries at common locations, potentially reducing the routing effort associated with traditional home delivery. Consequently, a growing body of research has investigated how home delivery and pickup options can be jointly incorporated into last-mile distribution planning.
Several routing problems have incorporated this combination of home delivery and alternative pickup locations. For example,
Grabenschweiger et al. [14]
study a vehicle routing problem with heterogeneous locker boxes in which customers can be served either at home within a time window or through a locker station. Similarly,
Tilk et al. [25]
introduce the vehicle routing problem with delivery options, where customers may be associated with alternative delivery locations and time windows and the carrier selects the final delivery option while satisfying a prescribed service level regarding customer preferences.
Dumez et al. [12]
also consider multiple delivery options characterized by preference levels and time windows, while accounting for capacities at shared delivery locations.
Janinhoff et al. [16]
consider a multitrip setting combining home delivery, parcel lockers, and pickup stations, while
Dell’Amico et al. [10]
integrate parcel lockers into a pickup-and-delivery problem with time windows. These studies show how alternative delivery locations can provide additional flexibility to last-mile routing. However, the locations of the pickup facilities are generally treated as given rather than being jointly determined with the delivery routes.
While the previous studies assume that the locations of the alternative pickup facilities are given, other studies have integrated location into last-mile distribution planning. In this direction,
Janjevic et al. [17]
incorporate the location of collection-and-delivery points within the strategic design of an e-commerce distribution network, considering the interaction between facility placement and the resulting distribution operations. More closely related to a location-routing setting,
Grabenschweiger et al. [15]
consider a multi-period problem in which locker locations are selected while home-delivery, locker-assignment, and routing decisions are jointly determined. More recently,
Ozyavas et al. [22]
study a delivery network in which parcel lockers can serve both as customer collection points and as transfer points for the delivery company, jointly considering their location and the associated routing decisions. These studies highlight the importance of integrating the design of the pickup network with last-mile distribution decisions rather than considering facility location and vehicle routing separately.
Related problems have also been studied within two-echelon distribution systems. For instance,
Zhou et al. [28]
consider a multi-depot two-echelon vehicle routing problem with delivery options, in which goods are transported from depots to satellites and subsequently delivered to customers, while intermediate pickup facilities provide an alternative for parcel collection. Similarly,
Enthoven et al. [13]
integrate parcel lockers and cargo bikes into a two-echelon distribution system, where customers can either collect their parcels from covering locations or receive home delivery through vehicles operating from satellite facilities. Although these problems share some characteristics with the LRP-DP, their distribution structure is fundamentally different. In two-echelon systems, intermediate facilities primarily support the consolidation or transfer of goods between distribution stages. In the LRP-DP, collection points instead constitute alternative final service locations, and the same route can visit both activated collection points and customers offered home delivery.
Beyond the configuration of the delivery network, customer preferences constitute another important dimension of last-mile delivery. This is particularly relevant for attended home delivery, where customers may value delivery times differently and the attractiveness of home delivery therefore depends on when the service is provided.
Agatz et al. [1]
highlight the trade-off between operational efficiency and customer service when designing delivery time slots. More recently,
Amorim et al. [2]
, using empirical data from an online grocery retailer, show substantial heterogeneity in customer preferences for delivery-service attributes. In particular, they find that delivery speed, time-slot precision, and timing are important drivers of customers’ time-slot selections. These findings support the explicit consideration of time-related customer preferences in last-mile delivery decisions rather than treating feasible delivery times as equally attractive to customers.
From an optimization perspective, customer preferences have been incorporated directly into last-mile delivery models.
Tilk et al. [25]
allow customers to express preferences among alternative delivery locations and time windows; however, the carrier ultimately selects the delivery option while satisfying a prescribed service level regarding customer preferences. In attended home delivery,
Mackert et al. [21]
explicitly incorporate customer choice among delivery time slots, recognizing that the alternatives offered by the retailer influence the subsequent customer response. More recently,
Zhou et al. [29]
consider both home-delivery time slots and out-of-home locations under heterogeneous customer preferences. In their setting, the retailer determines which delivery alternatives and prices to offer, after which customers select among the available options according to a stochastic choice model. These studies represent an important shift from treating customer preferences only as service attributes toward explicitly accounting for how customers respond to the delivery alternatives offered by the logistics provider.
Customer preferences are often associated with compensation mechanisms offered to customers. For instance,
Grabenschweiger et al. [14]
and
Grabenschweiger et al. [15]
include compensation costs associated with serving customers through locker boxes, while
Dell’Amico et al. [10]
consider discounts granted to customers choosing self-service at lockers. Similarly,
Enthoven et al. [13]
incorporate customer-specific connection costs reflecting the attractiveness of alternative delivery modes. More recently,
Peng et al. [23]
explicitly consider financial incentives offered to customers to encourage delivery postponement or switching from home delivery to pickup alternatives. These approaches internalize customer-related service consequences in the objective function, although the associated costs are typically linked to the selected delivery mode, service adjustment, or incentive offered rather than to explicit ranked preferences over both delivery times and collection points.
When customers retain autonomy over their final service choice, the interaction between the service provider and the customers can naturally be represented as a hierarchical decision process. Bilevel optimization provides a suitable framework for such settings, as the provider first determines the alternatives made available and customers subsequently respond according to their own preferences. This perspective has been explored in the facility-location literature.
Camacho-Vallejo et al. [6]
consider an uncapacitated facility location problem in which the company determines which facilities to open, while customers are assigned according to their ordered preferences over the available facilities.
Casas-Ramírez et al. [7]
extend this setting to capacitated facilities, with customers seeking service from their most preferred facilities according to predetermined preference lists. Similarly,
Calvete et al. [4]
incorporate restrictions on the number of customers that can be allocated to each facility while preserving customer autonomy in selecting their most preferred open facility. These studies demonstrate how bilevel optimization can explicitly capture hierarchical interactions between a service provider and customers whose decisions are driven by ranked preferences.
Bilevel optimization has also been used to model hierarchical interactions in last-mile delivery.
Cerulli et al. [9]
consider a peer-to-peer logistics platform in which the platform assigns delivery requests to independent carriers, who subsequently decide which requests to accept according to their own profitability. More closely related to customer choice,
Peng et al. [23]
study a last-mile delivery system in which the operator offers financial incentives to encourage customers to accept delayed delivery or switch from home delivery to alternative pickup modes. Customers then decide whether to accept these offers, while the operator anticipates their responses when planning the delivery operations. Although the followers and their decision mechanisms differ across these applications, these studies illustrate the relevance of bilevel optimization when last-mile decisions depend on the autonomous responses of other actors.

The comparison in Table
1
shows that, although previous studies have addressed several components of the LRP-DP, important differences remain in how customer preferences, preference-related costs, and customer responses are integrated with operational decisions. Location-routing models typically retain control over the final delivery alternative, whereas models with endogenous customer choice generally assume a predetermined pickup infrastructure. Moreover, customer preferences are commonly represented through service levels, eligibility rules, stochastic utility, or incentive mechanisms, and preference-related costs are typically associated with compensation, discounts, or service adjustments rather than with explicit rankings over both home-delivery time intervals and collection points.
The LRP-DP bridges these perspectives by jointly considering collection-point location, routing and scheduling, and endogenous customer responses. Customers rank jointly home-delivery time intervals and candidate collection points, and select among the alternatives made available by the company accordingly. This interaction naturally leads to a hierarchical decision process in which the company anticipates customer choices when planning its operations.

In the LRP-DP the company must determine the set of collection points to activate, as well as the set of customers who may potentially receive home delivery. In addition, the company must design a service route that starts and ends at the depot and visits all activated collection points as well as all customers selected for home delivery.
Customers who are not selected for home delivery must collect their parcels from an activated collection point according to their preferences. Moreover, a customer may reject home delivery if the offered delivery time is less preferred than another available option. That is, if there exists an activated collection point at which the customer prefers to pick up the parcel rather than receive it at home at the proposed delivery time, the customer will choose that collection point instead. In such a case, the customer will select their most preferred activated collection point.
Customers who ultimately receive home delivery receive compensation based on the deviation of their delivery time from their ideal service time, whereas customers who receive service at collection points, receive compensation based on the closest activated collection point.
The decisions of the company are the following:
-
A set of collection points to be activated.
-
A set of customers selected to potentially receive home delivery.
-
A service route, starting and ending at the depot, that visits all selected customers as well as all activated collection points.
-
A home-delivery time for each selected customer, as determined by the service route.
Given a company’s solution, each customer must decide:
-
Whether to accept the proposed home-delivery time (if home delivery is offered).
-
Which activated collection point to use for parcel pickup when home delivery is rejected or not offered.
The objective function minimizes the following cost-related terms:
-
The setup cost of the activated collection points.
-
The total routing cost for the selected customers and the activated collection points.
-
The economic compensations paid to customers for the service they receive.
The LRP-DP consists of determining a company’s solution that minimizes the cost objective function described above while accounting for the customers’ optimal reactions based on their preferences.

A Mixed-Integer Programming Heuristic for the LRP-DP
The proposed Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP) heuristic follows a two-phase structure designed to construct high-quality feasible solutions that can be used as warm starts for the single-level MILP formulations proposed in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
. Phase 1 solves an approximation, consisting of a reduced location-routing problem considering only the depot and the candidate collection points. The objective of this phase is to identify a promising subset of collection points, together with initial customer-to-collection-point assignments and a routing structure connecting the selected collection points. In Phase 2, this partial initial solution is used as input to a parameterized, leader-oriented version of the MILP formulation, in which only the collection points selected in Phase 1 may be opened. In addition, customer rejection is not allowed; thus, each customer must be served through home delivery, if proposed by the leader. This auxiliary formulation reduces the search space and constructs a complete feasible solution that can subsequently be used as an incumbent for the original MILP formulation.

However, the model accounts for customers’ preferences over collection points.

6.1
Warm-Start Strategy for the MILP Formulation
As will be seen, the MIP heuristic described in the above section produces a feasible solution that, in general, is of good quality. Moreover, the heuristic solution can also be used as a warm-start solution to enhance the performance of the MILP formulation(s).
Two alternative strategies can be followed: (
i
i
) the solver runs for a limited (small) computing time and stops immediately after finding a feasible solution better than that of the warm start; or, (
i
​
i
ii
) the solver uses the heuristic solution to reduce the search space to those better than it, and runs until a provable optimal solution has been found or until a maximum (large) time limit has been reached.
In either case, when the heuristic is applied prior the MILP, the solution produced by the heuristic is provided to the solver as a warm-start solution, which is used as the initial incumbent solution. The solver then focuses the search on finding solutions better than the initial one and can either preserve or improve the initial solution.

7
Experimental Results
In this section, we present the results of the computational experiments conducted for the LRP-DP. First, we compare the computational performance of the SLR, MILP-I, and MILP-II formulations developed for the problem under study. Based on this comparison, we then use MILP-II to assess the computational tractability of the LRP-DP across instances of increasing size and to identify the limitations of the exact solution approach. Finally, we evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed MIP heuristic, with particular emphasis on large-size instances for which the exact formulation becomes computationally challenging. All experiments were conducted on a desktop computer equipped with an Intel® Core™ i7-11370H @ 3.30 GHz processor and 32 GB of RAM. The mathematical formulations and the MIP heuristic were implemented in Python 3.10 and solved using Gurobi 13.0. Before presenting the computational results, we first describe the benchmark instances used in the experiments.

8
Conclusions and Future Research
This paper introduced the LRP-DP, a last-mile location-routing problem that explicitly incorporates service flexibility by allowing customers to choose between home delivery and activated collection points according to their ranked preferences. The company determines which collection points to activate, which customers to offer home delivery, and the corresponding delivery route, while customers subsequently decide whether to accept the proposed home-delivery service or collect their parcels from an activated collection point. This hierarchical interaction was modeled through a bilevel optimization framework. The model also incorporates compensation mechanisms associated with service inconvenience. In particular, compensation for home delivery is based on the deviation of actual service times relative to the customers ideal service times, whereas compensation for collection-point service is based on the closest activated collection point rather than on the collection point ultimately selected by the customer, thereby separating the service conditions provided by the company from additional travel resulting from the customer’s individual preferences.
From a methodological perspective, we analyzed the structure of the followers’ problems and exploited it to derive equivalent single-level reformulations of the bilevel model. Two MILP reformulations were proposed, with MILP-II, which uses only primal variables, providing the best computational performance among the formulations considered. Nevertheless, the computational experiments also showed that the problem becomes increasingly challenging as the number of customers and candidate collection points grows. To address this limitation, we developed a two-phase MIP heuristic that constructs feasible solutions and uses them to warm-start MILP-II. The resulting strategy becomes particularly effective for the larger instances, improving both the final objective values and the optimality gaps within the same computational budget.
The computational analysis also highlights the operational relevance of explicitly considering customer choice. In particular, comparison with the high-point relaxation, in which the company controls the collection-point assignments, shows that accounting for customer preferences changes the resulting location-routing decisions and improves preference satisfaction. The bilevel solutions increase the percentage of customers receiving their first-ranked service alternative and reduce the average preference rank. The results show that the increase in the overall cost is moderate compared with the improvement in customer satisfaction. These results indicate that ignoring customer autonomy may lead to service configurations that appear attractive from the company’s perspective but are less aligned with the service that customers would actually choose. Thus, incorporating customer choice provides a more realistic representation of the interaction between operational planning and service preferences in last-mile delivery.
Several extensions provide opportunities for future research. First, the computational difficulty observed for larger instances motivates the development of more scalable exact and heuristic algorithms. A related direction is the incorporation of machine learning techniques to enhance the performance of the proposed optimization methods. Second, the models assume deterministic customer preference rankings. Considering uncertain, partially observed, or dynamically evolving preferences would provide a natural extension and could lead to stochastic or robust variants of the LRP-DP. Finally, the problem could be extended by incorporating additional features commonly considered in location-routing problems, such as capacitated collection points, heterogeneous delivery resources, and other operational constraints.
