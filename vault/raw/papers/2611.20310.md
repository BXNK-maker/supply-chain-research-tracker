Abstract
Arc-flow formulations can provide strong relaxations for mixed-integer program formulations of vehicle routing problems, but the associated networks may be prohibitively large. We study the setting in which multiple arc flow relaxations may be applied, and introduce a dual-guided aggregation and refinement framework that constructs substantially smaller arc-flow formulations while recovering the LP bound of the original networks. Dual information is used both to identify states that can be aggregated without weakening the bound and to guide node disaggregation. We show that the resulting procedure converges finitely to the arc-flow bound of the full arc-flow formulation. The framework retains the original decision variables, allowing cutting planes to be added directly in the original variable space. Moreover, it produces an explicit MILP formulation without relying on column generation or pricing to determine the arc-flow relaxation bound. We apply our approach to the vehicle routing problem with time windows for which we define two arc flow relaxations, as well as two families of cutting planes over the original variable space. Computational experiments show that strong bounds can be obtained using substantially aggregated networks. The resulting root bounds are competitive with those obtained by the branch-and-price solver RouteOpt, while retaining a formulation that can be solved directly by a general-purpose MILP solver.

Introduction
Strong discrete relaxations of mixed-integer linear programs (MILPs) for vehicle routing problems can be obtained from state-transition networks arising from dynamic programming. The associated arc-flow formulations provide extended formulations of the convex hulls of these relaxations and can substantially strengthen the original compact formulation. Their practical use, however, may be limited by the size of the underlying networks. A detailed state description produces a strong formulation, but may require a prohibitively large number of nodes and arcs.
One approach to this difficulty is to avoid representing the full discrete solution space explicitly, and apply a Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation that is solved by column generation. In this case, the pricing problem to generate new variables can be based on an implicit labeling algorithm over the dynamic program
(
Dantzig and Wolfe 1960
)
. For many problems, in particular vehicle routing applications, the resulting dual bounds are often substantially tighter than the linear programming relaxation based on the standard compact formulation
(
Desrochers et al. 1992
)
. For solving the original MILP, however, one usually resorts to branch-and-price
(
Barnhart et al. 1998
)
, or branch-cut-and-price
(
Gschwind et al. 2019
)
. These methods provide powerful solution frameworks, with specialized components for pricing, branching, and cutting planes. For instance, branching rules must remain compatible with pricing, often requiring specialized schemes such as successor-based branching
(
Ryan and Foster 1981
)
; and incorporating strong inequalities can be difficult when they do not admit a clean representation in the pricing subproblem
(
Jepsen et al. 2008
,
Archetti et al. 2011
)
.
On the other hand, explicit arc-flow formulations can be added directly to the compact MILP formulation together with constraints that link the arc-flow variables to the original variables. This can work well for suitably small networks, e.g., as the state-transition graph of a dynamic program
(
Eppen and Martin 1987
,
Martin et al. 1990
)
, multi-valued decision diagrams
(
Cire et al. 2019
)
, and relaxed decision diagrams
(
Bergman and Cire 2018
,
van Hoeve 2022
)
. For many large-scale problems, however, an explicit embedding of arc-flow models is not practical due to their large size.
In this work, we introduce Dual-Guided Aggregation and Refinement (DGAR), an iterative aggregation and refinement procedure that yields an arc-flow formulation of small size while recovering the relaxation bound of the original arc-flow formulation.
Our contributions are as follows. First, we establish a dual condition for bound-preserving aggregation of arc-flow formulations. Second, we develop a complementary dual-guided refinement procedure that disaggregates nodes to strengthen the formulation. Third, we combine these operations into an iterative procedure and prove finite convergence to the bound defined by the original arc-flow representation. Lastly, we evaluate our approach on the vehicle routing problem with time windows, combining two discrete route relaxations with cutting planes in the original routing variables and reduced-cost arc removal. The resulting explicit formulation can be passed directly to a general-purpose MILP solver. Our computational study examines bound quality, the contribution of cutting planes, root-node effort, and formulation size, followed by the use of the strengthened formulation for integer optimization.

Related Work
Our work is most closely related to three approaches for obtaining strong formulations:
Dantzig-Wolfe reformulations and column generation, arc-flow formulations,
and methods based on state aggregation and iterative refinement.
Dantzig-Wolfe reformulations strengthen the linear programming relaxation of an MILP by replacing the relaxation associated with a subset of its constraints by the convex hull of the corresponding discrete feasible points
(
Dantzig and Wolfe 1960
)
. The resulting formulation can provide a substantially stronger bound, but generally requires exponentially many variables. Column generation avoids enumerating these variables by maintaining a restricted master problem and generating improving variables, or columns, through a pricing problem. When the discrete set admits a dynamic programming representation, the pricing problem can be solved over its state-transition network, with each source-sink path representing a discrete solution
(
Gilmore and Gomory 1961
,
Desrochers et al. 1992
)
. For integer optimization, column generation is typically embedded within branch-and-price, and often branch-cut-and-price
(
Barnhart et al. 1998
)
. This requires branching rules compatible with pricing and may require specialized procedures for stabilization, cutting planes, and primal heuristics. This often leads to increasingly sophisticated computational frameworks, e.g., for solving vehicle routing applications
(
Pessoa et al. 2020
)
. See
(
Desrosiers et al. 2026
)
for a comprehensive treatment of branch-and-price.
Arc-flow formulations provide an alternative representation of discrete sets in which solutions are represented by source-to-sink paths in a directed network. A particularly important construction arises from dynamic programming, where the state-transition graph of a dynamic program defines the network
(
Wolsey 1973
,
Martin et al. 1990
)
. Arc-flow representations can also be obtained from other graphical representations, including decision diagrams
(
Bergman and Cire 2018
,
van Hoeve 2022
)
, or through problem-specific network constructions; see
de Lima et al. (2022)
for a survey.
Arc-flow and Dantzig-Wolfe reformulations are closely related and provide the same relaxation bound when defined over the same discrete set
(
Yamín et al. 2026
)
. Arc-flow formulations replace the potentially exponential number of path variables by an explicit network, making it possible in some cases to solve the resulting formulation directly with a general-purpose MILP solver. Their effectiveness, however, depends on the size of the network, and detailed dynamic programming representations can themselves produce prohibitively large networks.
This trade-off has motivated several approaches for reducing the computational burden of arc-flow formulations. One direction is to use decomposition methods to solve the arc-flow MILPs.
Hosseininasab and van Hoeve (2021)
apply a Benders decomposition to solve a combined arc-flow formulation for the multiple sequence alignment problem as an MILP.
Kowalczyk et al. (2024)
use branch-and-price to solve an arc-flow model based on a decision diagrams representation for parallel machine scheduling problems. Likewise,
de Lima et al. (2023)
develop a computational framework for solving arc-flow models that combines column-and-row generation, reduced-cost variable fixing, and a specialized branching scheme. Our approach instead aims to minimize the size of the arc-flow formulation and leverage off-the-shelf MILP solvers.
Many works have applied state aggregations in some form to solve smaller-sized problems more efficiently. In the context of dynamic programming,
Bean et al. (1987)
develop a state aggregation and disaggregation procedure find solutions with a guaranteed optimality error bound. For pricing problems in the context of column generation for vehicle routing problems, state-space relaxations have been instrumental in providing strong bounds efficiently
(
Christofides et al. 1981
,
Baldacci et al. 2011
)
; these relaxations define aggregate states implicitly by re-defining the dynamic program. Relaxed decision diagrams, on the other hand, apply a node merging function that aggregates states and which yield dual bounds for discrete optimization problems
(
Bergman et al. 2016
)
. In a related network-reduction setting,
Bergman et al. (2022)
construct dynamic programming networks for multiobjective discrete optimization and develop node-merging and arc-removal operations that reduce these networks while preserving the Pareto frontier.
Lastly, iterative refinement has been used for constructing network representations of smaller size than the exact state-space.
Decremental state-space relaxations improve column generation by incrementally strengthening a relaxed pricing problem
(
Righini and Salani 2008
)
.
Dynamic discretization discovery iteratively refines a state-space network by discretizing state values such as time
(
Boland et al. 2017
)
.
Clautiaux et al. (2017)
present an iterative aggregation and disaggregation method to solve arc-flow formulations by strengthening an initial relaxed arc-flow formulation.
Lastly, column elimination iteratively removes infeasible paths from a decision diagram and more generally computes locally refined dynamic programming state-transition graphs as arc-flow formulations
(
van Hoeve 2022
,
Karahalios and van Hoeve 2026
)
.

10
Computational Experiments
The computational study examines whether dual-guided aggregation and
refinement makes strong arc-flow formulations practical. We first evaluate
the quality of the root bounds and the effort required to obtain them,
including the contribution of cuts. We then examine the size of the
formulations constructed by the procedure. Finally, we test their use for
integer optimization through branch and bound. We refer to the implementation
as Dual-Guided Aggregation and Refinement (DGAR).

11
Conclusion
We developed a general methodology for reducing the size of arc-flow
formulations while retaining the strength of their LP relaxations. Starting
from a family of arc-flow representations of discrete relaxations, we showed
that nodes with equal flow-balance dual multipliers can be aggregated without
changing the LP bound. When an aggregation is too coarse, a restricted LP on
the original networks guides node disaggregation to strengthen
the formulation. Combining these operations yielded an iterative procedure
that converges finitely to the LP bound defined by the original
arc-flow representations.
The framework allows strong arc-flow relaxations to be represented by
smaller explicit formulations. By retaining the original routing variables,
problem-specific cutting planes can be incorporated directly, while
reduced-cost information can be used to remove arcs that cannot participate
in an improving integer solution. The resulting formulation combines these
strengthening techniques in a model that can be solved by a general-purpose
MILP solver.
We applied the methodology to the VRPTW using two arc-flow relaxations, one based on vehicle capacity and the other combining time windows with relaxed elementarity. We also developed and integrated two families of cutting planes to further strengthen the formulation. The computational results demonstrate that strong LP bounds can be obtained using substantially aggregated networks, yielding formulations that are orders of magnitude smaller than the original arc-flow formulations. The resulting root bounds were competitive with those obtained by the branch-and-price and branch-cut-and-price methods considered in our experiments. Moreover, these bounds were obtained without column generation or pricing, while retaining an explicit formulation that can be solved directly by a general-purpose MILP solver for integer optimization.

Restricted-Action Warm Start for the LP
This procedure concerns the solution of an LP at a fixed aggregation;
it is separate from the restricted LP used to compute refinement potentials.
Its seed consists of routing edges that carried flow in earlier iterations,
all source- and terminal-incident routing edges, and the five cheapest
outgoing edges of each customer. First solve with all other routing
variables fixed to zero. Then release these temporary restrictions and
solve the full LP, using the first basis as a warm start for primal simplex.
Only the second optimum is used as the lower bound. If nonnegativity of
x

is supplied by the arc-flow coupling, an upper bound of zero suffices
to fix a routing variable to zero. Each such fixing also sets every
associated arc-flow variable to zero in every network.
The final unrestricted solve is essential: the restricted optimum alone
is an upper bound on the full LP optimum, not the lower-bound certificate
required by the algorithm. No pricing-based stopping certificate is used
by this procedure. In the reported implementation it was enabled for the

D.1
Coverage and Run Selection
The main 32-thread DGAR, compact, and RouteOpt root comparisons contain all
168 instances. DGAR uses the
pass2
configuration from source revision
cc89eaa
. All 168 root procedures terminate with the recorded reason
no_progress
; none stops at the root time limit. This implementation
stopping condition does not independently establish equality with the fully
disaggregated LP bound, which was not computed for these instances. Bound comparisons use a
tolerance of 0.05 objective units to accommodate RouteOpt’s one-decimal
output. Negative gaps within numerical tolerance are displayed as zero.
All calculations use unrounded values; continuous results are reported to
two decimal places.
BP-NG5 has 131 valid completed roots and BP-NG9 has 130. Their common valid
set contains 128 instances. BP-NG5 has 37 unfinished roots; BP-NG9 has 35
unfinished roots and three completed objectives above the reference value
(RC207_50, R208_100, and RC208_100). These observations are excluded from
successful-root comparisons and listed in the accompanying per-instance
files. A final MIP bound is never substituted for a missing root bound.
The BP-NG runs used the same workstation as the other methods. Their solver
thread setting is listed as the Gurobi default in the treatment index and is
not reported in the per-instance outputs. They are used for the bound
and integer comparisons; the matched 32-thread root-time comparison includes
DGAR, compact, and RouteOpt.
The cut ablation uses the matched one-thread arms A and C from revision
f74e8a2
. Their non-cut settings agree, while the four switches
controlling capacity and local-area separation are disabled in C. The
one-thread configuration also differs from the main 32-thread configuration
in LP restriction and LP algorithm settings; it is not a thread-only scaling
experiment. Two uncut runs reach their iteration limit and are excluded from
the 166 completed pairs: RC204_50 has a partial uncut gap of 8.14% and
RC208_50 a gap of 13.96%. Their completed cut-enabled one-thread gaps are
4.30% and 9.24%, respectively. Both instances remain in the complete
32-thread comparison.
The one-thread cut records contain 19 small downward lower-bound changes,
with maximum magnitude 0.009 objective units. These are within the stated
0.05-unit comparison tolerance; the figures and data retain the original
values rather than enforcing monotonicity.
D.2
Timing and Model-Size Definitions
DGAR root wall time is the recorded total program time less the final-MILP
stage timer. The latter includes the final model construction and solve.
The root measure thus includes initial setup, input graph construction,
and the compression loop. Main/projector LP-call wall time is the sum of
lower_bound_lp_solve_wall
and
projector_lp_solve_wall
. The local-area category combines the
LA-NG and three-customer separation timers, counting the aliased timer only
once. Other root work is the exact residual. Solver runtime counters are
reported separately and do not capture every separator solve; overlapping
stage timers are never added together.
