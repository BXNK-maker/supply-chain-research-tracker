Abstract
We present a complete, GPU-resident pipeline for multi-robot 3D inspection of
underwater structures. Given a triangle mesh and a fleet of
K
K
robots, each
carrying a short-range, limited field-of-view camera, the pipeline computes
6-DOF trajectories that jointly cover a target fraction of the surface while
minimizing a linearly interpolated makespan/total-cost objective. The joint
problem is NP-hard, so we decompose it into five core stages — candidate
viewpoint generation, visibility computation, set cover, min-max vehicle routing
solved as a mixed integer program (MIP) with lifted Miller–Tucker–Zemlin
subtour elimination, and priority-based multi-agent path finding with
continuous-time inter-robot conflict checking — flanked by mesh loading and
surface sampling as preprocessing and by trajectory smoothing as
postprocessing. Our contributions are: an end-to-end GPU-accelerated
integration of the five stages, with its kernel design documented; a GPU batch
adaptation of Lien’s
ε
\varepsilon
-visibility with four custom CUDA kernels; a
β
\beta
-aware per-vehicle tour upper bound derived from
the interpolated objective, supplementing the Desrochers–Laporte coefficient
lift with forbidden-pair cuts; a 4D space-time extension of Zhou and Zeng’s GPU
parallel-frontier A*; and an experimental evaluation on 14 meshes (up to
7.2
7.2
M faces) that ablates the routing cuts and the parallel-frontier search,
audits collision freedom and waypoint attainment
after

smoothing, and measures the end-to-end gap against analytical and LP-relaxation

publicly available.

Introduction
Autonomously inspecting the surface of a complex three-dimensional structure
underwater is important across engineering and science: commercial shipping
relies on regular hull inspection for biofouling and corrosion; offshore
wind-farm operators need periodic condition assessment of monopile foundations;
harbor and dam operators inspect submerged walls; and underwater archaeology and
marine biology require dense photographic surveys of wrecks and coral
reefs
McMahon et al. (2023)
;
Carreno et al. (2020)
;
Thompson and Guihen (2019)
. These tasks share a
common structure: a fixed surface must be visually covered, and each surface
region has to be seen from within a limited range by a camera with a finite
field of view. Single-robot solutions scale poorly; a fleet of small autonomous
underwater vehicles (AUVs) can cover a complex surface faster and can cover for
each other if one vehicle fails mid-mission.
The underwater setting motivates two design choices. Perception range is short,
so the planner must bring viewpoints close to the surface (our camera far plane
defaults to
6
​
m
6\,\text{m}
). And because in-mission re-tasking and high-bandwidth
communication are limited, careful
pre-mission planning
is the dominant
lever for mission success, so we target offline planning.
Scope and assumptions.
We address the
offline planning layer
in isolation, under a deliberately
idealized model of everything around it: a geometric prior exists as a mesh
(from a bathymetric or photogrammetric survey), localization, map registration
and execution are exact, and visibility is a deterministic function of geometry.
Currents, acoustic bandwidth, energy, sensor noise and turbidity are not
modelled — they inform our defaults (short far plane, low cruise speed,
generous margins) but do not enter the
optimization. The evaluation is therefore deterministic simulation, and every
reported quantity is a property of the
plan
, not of a deployment. These
assumptions make the joint problem separable and let us isolate each stage’s
contribution; Section
6

violate first.

and multi-agent path finding — so the framework is a

Inspection planning in robotics.
The robotics literature attacks the same task from the opposite end, coupling

Limitations.
The formulation is open-loop and geometry-only, so the assumptions of

bound what these results mean; three would break first

(i) Localization and map error:

(ii) Disturbances:

(iii) Vehicle class:
we assume untethered AUVs, whereas for the tethered
ROVs common in practice tether length, snag avoidance and operator workflow
would dominate and reshape the routing formulation. Beyond modelling, the
decomposition costs global optimality, priority-based MAPF is incomplete and
would need a CBS variant for denser fleets, and most stages are slow or
infeasible on CPU-only hardware.
We also compare only against lower bounds and variants of our own pipeline, not
an external planner. Published structural-inspection planners
(e.g.

next-best-view objectives rather than a min-max fleet objective, and are
evaluated on their own vehicle and sensor models, so a head-to-head number would
mostly measure our reimplementation of them. Lower bounds avoid that but only
certify a factor from optimal; showing we advance the state of the art would
need a shared multi-robot 3D-inspection benchmark with a common sensor model and
objective, which does not yet exist.
Future work.
A location-routing formulation coupling viewpoint selection with travel cost; a
CBS-based MAPF for dense fleets; a longer-horizon or decomposition-based VRP
solve, since our routing results are budget- rather than formulation-bound;
an active-perception layer and currents-aware planning toward deployment; and
differentiable visibility for gradient-based pose refinement.
