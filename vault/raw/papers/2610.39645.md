Abstract
Electric vehicles produce no tailpipe emissions, but charging them produces emissions that depend on the time-varying generation mix of the electricity consumed. We study an Electric Vehicle Routing Problem with a carbon-aware objective that accounts for charging-related emissions alongside conventional routing costs. The problem determines routing, charging, and waiting decisions, allowing charging demand to be shifted toward periods with lower grid carbon intensity. Because these decisions depend on forecasts of future carbon intensity, we propose a decision-focused learning approach so that the training of the model employed for predicting the carbon intensity of the grid over time is solver-aware. In particular, we train a linear predictor using approximate
SPO
+ directions and employ a time-limited Large Neighborhood Search followed by exact postprocessing as the underlying solver. Experiments on realistic Germany-based instances show that explicitly considering charging-related emissions reduces realized emissions by 28.76% on average relative to carbon-blind optimization, although at the expense of higher non-carbon operating cost. Moreover, decision-focused training reduces realized total cost by 2.30% on average relative to conventional prediction-focused learning and performs better on 75.5% of the test instances, despite substantially lower pointwise predictive accuracy. Finally, we show that its average advantage remains positive across varied solver configurations and carbon-cost weights.

Introduction
Road transport electrification is an important strategy for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. In this context, the EU Electrification Action Plan
(
European Commission, 2026
)
proposes increasing electricity’s share of the EU’s final energy consumption to 46% by 2040. Transport is particularly relevant to this transition, as it accounts for almost one third of final energy consumption in the EU, approximately 75% of which is attributable to road transport
(
Eurostat, 2026
)
. Electrifying road transport can therefore play an important role in achieving the EU’s broader electrification objectives. However, although electric vehicles produce no tailpipe emissions, their operation still generates indirect emissions through the production of electricity used for charging. Reducing these emissions requires attention to both electricity generation and how vehicles charge.
Charging-related emissions depend on both the amount of electricity consumed and its carbon intensity, which varies over time with the electricity generation mix. Consequently, two routing solutions with similar energy consumption can produce substantially different emissions. An energy-minimizing solution is therefore not necessarily emission-minimizing, since charging can be shifted toward periods of lower grid carbon intensity. Exploiting this opportunity requires coordinating routing, charging, and waiting decisions while satisfying operational constraints. We therefore study a carbon-aware Electric Vehicle Routing Problem (EVRP) that balances conventional operating costs against charging-related emissions under time-varying grid carbon intensity. Since future carbon intensity is unknown when these decisions are made, this coordination also depends on the forecasts used to guide optimization.
The classical approach to solving such problems is via two sequential steps: first training a predictive model, and then treating the prediction output as an input to an algorithm for solving the optimization problem. This approach, known as
prediction-focused learning (PFL)
(
Mandi et al., 2024
)
, treats prediction and optimization as separate tasks. However, high prediction accuracy does not necessarily translate into better decisions, as prediction errors differ in their impacts on the downstream optimization problem. In contrast,
decision-focused learning (DFL)
incorporates the downstream optimization problem into model training to improve decision quality
(
Mandi et al., 2024
)
. In this paper, we develop a
DFL
-based method for the
Electric Vehicle Routing Problem (EVRP)
, training a linear predictor of grid carbon intensity over the routing horizon to maximize the use of clean electricity for charging. In particular, we train this predictor using approximate
SPO
+ directions, employing a
large neighborhood search (LNS)
followed by exact fixed-route optimization of charging quantities and waiting times as the downstream solution method.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
•
We formulate a carbon-aware
EVRP
that jointly optimizes routing, charging, and waiting under time-varying grid carbon intensity.
•
We develop a scalable solution procedure combining
LNS
with A
∗
for charging-path construction and exact fixed-route postprocessing. The exact postprocessing step refines the charging quantities and waiting times of the routes produced by the
LNS
.
•
We integrate this solution procedure into an approximate
SPO
+ training loop, allowing the carbon-intensity predictor to be trained
directly with respect to its downstream routing performance.
•
We evaluate the approach on realistic instances based on the
German road network and electricity data, demonstrating that
DFL
reduces average realized total cost relative to
PFL
and other baselines.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the literature relevant to the study. Section
3
defines the considered
EVRP
and presents its
mixed-integer linear program (MILP)
formulation. Section
4
introduces the proposed
DFL
framework and its downstream solver, including the
LNS
and the exact fixed-route postprocessing step. Section
5
evaluates the methodology on realistic Germany-based instances and compares it with conventional
PFL
. Finally, Section
6
summarizes the main conclusions and outlines directions for future research.

Literature Review
We first review papers where the problem tackled is relevant to the one considered in this work. Then, we present an overview of the most relevant existing methods for
DFL
. Finally, we review the most relevant applications of
DFL
, particularly the
SPO

Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs)
and compare them with the application presented in this paper.
2.1
Carbon-Aware Routing and Charging
The
Electric Vehicle Routing Problem (EVRP)
is a variant of the classical
VRP
in which the vehicles employed are electric. This variant has become popular in recent years as electric vehicles have been gaining prevalence in real-world routing applications. The main advantages of using electric vehicles in routing applications include lower running costs and lower pollution. Lower costs are due to three main reasons: (i) electricity is usually cheaper than fuel, (ii) electric drivetrains are more efficient, and (iii) electric drivetrains generally require less maintenance than combustion drivetrains. On the other hand, electric vehicles can also produce less pollution as they do not burn fuel during driving, and the electricity used for charging the batteries can be generated from low-polluting sources.
An important detail is that, if the generation of electricity is done using carbon-intensive sources, such as coal, oil or natural gas, the use of electric vehicles can lead to higher lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions than simply employing efficient hybrid or gas-powered vehicles. This is highlighted in
Singh et al. (2024)
, where the authors show that in some regions of the United States, electric vehicles lead to higher lifecycle greenhouse emissions than some efficient hybrid counterparts due to a high grid carbon intensity. This motivates the main idea of this paper, which is to account for the variability in the carbon intensity of the grid, with the aim of charging during lower carbon intensity periods and ultimately using cleaner electricity for charging.
Most
EVRP
papers focus on minimizing the amount of vehicles employed, the total energy consumption, the total travelling time, or a combination of these values
(
Kucukoglu et al., 2021
)
. To our knowledge, there are no
EVRP
papers focusing on directly minimizing the carbon intensity of the electricity used for charging. In the wider field of routing problems, some similar ideas have been applied to end-to-end routing, for example in
Su et al. (2025)
, the authors consider the time-varying carbon intensity of the grid and present an optimization model to schedule the charging of electric trucks in long-haul end-to-end routes across the United States. Another related paper is
Jarvis et al. (2022)
, which concerns the scheduling of electric bus charging, but instead of explicitly considering the carbon intensity of the grid over the routing horizon, they predict the excess in clean energy originating from wind generation and aim to maximize the charging done during periods with a surplus of clean energy.
One of the
EVRP
papers conceptually closest to ours is
Miyabe et al. (2025)
, in which the authors consider the coordination of charging with solar energy production. In particular, they consider time-varying local surplus power from solar generation associated with each charging station, aiming to maximize the use of solar power for charging. Our paper instead focuses on treating the carbon intensity of the whole grid as uncertain and on forecasting it before making routing decisions. We consider a single forecasting model with the task of predicting the carbon intensity of the grid over time, while in the compared work they use an independent forecasting model for each station, which gathers features from a local area around it. Moreover, in the present paper we apply
DFL
to make the training process aware of the downstream routing solver.
2.2
Decision-Focused Learning
Many real-world optimization problems follow a similar structure, in which the formulation of the problem depends on a vector of uncertain values that need to be predicted. Most commonly, these uncertain values appear in the objective function. Classical examples of this setting include routing applications in transport where travel times are not known in advance and portfolio allocation problems, in which the future value of assets is also unknown and needs to be predicted instead.
The structure of these types of problems is generally denoted as Predict-then-Optimize and the classical way of solving them consists of treating the prediction and optimization steps as independent. As mentioned in the introduction, this approach is usually denoted as
prediction-focused learning (PFL)
. Under this approach, the prediction model is trained independently of the optimization problem, generally via standard supervised learning aiming to maximize prediction accuracy.
Decision-focused learning (DFL)
is an alternative to
PFL
that consists of making the training of the prediction model aware of the optimization problem. The main idea is to train the prediction model so that the performance of the downstream optimization algorithm is maximized, without explicitly optimizing its pointwise predictive accuracy.
The motivation behind this approach lies in the fact that small prediction errors can lead to big drops in downstream performance. The main challenge in
DFL
is that training requires information about how the solution of the downstream optimization problem changes with the predicted parameters. This solution mapping is generally available only implicitly through an optimization solver and for combinatorial optimization problems it is often discontinuous and piecewise constant, so its derivatives are either undefined or equal to zero almost everywhere.
Following the taxonomy by
Mandi et al. (2024)
, gradient-based
DFL
methods can be organized into four families that apply to different classes of downstream optimization problems and obtain informative gradients in different ways. Broadly, these approaches seek to minimize decision regret, which is the increase in the true objective value caused by using the decision induced by the predicted coefficients rather than an optimal decision under the true coefficients.
First, we have approaches based on analytical differentiation that differentiate optimality conditions or iterations of a solution algorithm of continuous optimization problems
(
Amos and Kolter, 2017
;
Kotary et al., 2023
;
Domke, 2012
)
. The second consists of methods that replace the original optimization problem with a smooth approximation, so that the resulting solution mapping can be differentiated
(
Wilder et al., 2019
;
Ferber et al., 2020
)
. The third consists of perturbation-based methods that estimate gradients from the solutions obtained with the initial parameters perturbed
(
Domke, 2010
;
Berthet et al., 2020
;
Niepert et al., 2021
)
. Finally, surrogate-loss methods replace decision regret with a tractable loss designed to promote downstream solution quality
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
;
Mulamba et al., 2021
;
Mandi et al., 2022
;
Shah et al., 2022
)
. These approaches differ in their assumptions about the optimization problem, their need for access to solver internals, and the number and type of optimization calls required during training.
In general, there is no universally preferable
DFL
method: the appropriate choice depends on the downstream problem, the predictive model, the available access to the optimizer, and the computational cost of solving it.
Among these approaches, the surrogate loss
SPO
+
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
)
is particularly compatible with the problem considered in this work. This is because the uncertain carbon-intensity vector enters the downstream
EVRP
linearly through the amount of energy charged in each time period, while the feasible region is independent of this prediction. Furthermore,
SPO
+ requires only solutions under modified objective coefficients and not differentiation through the internal operations of the solver, which makes it suitable for a problem involving discrete routing decisions. Although alternative perturbation-based
DFL
methods could also be applied,
SPO
+ provides a direct, theoretically motivated surrogate for decision regret while requiring comparatively few calls to the computationally expensive downstream solver.
The original
SPO
+ construction assumes access to an exact optimization oracle. In our setting, repeatedly solving the full
EVRP
to optimality during training would be computationally impractical. We therefore use an approximate downstream solver consisting of a time-limited
LNS
followed by exact fixed-route postprocessing. The resulting update directions are approximate
SPO
+ subgradients and their effectiveness should therefore be assessed empirically as the theoretical guarantees established under exact optimization are not necessarily inherited. Our experimental evaluation shows that training with these approximate subgradients effectively improves downstream solution quality relative to conventional
PFL
.
2.3
Decision-Focused Learning for Vehicle Routing
Applications of
DFL
to vehicle routing remain relatively limited compared with applications to simpler combinatorial problems such as shortest paths. The work most closely related to our setting is
Chu et al. (2023)
, which considers a last-mile delivery problem formulated as a capacitated
VRP
with uncertain travel times. The authors train a linear travel-time prediction model using
SPO
+ and employ a simulated annealing heuristic to obtain the routing solutions required during training. This combination of
SPO
+, vehicle routing, and a heuristic optimization oracle is particularly close to the approach adopted in this paper. In both cases, the use of a heuristic makes it possible to perform the repeated routing computations required by decision-focused training without solving an exact formulation at every iteration. The main differences concern the predicted quantity and the downstream problem:
Chu et al. (2023)
predict arc travel times for a last-mile CVRP and use simulated annealing, whereas we predict time-dependent grid carbon intensities for an
EVRP
with charging decisions and use a time-limited
LNS
followed by exact fixed-route postprocessing as the downstream solver.
Another direct application of
DFL
to vehicle routing is presented by
Mandi et al. (2021)
. Their objective is to learn implicit preferences from historical routes, represented through predicted arc probabilities, and incorporate these into a CVRP. Rather than using
SPO
+, they treat an exact mathematical-programming solver as a black box and obtain surrogate gradients through additional solves with perturbed objective coefficients. This work demonstrates the applicability of
DFL
to a CVRP, but differs from ours both in its learning objective and in its reliance on exact optimization. It also illustrates the importance of the amount of available training data, since the authors report that the decision-focused approach does not improve out-of-sample route quality in their limited-data experiments.
DFL
has also been applied to other related routing settings.
Tian et al. (2023)
consider a maritime routing problem in which port-state-control officers must select and sequence ship inspections based on predictions of ship deficiencies. Since repeatedly solving the NP-hard routing problem during training is computationally expensive, the authors use surrogate losses based on noise-contrastive estimation and a pool of previously generated feasible solutions, thereby reducing the need for reoptimization. Although this is not a vehicle-delivery problem and does not use
SPO
+, it highlights a central difficulty shared with our setting: incorporating a hard routing problem into the learning loop requires controlling the computational cost of repeated optimization.
Taken together, these studies show that
DFL
can improve routing decisions, while also emphasizing the need for scalable downstream optimization.

Problem Description
We consider a carbon-aware electric vehicle routing problem with time windows. A homogeneous fleet serves customers from a central depot while adhering to capacity, battery, time window, and routing horizon constraints. Vehicles leave the depot fully charged and can visit charging stations, where they may wait before charging if beneficial. Upon returning to the depot, vehicles recharge to full capacity. Routing, charging, and waiting decisions are jointly optimized to minimize operational costs and indirect charging emissions. 4
Methodology
This section presents the proposed decision-focused learning framework and its downstream optimization algorithm. Figure
2
summarizes the complete methodology. A linear model predicts grid carbon intensity over the routing horizon, and these predictions parameterize the carbon-related terms of the
EVRP
objective. A time-limited
LNS
followed by exact fixed-route postprocessing is used as the downstream solver. As already mentioned, this approximate downstream solver is necessary because repeatedly solving the full
MILP
to optimality for every training instance and parameter update would be computationally impractical for the realistic problem instances we are interested in.
During training, this solver is applied under transformed carbon-intensity vectors to construct approximate
SPO
+ subgradients. At the end of each epoch, the current predictor is evaluated on the validation set by computing a validation regret. This value is the average relative difference between the costs of the solutions obtained from the current predictions and those of the corresponding precomputed reference solutions, with both evaluated under the true carbon intensities. Validation regret is used for model selection.
During evaluation, the trained predictor supplies forecasts to the same algorithm, and the resulting solutions are evaluated under the realized carbon intensities.

5
Experimental Evaluation
We evaluate the proposed methodology on synthetic
EVRP
instances constructed using real-world German road-network, charging-infrastructure, weather, and electricity-system data. Germany provides a relevant setting because its grid carbon intensity varies substantially over time as the generation mix changes. This temporal variation creates opportunities to reduce charging-related emissions by optimizing routing, charging, and waiting decisions.
We focus on long-haul routes spanning multiple days. The need for en-route charging, together with carbon-intensity variations across multiple days, makes this setting suitable for examining how charging locations, quantities, and timing can be coordinated to balance operating costs and emissions.

6
Conclusions and Future Work
This paper presented an
EVRP
formulation that incorporates charging-related emissions into its objective. The proposed formulation jointly optimizes routes, charging, and waiting decisions while accounting for temporal variation in grid carbon intensity. This is motivated by the substantial temporal variation in the electricity generation mix of some regions, since charging during periods of high grid carbon intensity can substantially increase charging-related emissions and, in sufficiently carbon-intensive grids, can even erode the lifecycle emissions advantage of electric vehicles over efficient hybrid alternatives.
We presented a decision-focused learning approach for tackling this problem. A linear predictor was trained using approximate
SPO
+ subgradients to predict the carbon intensity of the grid over the routing horizon, while a time-limited
LNS
with exact postprocessing was used as the downstream solver.
The experimental results demonstrate the usefulness of this carbon-aware formulation, as the solutions produced reduced emissions by
28.76
%
28.76\%
on average relative to carbon-blind optimization, while increasing the non-carbon cost by
11.06
%
11.06\%
. Under the baseline objective coefficients, this trade-off resulted in a
20.24
%
20.24\%
reduction in realized total cost.
Moreover, the results also illustrate the distinction between predictive accuracy and downstream decision quality. Although the
PFL
-trained model achieved substantially lower forecasting MAE and RMSE, the
DFL
-trained predictor produced better solutions on 75.5% of the test instances relative to
PFL
. Its mean advantage also remained positive across the evaluated
LNS
configurations and carbon-cost weights, indicating robustness to the solver and carbon cost configurations. The exploratory monthly analysis nevertheless revealed weaker performance toward the end of the year, indicating that the benefits of decision-focused training were heterogeneous across the evaluation period.
The principal limitation is computational cost: the selected epoch (72) was reached after approximately 44 hours, while the complete 100-epoch training run required approximately 64 hours. To alleviate this issue, future work could consider further reducing the time limit assigned to the
LNS
during training and exploring the trade-off between this time limit and downstream performance. Another option would be to construct a surrogate model to reduce the number of
LNS
and postprocessing executions.
Future work could also investigate stratified or seasonally balanced training samples and examine whether the observed seasonal deterioration is caused by limited training coverage, changes in grid behavior, or characteristics of the routing instances. Also, richer predictive models could be considered. Finally, further extensions could consider routes spanning several electricity bidding regions, with region-specific carbon-intensity predictions.
