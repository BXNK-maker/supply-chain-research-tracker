Abstract
A key source of uncertainty in multi-agent systems is the behavior of other agents. Game-theoretic control approaches ignore this uncertainty, while robust control approaches treat others as fully adversarial.
In this paper, we introduce strategically robust multi-agent control to populate the spectrum between these two paradigms.
Specifically, in dynamic games, we propose that players are
strategically
robust and seek protection against misspecified control policies of the other players.
This formulation induces a novel equilibrium concept,
strategically robust dynamic equilibrium
, that interpolates between game-theoretic control and robust control. We study this concept across canonical open- and closed-loop settings and show that accounting for strategic robustness also has computational benefits over classic approaches in game-theoretic control. Numerical experiments on vehicle platooning, inventory management, and network control illustrate resilience to misspecification, and also exhibit a “free-lunch” phenomenon in which strategic robustness can improve performance or social welfare.
Index Terms:
Multi-agent control, Dynamic games, Game theory, Robust control, Optimal control.

Introduction
In multi-agent systems like autonomous driving
[
1
]
, electricity markets
[
2
]
, and real-world robotics tasks
[
3
]
, one of the largest sources of uncertainty is the behavior of others. This
strategic
uncertainty can arise from various sources such as misspecified models of others’ intent, irrational behavior, or limited computation. Unlike standard exogenous disturbances commonly studied in control, strategic uncertainty is endogenous to the problem: by changing their behavior, an agent affects the uncertainty they are trying to address.
Two common approaches in multi-agent control are to ignore strategic uncertainty and compute game-theoretic controllers under the implicit assumption that other agents’ objectives are well-modeled, or to account for strategic uncertainty through adversarial or worst-case modeling, i.e., robust control.

In game-theoretic control, agents are modeled as rational decision-makers minimizing their own control cost.
The natural solution concept is that of Nash equilibrium, a configuration at which no agent can lower their cost by unilaterally changing their control policy.
While natural in theory, the practical deployment of Nash equilibrium control policies suffers from a fundamental limitation: policies are brittle, i.e., sensitive to misspecification of the other agents’ control policies. For instance,
[
4
]
demonstrates that adhering to a standard Nash equilibrium when opponents are non-rational can lead to significantly suboptimal outcomes.
In addition to their poor robustness, Nash equilibria also pose significant computational challenges, and can generally be computed efficiently only under structural assumptions, such as monotonicity of the pseudo-gradient or the invertibility conditions arising in linear-quadratic games
[
5
]
, which restrict the class of problems that can be solved in practice.
In robust control, instead, the other agents are modeled as adversarial disturbances, whose goal is to maximize the control cost.
The classical
ℋ
∞
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
control problem can be cast as a zero-sum game between the control engineer, who minimizes control cost, and a fictitious agent, who instead maximizes control cost and whose influence is attenuated only through a penalty on its magnitude.
A natural way to inject robustness to strategic uncertainty is thus to treat the control policies of the others as exogenous disturbances, as in, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Doing so, however, completely disregards the underlying game-theoretic interactions: the other agents are not arbitrary disturbances, but decision-makers with their own objectives, whose behavior should be expected to remain close to the policies prescribed by the game.
Thus, while computationally tractable, it inevitably leads to overly conservative behavior and sacrifices nominal performance. In other words, game-theoretic control trusts the other agents entirely, while robust control treats their control inputs as arbitrary disturbances.
In this paper, we populate the spectrum between these two solution concepts; see Figure
1
. We take inspiration from recent work on strategic risk aversion in multi-agent reinforcement learning
[
9
]
and strategically robust game theory in one-shot games
[
10
]
, from which we also inherit the name “strategic robustness.”
Specifically, we propose that agents choose their strategy not by playing against the policies of the other agents (as in game-theoretic control) but instead against a fictitious adversary who seeks to inflict maximum damage (as in robust control), but pays a penalty for deviating from the policies of the other agents.
This deviation penalty controls how tightly the adversary is anchored to the other players’ policies.
When it is prohibitively large, deviations are too expensive and the adversary is forced toward the policies of the other agents, recovering standard game-theoretic control.
As the penalty weakens, the adversary gains power and the player’s policy becomes increasingly robust. When an explicit disturbance penalty keeps the adversarial problem well posed, the zero-deviation-penalty limit is a classical worst-case
ℋ
∞
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
control problem—a connection we make precise in this paper.
Together with the direct disturbance penalty, the deviation penalty defines a two-parameter family containing the standard Nash and
ℋ
∞
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
problems as distinct limiting cases.
Beyond our formulation, our work also highlights other advantages of strategically robust (game-theoretic) control. In particular, it often enjoys better computational properties compared to standard game-theoretic control and can sometimes lead to more efficient and performant outcomes.
We instantiate these ideas in the setting of discrete-time dynamic games. Dynamic games are used in a wide variety of control applications, including robotics
[
11
]
, aerospace
[
12
]
, and economics
[
13
]
, to name a few.
In these games, the state of a dynamical system is controlled by a set of agents that select policies to minimize their personal control cost, which depends on the state of the system and on the agents’ inputs.
Specifically, we study two classes of dynamic games: the finite-horizon open-loop setting, with linear dynamics, convex costs, and convex constraints, and the finite-horizon closed-loop linear-quadratic setting, which we further extend to the infinite horizon.
Contributions
We introduce strategically robust dynamic equilibria, a solution concept for dynamic games that protects agents against strategic uncertainty, i.e., uncertainty about the behavior of other agents.
Strategically robust dynamic equilibria allow us to interpolate between game-theoretic control (Nash equilibria) and robust control (
ℋ
∞
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
), all of this while being “more computationally tractable” than standard Nash equilibria.
Specifically, our contributions are fourfold.
1.
Open loop.
After establishing existence of strategically robust dynamic equilibria, we give verifiable conditions under which a reweighted pseudo-gradient is strongly monotone.
These conditions imply a unique equilibrium and global linear convergence of the projected pseudo-gradient method on compact feasible sets, or when the pseudo-gradient is globally Lipschitz.
2.
Closed loop, finite horizon.
We characterize closed-loop strategically robust dynamic equilibria via coupled backward Riccati equations. This way, we show that, under standard conditions, the strategically robust dynamic equilibrium is unique (in the subgame-perfect sense), linear, and Markovian. Strategic robustness additionally yields a sufficient condition for invertibility of the coupled stage system.
3.
Closed loop, infinite horizon.
Outside of the zero-sum setting, Nash equilibria of infinite-horizon games are notoriously hard to compute.
We show that, with sufficient robustness, a stationary linear equilibrium exists, is unique in a ball that becomes arbitrarily large as agents become more robust, and is reached at a geometric rate by best-response iteration—every step of which is a classical
ℋ
∞
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
design.
4.
Experiments.
Strategic robustness protects agents against misspecified policies and adversarial perturbations of other agents’ controllers. In our examples, we empirically demonstrate that it can also lead to a “free-lunch” effect, in that it coordinates the agents and improves individual nominal performance or social welfare.
