1.4
Techniques
Algorithmic framework.
Our algorithms for HetTSP and HetCVRP use the same basic framework. We order the vehicles from fastest to slowest and consider a tentative value for the makespan. For a client, we call the walk from the depot to the client and back to the depot its round trip. We then identify the slowest vehicle that can complete this round trip within the tentative makespan, which we call the client’s slowest reachable vehicle. Every slower vehicle would require more than the tentative makespan for this round trip. We then divide the ordered vehicles into consecutive groups. For each group, the clients whose slowest reachable vehicle belongs to that group form a layer client set. The layer client sets are pairwise disjoint and form a partition of the clients, ordered from those that require faster vehicles to those that can also be served by slower vehicles.
For each layer client set, we construct a tour through its clients and the depot using the Christofides–Serdjukov algorithm
(
Christofides, 1976
;
Serdjukov, 1978
)
. We compute a minimum spanning tree on the layer client set together with the depot using the original distances, add a minimum-length perfect matching on its odd-degree vertices, and shortcut an Euler traversal to obtain the tour. For HetCVRP, we also apply the tour-splitting approach to obtain capacity-feasible tours. Thus each layer client set has a closed walk starting and ending at the depot: one tour for HetTSP, or a concatenation of capacity-feasible tours for HetCVRP. We concatenate these closed walks in their order and assign subwalks of the resulting walk to the vehicles from fastest to slowest, proceeding in the order of the walk. Each assigned subwalk is extended as far as possible, provided that adding direct connections from its endpoints to the depot keeps the route within the vehicle’s permitted length. This permitted length is its speed times a makespan bound computed from the tentative makespan, the constructed closed walks, and the vehicle groups. This bound may exceed the tentative makespan.
The tentative makespan guides the construction but is not assumed to be feasible. The algorithm enumerates a polynomial number of tentative makespans and partitions into vehicle groups and returns the best resulting solution.
Why the framework improves the guarantees.
With nonuniform speeds, it is not enough to divide a tour into short parts: a short part may still be too far from the depot for a slow vehicle to serve. The approach of
Gørtz et al. (2016)
handles this interaction by constructing a Level-Prim tree, decomposing it into subtours containing the depot that satisfy conditions for assignment, and then assigning these subtours through a scheduling argument. Our approach differs in how the clients are organized, how the constructed walks are assigned, and how capacity is incorporated.
The Level-Prim tree of
Gørtz et al. (2016)
organizes vertices by distance ranges, with compatibility with vehicle speeds enforced through the subsequent decomposition and assignment. Our layer client sets use the slowest reachable vehicle of each client directly. Every client in a layer client set can be reached within the tentative makespan by the first, and hence a fastest, vehicle of its vehicle group, while no vehicle appearing after that group in the speed order can make the same round trip in that time. This gives an upper bound on distances for the final assignment and a lower bound for the tour analysis. For the analysis, we fix an optimal HetTSP solution for HetTSP and an optimal split-delivery HetCVRP solution for both versions of HetCVRP. Among the enumerated tentative makespans, one gives every client the same slowest reachable vehicle as the makespan of this optimal solution. For that value, the clients of a layer client set are served only by vehicles in the corresponding vehicle group or in earlier, faster groups. The routes of the optimal solution can then be shortcut to tours whose client sets are pairwise disjoint and cover the layer client set. Deleting one edge incident to the depot from each nonempty tour produces a spanning tree, and each deleted edge has length at least the common lower bound on the clients’ distances from the depot. The minimum spanning tree chosen by our algorithm has no greater length, so its length bound also benefits from these deletions. Adding a minimum-length perfect matching and shortcutting then gives an upper bound of three halves of the available route length minus the depot saving obtained by combining these deletions with the bounds on the optimal vehicle routes. The depot saving becomes useful when we concatenate the closed walks for different layer client sets and divide the resulting walk among the vehicles. In this allocation, a vehicle may be assigned a subwalk that spans consecutive layer client sets, while the returns to the depot already present in the closed walks are retained. The lengths of the assigned subwalks are analyzed together with the direct connections from their endpoints to the depot. The vehicle groups are chosen from the slowest end toward the fastest so that the depot savings in the tour bounds offset part of the length of these direct connections. In this way, tour construction and vehicle assignment are analyzed together, without the separate scheduling step used by
Gørtz et al. (2016)

Capacity is handled directly within the same framework. After building the tour for a layer client set, we divide it into capacity-feasible tours using the tour-splitting approach. The additional length is bounded by an additive value depending on the client demands and their distances from the depot. Thus capacity enters the same analysis as an additive term, instead of through the reduction of
