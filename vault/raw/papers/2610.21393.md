Self-Guided LLM Reasoning for Error Diagnosis in Vehicle Routing Problems
Ni Zhang
Jianan Zhou
Yue-Jiao Gong
Zhiguang Cao
Abstract
Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) are fundamental to many real-world applications. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have spurred progress in solving these NP-hard problems. However, most existing approaches assume that input instances are error-free, neglecting the presence of faults commonly encountered in real-world data. In this work, we systematically investigate the capability of LLMs to detect (and correct) errors in VRP instances, and identify key limitations, particularly in handling complex instances or errors that substantially increase the LLM’s reasoning burden. To overcome these challenges, we propose a self-guided reasoning framework in which LLMs autonomously generate code that provides structured insights to aid in error diagnosis. Experiments across diverse problem settings and LLMs demonstrate that our framework significantly improves error detection performance, achieving near-perfect accuracy.

Introduction
Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) are widely deployed in real-world decision-making pipelines across last-mile delivery
(
Tiwari and Sharma 2023
;
Tilk et al. 2021
;
Kitjacharoenchai and Lee 2019
)
, transportation
(
Gayialis et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
, and ride-sharing
(
Lin et al. 2012
;
Guo et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
. While decades of research have produced highly efficient optimization algorithms, the reliability of these pipelines remains fragile: subtle inconsistencies or latent errors in problem instances can lead to solver failures or infeasible solutions.
Most VRP solvers assume that input instances are well-formed and feasible. In practice, however, instances may contain errors such as formatting issues, missing fields, or violated feasibility constraints. Importantly, many of these faults are
latent
as they are not immediately evident from the instance specification and require additional analysis to be identified.
Given the diversity of VRP formulations and input formats across problem settings, designing general-purpose programs for automatic error detection is highly challenging.
As a result, prior approaches typically rely on domain expertise or post hoc analysis of solver outputs to infer potential instance-level issues, which is both time-consuming and expertise-dependent.
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a promising new direction. With strong language understanding and reasoning capabilities, LLMs can interpret diverse inputs and reason about structured information. These capabilities naturally prompt following questions:
a) Can LLMs be enabled to identify and correct errors in VRP instances? b) How and why does such reasoning succeed or fail? c) Under what conditions do LLMs struggle to identify errors, and how can these limitations be effectively mitigated?
Recent work has begun to explore this direction. For example, MOID
(
Li et al. 2025
)
attempts to identify potential instance-level issues by examining the feasibility of solutions produced by a solver. However, it heavily relies on solver outputs and thus inherits their limitations, since infeasible solutions may arise from solver deficiencies rather than errors in the input instance. Moreover, a comprehensive evaluation of LLMs’ capability to directly identify and correct errors within VRP instances remains absent to date.
In this paper, we systematically investigate the capability of LLMs to diagnose errors in VRP instances. To address the aforementioned research questions, we evaluate: (i) LLMs’ ability to handle increased error complexity, (ii) their ability to cope with increased instance complexity, and (iii) the effect of iterative reasoning on error detection performance.
Our findings show that (i) LLMs perform well in detecting formatting errors and relatively simple constraint violations, such as capacity and demand constraints. However, their ability to identify more complex constraint violations, including time windows and distance limits, as well as scenarios with larger numbers of errors, is substantially weaker. (ii) Detection performance degrades as instance scale and constraint counts increases, indicating limited robustness under increased instance complexity. (iii) While iterative reasoning improves the detection of formatting issues and simple constraint violations, it provides limited benefit for identifying more complex errors.
In summary, we find that LLMs are effective at identifying and correcting errors in relatively simple settings, whereas their performance degrades when confronted with instances or errors of higher complexity. To address limitations, we propose a self-guided reasoning framework in which the LLM generates analysis code as an intermediate reasoning tool. This code enables the model to extract structured insights that facilitate error detection while alleviating overall reasoning burden. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We present a comprehensive study of LLMs’ capability to detect errors in VRP instances, including a systematic analysis of how error complexity, instance complexity and reasoning depth affect detection performance.
•
We propose a self-guided reasoning framework in which LLMs generate code to derive intermediate reasoning insights, addressing their limitations in handling complex errors while reducing the overall reasoning burden.
•
Extensive experiments across diverse problem settings, including vehicle routing and bin packing, demonstrate that our framework significantly enhances error detection performance, achieving near-perfect accuracy.

Related work
Existing uses of LLMs in VRPs broadly fall into two categories. The first line of work focuses on using LLMs to directly generate solutions. For example, OPRO
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
employs LLMs to produce solutions directly, LEMA
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
leverages LLMs to perform genetic search, and Jiang et al.
(
Jiang et al. 2025
)
fine-tune LLMs for solution generation. The second line of work instead leverages LLMs to generate heuristic code or optimization operators for solving VRPs. FunSearch
(
Romera-Paredes et al. 2024
)
adopts an island-based evolutionary framework to guide LLMs in generating heuristic programs, while EOH
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
integrates LLMs with evolutionary algorithms to automatically discover improved heuristics. ReEvo
(
Ye et al. 2024
)
further augments the evolutionary loop with reflective self-feedback, enabling the LLM to critique and refine its own proposals, and HSEvo
(
Dat et al. 2025
)
introduces a hierarchical evolutionary structure with diversity-aware selection over modular heuristic components. AFL
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
extends this paradigm with an agentic framework for solving complex VRPs through multi-stage reasoning and coding. Overall, these works demonstrate the growing potential of LLMs.
However, all of these methods assume that input instances are feasible and error-free, and therefore lack the capability to handle instance-level errors that frequently arise in practice. MOID
(
Li et al. 2025
)
takes a step toward error detection by analyzing solver execution, but its reliance on potentially unreliable solvers makes it difficult to distinguish between instance-level errors and solver-induced failures. As such, it serves as an initial attempt rather than a comprehensive solution. In this work, we bridge this gap by enabling LLMs to directly identify and reason about instance-level errors.

3.1
Error Complexity
We analyze LLMs’ ability to handle varying levels of error complexity, including constraint violations across different problem variants and varying numbers of errors.
Problem Variant.
We analyze error detection performance across 7 simple VRP variants, each containing one or two types of constraints.
As shown in Fig.
1
, LLMs achieve consistently high detection rates for formatting errors and faults associated with simple constraints (e.g., capacity (C)), indicated by the red bars. These results suggest that LLMs are effective at identifying errors that can be detected through basic inspection or straightforward logical checks.
However, detection rates degrade substantially as constraint complexity increases. For variants involving backhaul (B) constraints, accuracy declines noticeably. For more complex constraints, such as time windows (TW) and distance limits (L), detection rates drop sharply and approach zero.
This highlights a key limitation: while LLMs perform well on formatting errors and simple constraint violations, they struggle with errors that require complex calculations or numerical reasoning.

Effect of fault number and instance scale.
Error Number.
We further evaluate the impact of error number on LLM-based error detection by varying the number of injected errors and measuring detection performance across different VRP variants. To provide a more comprehensive assessment of its impact, we select variants with increasing constraint complexity, ranging from CVRP, which involves a single type of constraint, to MDOCVRPMBLTW, which incorporates six types of constraints.
Fig.
2
shows that detection performance consistently declines as the number of injected errors increases across all variants. This trend holds regardless of the underlying problem type, indicating that LLMs are highly sensitive to the accumulation of errors within an instance. These results suggest that a larger number of errors imposes a substantially greater reasoning burden on LLMs, as they must simultaneously identify more errors.

Instance Complexity
Beyond error complexity, intrinsic instance complexity may also impact LLMs’ ability to detect errors. To study this, we conduct experiments across varying instance scales and constraint counts.

We examine the effect of instance scale on error detection performance by comparing instances of different sizes. As shown in Fig.

This performance degradation indicates that larger instance significantly increase the complexity of instance-level reasoning. As the number of nodes grows, LLMs reason over a larger set of constraints and dependency relationships, which exacerbates the reasoning burden and makes accurate error identification increasingly difficult.

3.3
Reasoning Depth
Iterative reasoning is an effective approach to improve error detection. To evaluate its impact, we analyze the role of reasoning depth through an iterative process, in which the LLM identifies errors, the instance is repaired accordingly, and the LLM then reasons again on the updated version. As shown in Fig.
3
, increasing reasoning depth consistently improves detection rates across VRP variants. For variants with relatively simple constraints (e.g., CVRP and CVRPB), repeated reasoning yields performance gains, with detection rates approaching 100% after several iterations.
However, for problems with more complex constraints, the benefits of deeper reasoning diminish. In particular, for instances with constraints such as time windows and combinations of multiple constraints, detection performance remains far from perfect. These results suggest that while iterative reasoning can facilitate error detection by LLMs, it remains insufficient for handling complex errors, such as violations involving intricate constraints or combinations of constraints that require sophisticated feasibility reasoning.
4
Self-Guided Reasoning Framework
Our analysis of LLMs’ ability to detect instance-level errors reveals a key limitation: their difficulty in handling instances or errors of higher complexity which may require advanced reasoning.
To address this, we propose a self-guided reasoning framework in which the LLM first constructs a reasoning chain for error detection and then uses it to guide the generation of analysis code. The generated code yields structured insights that the LLM can reference, enhancing its ability to identify errors while alleviating the overall reasoning burden.
As illustrated in Fig.
4
, the framework begins with instance analysis, where the LLM analyzes the input instance by identifying the problem type, required section names, and associated constraints. Based on this analysis, the LLM derives a reasoning path that outlines how to analyze potential errors in the instance and generates corresponding function-level prompts for each step. Guided by these prompts, executable code is generated and run to extract relevant insights. Finally, using both the original instance and the extracted insights as references, the LLM produces a final report.
4.1
Instance Analysis
Given an input instance, the instance analysis stage aims to establish a concise and structured understanding of the problem specification.
The LLM first parses the raw instance to identify its essential characteristics, which are summarized as follows:
•
Problem type
, indicating the underlying optimization problem (e.g., CVRP) implied by the instance.
•
Required section names
, specifying the mandatory sections that a valid instance of the identified problem type should contain.
•
Associated constraints
, describing the feasibility conditions in the identified problem, such as capacity, time window and so on.
Based on this structured information, the LLM performs a self-validation step to identify potential inconsistencies among the problem type, required sections, associated constraints, and the instance content, and revises its analysis accordingly.
Finally, the LLM produces an instance analysis summary, providing an intuitive and reliable reference that helps reduce the reasoning burden in subsequent stages. The detailed prompt used for instance analysis is provided in Appendix
D.1
.

4.2
Insight Generation
The framework then proceeds to the insight generation stage, where the LLM derives structured insights for error analysis. A detailed example of the prompt used at this stage is provided in Appendix
D.2
. Based on the previously generated instance analysis summary, the LLM infers which parts of the instance are likely to contain errors. More specifically, it reasons about the potential locations of formatting errors, such as missing sections or invalid values, as well as feasibility errors, such as capacity violations, and then specifies a corresponding reasoning path.
Following this reasoning path, the LLM generates a set of function-level prompts, each corresponding to a specific analysis step. These prompts clearly describe the intended functionality, required inputs, and expected outputs, and are used to guide code generation. During this process, the LLM is instructed to ensure that the generated code reports detailed diagnostic information at the point of error detection, allowing for precise localization and subsequent revision.
The generated code is then executed to perform structured instance analysis and extract concrete evidence of potential errors. If execution failures or runtime bugs occur, the resulting bug messages are fed back to the LLM, which revises the code accordingly. Once execution succeeds, the analysis outputs are collected and summarized as error insights, which form the basis for subsequent reporting and decision-making.
Overall, in this stage, the LLM guides code generation to convert its reasoning into executable procedures.
This design offers two key advantages: (i) complex constraint calculations are offloaded to code execution, addressing the limitations of LLMs in precise numerical reasoning; and
(ii) programmatic analysis enables both efficient and comprehensive evaluation of the instance, substantially reducing the reasoning burden on the LLM.
4.3
Report Generation
In the final stage, the framework performs report generation by synthesizing the derived insights with the original instance information. Guided by the extracted error insights, the LLM systematically aligns the identified errors with the corresponding components of the input instance.
This enables the generation of a comprehensive and structured report that clearly outlines the types, locations, and potential causes of the detected errors. Based on this report, the LLM is further instructed to revise the instance accordingly, correcting the identified errors to obtain a valid and consistent instance specification. The prompt used for this stage is provided in Appendix
D.3
.

5.1
Comparison of Error Detection Rate
We conduct experiments on 48 VRP variants to assess error detection performance. The comparison of Error Detection Rate (EDR) is reported in Table
2
. As shown by these results, directly applying LLMs exhibits limited error detection capability on many variants, especially those involving multiple constraints or complex combinations, where the EDR often falls below 70% and in some cases even below 50%.
By contrast, the proposed self-guide approach consistently achieves improvements in error detection performance across all variants. In the most cases, self-guide raises the EDR to above 95%, and frequently reaches near-perfect detection rates, demonstrating its robustness under diverse problem settings and constraint combinations. Importantly, these performance gains are obtained without introducing significant additional computational overhead. As shown by the time statistics, self-guide only incurs a marginal increase in runtime compared with direct LLM inference, while delivering dramatically higher error detection accuracy.

Conclusion
In this work, we investigate error detection in vehicle routing problem instances and identifies fundamental limitations of large language models (LLMs) when confronted with instances or errors of higher complexity.
To address these challenges, we introduce a self-guided reasoning framework that enables LLMs to generate reasoning paths to guide code generation and produce additional insights, thus enhancing their own reasoning capabilities.
Extensive experiments across diverse problem settings and LLM backbones demonstrate that the proposed approach consistently and substantially improves error detection performance, achieving near-perfect accuracy and underscoring its effectiveness and generality.
Despite its effectiveness, the proposed self-guided framework has certain limitations. While we evaluate the framework on a wide range of COPs and LLM backbones, further validation on additional problem domains and real-world noisy datasets would be valuable to fully assess its generalization. Moreover, some errors in problem instances may interfere with the detection of other errors; for example, missing or malformed sections can obscure subsequent error detection. Although our framework alleviates this issue through iterative reasoning, such cascading effects cannot be entirely eliminated in the initial reasoning round.

In a standard VRP setting, all routes start and end at a depot, each customer must be visited exactly once, and various operational constraints must be satisfied. Due to the rich combination of complex constraints and diverse real-world application scenarios, VRPs are commonly used as a representative benchmark for COPs.

Appendix B
Dataset
B.1
Assumption
We construct our error detection dataset based on benchmark instances from the Route Finder dataset
(
Berto et al. 2025
)
. To evaluate the capability of LLMs in identifying instance-level errors, we systematically inject synthetic errors into valid VRP instances. To ensure that each faulty instance admits a unique and unambiguous interpretation, we impose several assumptions during dataset construction, as described below.
a)
Capacity and Distance Limit Correctness.
Capacity values and distance limits are assumed to be correct and are not subject to corruption. This assumption enables us to disambiguate between potential error sources, such as distinguishing demand-related errors from capacity violations, and separating node coordinate errors from distance limit misconfigurations.
b)
Single Error Per Node.
At most one error is injected per node. For example, if a node is assigned missing or invalid coordinates, no additional errors are introduced for that node. This constraint prevents overlapping errors that could lead to ambiguous error attribution.
c)
Depot Error Exclusion.
When distance limit or time window errors are present, no errors are injected at the depot. This restriction is imposed because depot errors would interfere with the computation of these constraints.
These assumptions enable controlled error injection while avoiding cascading or interacting errors, facilitating a reliable and interpretable evaluation of error detection performance.
B.2
Format Errors
(i)
Value Error.
Value errors refer to numerical values that violate basic validity constraints, such as identifiers being out of range or numerical fields taking negative values where only non-negative values are permitted. For example, a customer ID exceeding the defined index range or a negative demand value constitutes a value error in the CVRP dataset.
(ii)
Value Missing.
Value missing errors occur when required numerical fields are absent in an instance. Typical examples include missing demand values, service times, or coordinates for a customer node.
(iii)
Section Missing.
Section missing errors arise when the name of a required section in the instance description is absent, even though the corresponding data entries may still be present.
(vi)
Duplicate Node.
Duplicate node errors occur when the same node appears multiple times in an instance.
B.3
Feasibility Errors
(i)
Capacity (C).
This constraint is associated with exceed capacity errors, which occur when the total demand assigned to a vehicle exceeds its capacity limit. For LLMs, detecting such errors mainly requires a straightforward comparison between the demand and the capacity value. As a result, this error type is relatively easy to identify, and LLMs typically achieve high detection accuracy.
(ii)
Backhaul (B) and Mixed Backhaul (MB).
For backhaul and mixed backhaul constraints, customer demands may take positive (linehaul) or negative (backhaul) values, and feasibility requires that the absolute value of the accumulated demand along a route does not exceed the vehicle capacity. Compared with the standard capacity constraint, error detection under these settings involves an additional absolute-value operation, which slightly increases the reasoning difficulty and results in lower detection accuracy.
(iii)
Distance Limit (L).
For the distance limit constraint, we compute the maximum round-trip distance between the depot and all customer nodes, and subtract a small margin (0.1) to obtain the distance limit value. We then check whether the round-trip distance of each node to the depot falls within this limit and record related violations.
(iv)
Open Route (O).
Under the open route constraint, vehicles are not required to return to the depot after completing their routes. Accordingly, the error injection for the distance limit constraint is modified to compute the maximum one-way distance from the depot to all customers and subtract a small margin (0.1) to define the new distance limit value and record violations.
(v)
Time Window (TW).
For the time window constraint, we randomly select a node that has not been assigned any error and compute its travel distance from the depot. The left bound of the time window is set to 0.7 times this distance, and the right bound is set to 0.9 times the same distance, which is designed to make the time window infeasible for that node.
(vi)
Multi-Depot (MD).
Under the multi-depot setting, distance-related constraints are evaluated with respect to the nearest depot. Specifically, the distance limit is computed based on the maximum round-trip distance between each node and its closest depot, rather than a single fixed depot. Similarly, when injecting time window errors, the travel distance used to construct the time window bounds is calculated with respect to the nearest depot to the selected node.
