Abstract
With the expansion of complex transport infrastructure, new paths for linking different destinations have emerged, allowing the definition of multiple routes. The introduction of new roads may also lead to disruptions in traffic flow.
This problem can be generalized into a special case of
Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP)
, which aims to find the optimal route for a fleet of vehicles within a network of destinations.
Finding solutions to this problem requires an exponential number of operations, hindering the ability of exact solvers to deal with large instances,
which must therefore be tackled using heuristics, often leading to suboptimal results.
Quantum-based algorithms may allow us to find exact solutions in polynomial time with high probability, offering a potential future alternative to classical solvers and in this work, we focus on the
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
. Our novel contributions are a parameter-initialization strategy that improves the convergence probability and the integration of the subtour constraints directly into the optimization without using additional qubits by employing an approximation of exponential equality constraints. We believe that our results pave the way for effectively solving instances of
VRP
using quantum-based solutions, leveraging the space-efficient problem encoding and our initialization strategy, which limits the effect of barren-plateau under random parameter initialization.

Introduction
The resolution of high-complexity combinatorial problems still represents an open branch of mathematics and computing, with continual research for better solving algorithms capable of producing higher quality solutions in less time and ideally providing mathematically-provable exact solutions to the problems of interest. With combinatorial optimization, it is possible to solve a wide range of problems, highlighting its relevance in the field of logistics, where it can be used to find the optimal way of handling available resources
(
Boria and Paschos, 2011
)
, to optimize industrial processes
(
Crama, 1997
)
, and to improve healthcare systems
(
Abdalkareem et al., 2021
)
, aiding in the definition of optimal schedules aimed at minimizing management costs. Another relevant field of application is the management of vehicle traffic routes, i.e., a topic that has become more relevant with the spread of commercial vehicles, making traffic routing a crucial component of daily life with a significant impact on delivery systems and traffic management. As cities expand and new routes are created with the goal of improving traffic flow, the resulting road network can become less efficient without proper traffic routing
(
Braess et al., 2005
)
. The efficient routing of vehicles is also important to effectively reduce the cost of deliveries by grouping one or more expeditions whenever possible. This task results in a more effective use of available time and fuel, with subsequent economic and logistical advantages.
This traffic routing problem is globally recognized by the name
VRP
and is a well-known instance of NP-Hard
(
Karp, 1972
)
optimization.
The
VRP
can be further extended to model more complex scenario, by adding additional constraints, including (but not limited to):
Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP)
where the maximum volume / weight that each vehicle can carry is limited and customers request specific amounts of capacity,
Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Window (VRPTW)
where each customer requests that a vehicle perform the delivery within a desired time frame,
Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem (MDVRP)
where each vehicle is assigned to one of the multiple depots available within the network, or combinations of them such as
Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Window (CVRPTW)
. However, it is beyond the scope of this article to analyze these variations in detail, and we refer the reader to
Kumar and Panneerselvam (2012)
to delve further into the subject. This family of problems is well known to be
NP-hard
(
Arora and Barak, 2009
;
Karp, 1972
)
, which poses a challenge to current solving techniques as they require an exponential number of operations to solve them.
In the current state of the art, the
VRP
can be solved using either exact or approximate methods. The former are generally based on branch-and-bound algorithms
(
Laporte and Nobert, 1983
;
Carpaneto et al., 1989
)
that generate new solutions until a global optimum is found. These solvers are often part of general-purpose optimization suites, such as Gurobi or IBM CPLEX.
They offer flexible frameworks to solve instances exactly, at the cost of limiting the instance size. Alternatively, approximate methods rely on evolutionary algorithms and heuristics
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
with the goal of generating solutions that are close to the true optimum. A plethora of solvers exists to deal with different variations of the problem, using ad hoc implementations designed to find solutions to particular
VRP
subproblems in a short amount of time. These architectures, despite requiring approximations, enable solving larger problems and are the standard approach in commercial settings.
With the advent of
Quantum Computing (QC)
(
Preskill, 2018
;
Cerezo et al., 2021b
)
, new approaches to solve combinatorial problems have emerged. In particular, quantum computers are naturally suited to solve
Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO)
problems
(
Kim et al., 2025
)
, either via
Quantum Annealing (QA)
(
Rajak et al., 2023
)
(used in analog
QC
) or through its digital counterpart
QAOA
(
Farhi et al., 2014
)
, which is the focus of this article. The computational power of quantum devices is captured by the complexity class
Bounded-Error Quantum Polynomial (BQP)
(
Bernstein and Vazirani, 1997
)
. Although NP-hard problems are not believed to be efficiently solvable even by quantum computers, quantum heuristics such as
QA
and
QAOA
explore the solution space through fundamentally different mechanisms, namely superposition, entanglement and interference. This may yield practical speed-ups or higher-quality solutions on specific problem classes.
To exploit this potential, a problem must first be expressed as an Ising Hamiltonian: both
QA
and
QAOA
rely on the one-to-one correspondence between a
QUBO
formulation of the problem and a Hamiltonian whose ground state encodes the optimal solution. The quantum device is then used to prepare states with a large overlap with this ground state. The bottleneck of this approach lies in the conversion of a generic combinatorial problem into the
QUBO
formalism. This step, despite being always possible, may require–in case of inequality constraints–additional variables and generally require the introduction in the model of hyper-parameters that cannot be directly optimized. This overhead in the problem, given the inherit nature of combinatorial problem, grows quickly in the size of the problem, resulting in a
QUBO
formulation with an exponential number of terms.
Beyond the formulation, the training of
QAOA
is itself challenging. The classical optimization of the variational parameters is hindered by local minima and barren plateaus
(
McClean et al., 2018
)
, i.e., regions where the gradient vanishes exponentially with system size. Several strategies have been proposed to mitigate this, including layer-wise interpolation of parameters
(
Zhou et al., 2020
)
, warm-starting from classical relaxations
(
Egger et al., 2021
)
, and parameter transfer between instances. Many of these, however, depend on problem-specific information or on solving auxiliary problems.
The
VRP
has already been explored in the context of quantum-assisted optimization. The literature on this problem, and more generally quantum combinatorial optimization, can be grouped into two categories:
QA
solvers
(
Morita and Nishimori, 2008
)
and
QAOA
solvers
(
Farhi et al., 2014
)
. Even though both share the exact problem formulation requirements, the former is suitable for analog quantum computing platforms, like the DWave quantum annealers, which apply a continuous evolution to the system. The latter, instead, uses the circuit-based computational model to carry out the optimization and is more suitable for digital quantum devices such as IBM machines. The
QA
based approach can rely on a broader qubit count, allowing the resolution of larger problems. However, the execution time must be long enough to respect the
adiabatic theorem
. The large registry size of the DWave 2000 device allowed
Irie et al. (2019)
to solve
CVRPTW
up to 7 nodes, achieving
80
%
80\%
of generated valid solutions.
Another independent work solved
CVRPTW
using a different encoding of the problem, managing to tackle configurations up to 12 nodes.
The larger qubit count has also been combined with classical techniques; for instance,
Borowski et al. (2020)
decompose the
CVRP
through clustering algorithms into several instances of the Traveling Salesman Problem
(
Laporte, 1992
)
, solved independently using DWave Leap, achieving an optimality gap
(
Nemhauser and Wolsey, 1988
)
of
25.13
%
25.13\%
over a problem instance with
101

nodes and
8
8
vehicles.
A similar approach is used by
Feld et al. (2019)
, in which the
CVRP
instance is decomposed into two distinct optimization problems. The first problem, solved classically, is related to the clustering of the graph and is associated with an instance of the Knapsack Problem
(
Bernhard and Jens, 2008
)
; after this step, a quantum annealer is used to generate the routes on the clustered graph.
The problem has also been approached with
QAOA
solutions, with promising early results. In
Azfar et al. (2026)
, the
VRP
for 3 nodes is successfully solved using IBM Quantum System One, showing that the optimization can be performed even with
Noise Intermediate Scale Quantum (NISQ)
devices.
With
QAOA
, it is also possible to solve constrained problems; a good example is given by
Xie et al. (2024)
, in which the authors use an additional ancilla register together with a mixer that preserves feasibility for solving
CVRP
with 5 nodes.
A different approach is adopted by
Huang et al. (2025)
, which, inspired by classical exact solvers, employs
QAOA
alongside the column generation technique, solving
CVRP
up to 7 nodes.
In this work, we further explore the application of
QAOA
to the
VRP
, contributing mainly in the following aspects. First, we propose an initialization strategy for
QAOA
, aimed at mitigating optimization in ill-conditioned landscapes often present in quantum optimization routines. Then, building on
Montañez-Barrera et al. (2024)
, we introduce an unbalanced penalization term to remove the exponential parameter overhead that is usually required to define the
VRP
in the
QUBO
formalism. To the best of our knowledge, both the proposed initialization strategy and the definition of the subtour
QUBO
term represent a novelty in the context of
QAOA
and
VRP
resolution methods.
The remainder of the article is organized as follows: Section
2
provides a comprehensive introduction to
VRP
and its formalization as a
QUBO

QAOA
. In Section
3.1
we introduce our proposed novel initialization strategy for

. In Section
3.2
, we propose an encoding capable of including the subtour constraint, obtaining a correct solution with high probability; to the best of our knowledge, no other end-to-end

routine in the literature manages to integrate and solve the full
VRP
problem for a generic instance without an overhead in circuit size.
A summary of the results is reported and discussed in Section

3.1
Initialization strategy for QAOA
Combinatorial optimization through
QAOA
enables the application of quantum algorithms to real problems, even with
NISQ
devices, by leveraging its variational structure to learn a more noise-tolerant configuration of the system, capable of dealing with the problem at hand even in the presence of a reasonable amount of noise. However, this comes at the cost of dealing with the limitations of variational training protocols. In particular,
QAOA
suffers from the
barren plateau
phenomenon
(
Anschuetz, 2025
)
, caused both by the large size of the system and by the use of a global expectation value required to estimate the total energy. A direct consequence is an increase in the number of training steps due to an exponentially vanishing gradient of the cost function, which can make training infeasible. Several solutions to this problem have been proposed: in
(
Cerezo et al., 2021a
)
it is shown how a cost function depending only on a local subset of qubits may exhibit better training properties, whereas in
(
Yao and Hasegawa, 2025
)
the authors provide a procedure for converting variational circuits from a unitary 2-design
(
McClean et al., 2018
)
to a unitary 1-design using an additional ancilla register, improving gradient variance at the expense of more qubits; another well-known solution is to train the model layer-wise
(
Skolik et al., 2021
)
, simplifying the cost landscape and improving conditioning, at the expense of a more expensive training procedure scaling with the number of layers.
An alternative class of plateau-mitigation techniques focuses directly on improving the optimization landscape conditioning through parameter initialization heuristics. Trotterized Quantum Annealing
(
Sack and Serbyn, 2021
)
uses physics-inspired adiabatic evolution schedules to systematically determine parameter values. It has also proven effective to transfer optimal parameter from smaller problem into the interested instance
(
Zhou et al., 2020
;
Brandao et al., 2018
)
.

4
Conclusions
VRP
is a sensible topic with tangible repercussions on the economy and society. Therefore, improvements on this field are desirable and can lead to mitigation of costs and environmental preservation. In parallel, quantum computing and quantum optimization represent the frontiers in computation, allowing to tackle some classes of NP-hard problems in a tractable way as the
VRP
without the soubtour constraints. However, ignoring the subtour constraints means that the produced solutions needs to be filtered in post-processing to avoid unfeasible solutions. This post-processing is a computational burden that needs to be considered for practical implementations of classical or quantum optimization implementations to solve
VRP
. The usual approaches to face
VRP
using quantum techniques involve
QA
on quantum annealers or
QAOA
on circuit-based devices.
In this work, we improved the
QAOA
approach to solve
VRP
by introducing two novel techniques in this field. In particular, we successfully implemented a clever initialization of the
QAOA
parameters and we integrated the subtour constraints within the quantum computation without requiring additional qubits and classical post-processing. The proposed initialization strategy does not make any assumptions about the problem instance. It is therefore an effective method to improve the success rate of the training procedure and to mitigate barren plateaus, one of the biggest drawbacks in quantum optimization. Furthermore, compared with the standard slack-variable formulation, the proposed encoding reduces the number of required qubits by
𝒪
⁡
(
2
N
)
\mathcal{O}\left(2^{N}\right)
for instances with
N
N
, while achieving a high probability of sampling.
The presented methods are general in scope. With a different derivation of the inequalities, the unbalanced exponential codification of the constraints can also be applied to other problems, as shown in
(
Montañez-Barrera et al., 2024
)
. This enables a more qubit-efficient solution of optimization problems on quantum computers, at the expense of additional hyper-parameters that have to be tuned.
At best of our knowledge, we were the first to achieve an effective initialization of the
QAOA
parameters within the
VRP
context that is independent on the problem size. Moreover, we were the first to adopt the encoding of the subtour inequalities of
VRP
directly into the
QAOA
optimization.
We validated our contributes on different classes of
VRP
and showed how they can be used effectively in this context. In particular, we reported that our proposed parameters initialization is statistically better than a random initialization of the parameters and that our integration of the subtour constraint allows to find the correct optimal solutions of
VRP
for different classes of problems with
N
N
from 3 to 5.
Some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Due to computational demand, the experiments were restricted to small instances and carried out on noiseless simulators, so the effect of noise and decoherence on the proposed encoding has not been fully characterized.
Moreover, conversion into
QUBO
formalism of the constraints is highly sensible to the choice of the hyper-parameters, which determines whether the optimal feasible solution corresponds to the ground state of the resulting Hamiltonian.
Given the highly structured nature of combinatorial problems such as the
VRP
, it is plausible that these hyper-parameters can be determined through a metaheuristic based on problem invariants. Future works may therefore focus on finding hyper-parameter configurations that are either general or dependent on the instance class (i.e., as a function of
N
N
and
K
K
). Such configurations could serve as a starting point for the hyper-parameter search, which represents the bottleneck of any
QAOA
approach requiring a conversion of the original problem to
QUBO
.
Despite being limited in problem size by current
NISQ
restrictions, which also prevent seamless integration of inequality constraints, this work shows that the required qubit-register size can be reduced by engineering such constraints, improving the efficiency of the
QUBO
conversion. This paves the way for future implementations of
VRP
on fault-tolerant quantum computers, which may become a viable alternative or complement to classical routing methods. More broadly, we expect careful constraint engineering to remain a key ingredient in making quantum optimization competitive for industrially relevant logistics problems.
