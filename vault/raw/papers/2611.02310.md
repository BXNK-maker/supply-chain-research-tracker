Abstract.
Accurate electricity-demand forecasting is essential for the modern energy infrastructure. Recently, Times-Series Large Language Models (TS-LLMs) have emerged as a promising approach to forecasting more accurate demand from historical consumption data in zero- and few-shot settings, helping energy operators effectively plan supply and allocate energy resources.
However, these models are vulnerable to compromised sensor inputs that can distort forecasts and lead to erroneous market orders or unnecessary load shedding. Existing defenses focus primarily on end-device forecasting, overlooking the distinct data-drift, latency, and audit requirements across energy infrastructure. To address this gap, we extract from major energy standards and regulatory frameworks a three-layer conceptual model that captures the security postures for each layer. Guided by this model, we develop a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) detector coupled with signal repair at local control centers, designed to mitigate distributional data drift through complementary feature-based detection and clean-reference-calibrated repair while meeting latency budgets. We further deploy an evidence-grounded LLM auditing agent with human-in-the-loop validation for audit-critical Virtual Power Plants (VPPs). In our evaluation, the best MARL configuration achieves an F1 score above 0.91 across all dataset-attack pairs, while signal repair reduces adversarial-to-clean input Relative Squared Error (RSE) by up to 83%. The few-shot auditor detects 77.78% of attacks in the Sherlock dataset. On the evaluated hardware, detection and repair require 7.95 ms on average, with a worst-observed latency of 14.50 ms, remaining within the local-center latency budget informed by IEEE 1646.
1.
Introduction
With the rapid proliferation of AI, energy has emerged as a critical foundation for AI infrastructure, powering both the computational resources used to train large language models (LLMs) and the cloud infrastructure that delivers AI inference services to end users
(
Chen et al., 2025
;
Datacenters.com Cloud, 2025
)
. Leading cloud providers are now securing direct nuclear supply agreements
(
Morales, 2026
)
and co-locating data centers adjacent to generation assets
(
Room, 2025
;
Levy, 2025
)
. Within this infrastructure, electricity-demand forecasts derived from historical consumption data inform key decisions on supply scheduling, resource allocation, and market operations
(
Ma et al., 2025
)
. This growing reliance on energy infrastructure makes its security inseparable from AI service continuity. However, the need to protect this infrastructure is still underscored by real-world incidents. Industroyer malware exploited Operational Technology (OT) protocol automation to trigger the 2016 blackout
(
Abraham et al., 2025
)
, and federal agencies recently warned that state-sponsored actors are actively exploiting programmable logic controllers at US energy and water facilities
(
Nickel and Miller, 2026
)
. To model defenses across this heterogeneous infrastructure, we synthesize major energy standards and regulatory frameworks covering device communication, grid cybersecurity, and distributed resource aggregation
(
Etherden et al., 2016
;
Committee, 2014
;
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, 2021
)
. We derive from them a generalized three-layer model (Figure
1
): resource-constrained
end devices (L1)
, latency-sensitive
local centers (L2)
, and audit-critical
Virtual Power Plants (VPPs, L3)
, with distinct computational, latency, and audit requirements (as detailed in §
3
).
Traditional energy-demand forecasting relies on historical patterns (
e.g.,
daily activity cycles and seasonal variations), while Time-Series Large Language Models (TS-LLMs)
(
Das et al., 2024
;
Jin et al., 2024
;
Garza et al., 2023
)
leverage pre-trained representations for zero- and few-shot forecasting from historical data, making them a more promising demand forecasters at local control centers. However these capabilities do not guarantee robustness to
distributional data drift
: weather or behavioral changes (
e.g.,
heatwaves increasing cooling demand) can shift demand away from historical distributions and reduce forecast accuracy. Beyond such benign changes, these models are vulnerable to adversarial perturbations
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
, including black-box attacks that degrade forecasts using only input-output queries without parameter access
(
Liu et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. Yet existing defenses address only a fragment of this threat landscape. Specifically, adversarial training
(
Goodfellow et al., 2015
)
can be costly for large TS-LLMs and overfit known attack distributions
(
Rice et al., 2020
;
Yu et al., 2022
)
, while randomized smoothing
(
Cohen et al., 2019
)
provides guarantees under specific perturbation models that may not capture temporal attacks on energy data. Anomaly-detection classifiers (
e.g.,
Isolation Forest
(
Liu et al., 2008
)
, OC-SVM
(
Schölkopf et al., 2001
)
) and signal filters (
e.g.,
Kalman smoother
(
Kalman, 1960
)
, Savitzky-Golay
(
Savitzky and Golay, 1964
)
) must also accommodate benign distribution shifts
(
Fenza et al., 2019
;
Lu et al., 2019
)
rather than misclassifying or suppressing legitimate changes. Detection-only pipelines that discard suspicious inputs can interrupt forecast continuity, making signal repair an operational necessity. Meanwhile, detection and repair must also share the latency budget with message transmission when performed within control-message delivery paths subject to IEEE 1646’s 16 ms limit
(
IEEE, 2005
)
(§
3
). Beyond forecasting, VPPs require cross-device OT-log investigation, but manual auditing faces substantial evidence-preparation burdens and gaps in auditors’ OT-domain knowledge
(
Sahin et al., 2025
)
. Together, these gaps motivate adversarial detection and signal repair that accommodate benign distributional drift within latency constraints, complemented by evidence-grounded auditing at VPPs.
We address these gaps by modeling the energy infrastructure as three layers (§
3
) and developing a hierarchical defense that combines a cooperative
Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) detector with Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)-based signal repair
at local control centers (L2) with an evidence-grounded
LLM auditing agent
at VPPs (L3) (§
4
). We take distributional data drift into consideration through two complementary mechanisms. First, the MARL specialists combine evidence from six specialists, allowing the coordinator to assess a load change in context. Training across diverse temporal patterns and conditioning decisions on a rolling trusted context support context-responsive detection (§
4.1
). Second, for rejected inputs, a
Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)-based repair module
retrieves a similar clean historical window from the same user and calibrates wavelet soft-thresholds per window. Regarding latency, our experiments show that the full detection-and-repair pipeline achieves a mean latency of 7.95 ms and a worst observed latency of 14.50 ms on the evaluated hardware, both below the local-center budget (§
5.6
). At VPPs, the auditing agent correlates OT logs across devices to identify attacks and produces reports grounded in specific log entries for human-in-the-loop (HITL) validation (§
4.3
).
In summary, our contributions are threefold:
•
We systematically model energy infrastructure as three operational layers (
i.e.,
end devices, local control centers, and VPPs) and formalize the threat model for each layer, identifying its data streams, attack surfaces, attacker objectives, and operational constraints (§
3
).
•
We develop a hierarchical defense framework combining cooperative MARL detection and DWT-based signal repair at local control centers with evidence-grounded LLM auditing and HITL validation at VPPs, addressing distinct latency and audit requirements while preserving forecast continuity (§
4
).
•
We evaluate local-center defense across three energy datasets under DGA
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
and GenIAS
(
Darban et al., 2025
)
, and separately assess forensic auditing on Sherlock
(
Wagner et al., 2025
)
benchmark. The best MARL configuration achieves F1 above 0.91 on every dataset–attack pair, DWT repair reduces adversarial-to-clean input RSE by up to 83%, and the few-shot auditing agent detects 77.78% of attack windows. On the evaluated hardware, detection and repair take 7.95 ms on average (14.50 ms worst observed), below the local-center budget (§
5
).

Related Work
In this section, we review (i) time-series forecasting models and input-side adversarial threats, and (ii) forecast-integrity defenses and audit-governance challenges in energy infrastructure.
2.1.
Time-Series Forecasters and Attacks
Forecasting models.
Time-series forecasting has progressed from statistical methods (
e.g.,
ARIMA and exponential smoothing) to neural models (
e.g.,
DeepAR
(
Salinas et al., 2020
)
and LSTNet
(
Lai et al., 2018
)
) and Transformers (
e.g.,
Informer
(
Zhou et al., 2021
)
and PatchTST
(
Nie et al., 2023
)
). More recently, TS-LLMs offer zero- and few-shot forecasting capabilities. In this work, we study TimeGPT
(
Garza et al., 2023
)
, TimesFM
(
Das et al., 2024
)
, and TimeLLM
(
Jin et al., 2024
)
to cover three distinct designs: cross-domain time-series pretraining, decoder-only forecasting, and language-model reprogramming. However, forecasting alone does not ensure robustness to adversarial inputs
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
.
Input-side threats.
Gradient-based attacks (
e.g.,
FGSM/BIM
(
Goodfellow et al., 2015
)
and PGD
(
Madry et al., 2017
)
) have been extended to time-series classification
(
Oregi et al., 2018
)
and graph-based forecasting
(
Liu et al., 2022
)
. DGA
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
directly targets LLM-based forecasters through gradient-free model queries, producing misleading predictions with small input perturbations. GenIAS
(
Darban et al., 2025
)
, originally developed for anomaly generation, synthesizes realistic anomalous sequences through latent-space perturbations. These methods perturb time-series inputs; in our setting, corrupting the edge meter’s readings can therefore bias local-center forecasts and lead to incorrect VPP market decisions, motivating protection beyond the forecasting model alone.
2.2.
Defenses for Secure Energy Infrastructure
Forecast integrity.
Ma
et al.
(
Ma et al., 2025
)
detect adversarial TS-LLM inputs through
sampling-induced divergence
, checking forecast consistency across shortened input variants. TimeGPT
(
Garza et al., 2023
)
offers native anomaly detection, with its historical interface flagging observations outside prediction-based intervals. These detection mechanisms provide anomaly signals but do not specify how to repair adversarially perturbed inputs for downstream forecasting.
Audit governance.
Sahin
et al.
(
Sahin et al., 2025
)
study NERC-CIP compliance in the US electric power sector, identifying heavy evidence-preparation burdens, gaps in auditors’ OT-domain knowledge, and a one-size-fits-all audit process. Their study characterizes governance and audit-practice challenges rather than an algorithmic mechanism for detecting OT attacks from device logs. These findings motivate evidence-grounded forensic assistance with human validation.
Cross-layer defense gap.
These works address complementary security needs but do not jointly provide detection, input recovery, and forensic auditing under distributional data drift and layer-specific operational constraints. Our framework combines cooperative MARL detection with DWT-based repair at local control centers to preserve forecast continuity within their latency budgets, alongside evidence-grounded LLM auditing agent and human validation at VPPs.
3.
Threat Model
Figure 1.
Three-layer energy infrastructure model: entities, cross-layer interactions and issues.
This section formalizes the three-layer energy infrastructure model and analyzes the entities, data streams, attack surfaces, and attacker objectives at each layer. It then specifies the attacker capabilities relevant to our forecasting defense and log auditing, as well as the operational constraints and security needs of the infrastructure.
Energy infrastructure modeling and attacker’s goal.
Energy systems do not prescribe a fixed number of operational tiers, yet a recurring three-layer pattern emerges across major standards and regulatory frameworks: IEC 61850 separates field devices from bay-level and station-level control
(
Etherden et al., 2016
)
; NIST’s smart-grid cybersecurity guidelines stratify assets by communication domain and trust boundary
(
Committee, 2014
)
; and FERC Order 2222 formalizes the aggregation of distributed energy resources under VPP-like entities that interface with wholesale markets
(
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, 2021
)
. We extract from these frameworks a three-layer conceptual model that captures the hardware, latency, and governance heterogeneity driving distinct security postures. Alternative architectures such as microgrids or community energy systems map onto the same three tiers. Figure
1
illustrates the entities, cross-layer interactions, and issues in this model. Telemetry flows from end devices to local centers for forecasting, and the forecasts inform VPP market and dispatch decisions.
Each layer handles distinct data streams, exposes a distinct attack surface, and admits a distinct attacker objective:
•
L1: End devices.
Resource-constrained Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) connected smart meters and IoT sensors reporting raw time-series telemetry.
Attacker’s Goal:
Inject perturbed readings to induce downstream forecast deviations large enough to move outside operational tolerance bands.
•
L2: Local centers.
Latency-sensitive control systems (
e.g.,
Battery Control System, Energy Management System) that aggregate upstream telemetry and run TS-LLM demand forecasters.
Attacker’s Goal:
At this layer, the attacker similarly seeks to distort forecasts through input manipulation, but targets the broader telemetry used for local-center forecasting and operational assessment rather than individual meter readings at L1. To evade detection, the attacker may inject smaller or operationally plausible perturbations, including gradual distribution shifts, to bias downstream market-bidding or battery-dispatch decisions.
•
L3: Virtual power plants (VPPs):
Market-facing entities that aggregate site-level forecasts, Operational Technology (OT) logs, alarms, and balancing orders.
Attacker’s Goal:
Flood audit pipelines, forge logs, or execute OT-level command manipulation (
e.g.,
Industroyer-class attacks
(
Abraham et al., 2025
)
) to delay attribution and trigger incorrect market actions.
Attacker’s knowledge and capability.
(1)
Black-box access:
At the L1 and L2 layers, the attacker can query the operational TS-LLM forecaster but has no visibility into its parameters, gradients, or clean training data. Adversarial inputs are crafted via gradient-free or generative optimization
(
Liu et al., 2025
;
Darban et al., 2025
)
(evaluated in §
5
).
(2)
Partial compromise:
The attacker controls or impersonates a subset of telemetry or OT communication endpoints (
e.g.,
smart meters) but does not obtain administrative control of the forecasting pipeline or the VPP itself.
(3)
Defense knowledge:
The attacker is aware of the defense mechanism existence and algorithmic logic, but does not know the specific calibrated parameters.
Design requirements.
The defender cannot modify the frozen architecture or weights of the pre-trained TS-LLM, nor does it have access to online ground-truth labels. When scalar adversarial probabilities are required (
e.g.,
for the decision-tree mechanism in §
4.1
), an independent and lightweight projection layer is appended.
This head serves as an external defense wrapper rather than a model modification.
The following design requirements reflect the layer-specific constraints and auditing needs summarized in Figure
1
:
•
R1 (End devices): Compute constraints.
Resource constrained AMI hardware limits on-device computation. Since all L1 telemetries flow through L2 and the two layers share similar input-manipulation threat, we centralize detection and repair at L2 to address attacks originating at either layer.
•
R2 (Local centers): Latency & distributional data drift.
The defense must operate online within strict latency budgets (
i.e.,
16ms under IEEE 1646
(
IEEE, 2005
)
) and accommodate benign changes in energy-data distributions over time (
e.g.,
changes in renewable generation patterns
(
Fenza et al., 2019
;
Lu et al., 2019
)
), requiring both detection and immediate signal repair to preserve forecast continuity.
•
R3 (VPPs): Scalable forensic auditing.
Large OT log volumes and persistent domain knowledge gaps among auditors
(
Sahin et al., 2025
)
require efficient, evidence-grounded assistance for human forensic auditing. The auditing agent should automatically correlate OT logs across devices, identify suspicious events, and organize supporting evidence to reduce preparation effort and help auditors rapidly investigate attacks.
4.
Defense Strategies
This section presents MARL detection and DWT-based repair at local control centers (L2), LLM-assisted forensic auditing at VPPs (L3), and the cross-layer orchestration of our framework.
Building on our model and operational requirements in §
3
, we first describe how cooperative MARL detection (§
4.1
) and DWT-based signal repair (§
4.2
) work together to protect forecast integrity and preserve forecast continuity under local-center latency constraints.
We then present an evidence-grounded LLM forensic auditing agent designed to help human auditors efficiently investigate potential OT attacks (§
4.3
).
Finally, we define the data flow and evidence contract connecting end-device telemetry, local-center detection and repair decisions, and downstream VPP operations (§
4.4
).

4.3.
LLM Forensic Auditing Agent
To accelerate forensic auditing and support accurate investigation of potential OT attacks at VPPs, we deploy an LLM-based forensic auditing agent that operates over device error logs from the target infrastructure. The agent’s workflow consists of four stages:
•
Input ingestion.
The agent receives an accumulated OT-log window
ℒ
\mathcal{L}
, together with a run-time candidate-attack set
𝒜
\mathcal{A}
describing the attack types relevant to the deployment.
In the Sherlock evaluation, this set contains Industroyer, Drift-Off, Control & Freeze, and ARP Spoofing DoS.
•
Reasoning and correlation.
The agent analyzes log entries across devices and time windows, correlating anomalous patterns (e.g., repeated OT command sequences, measurement discontinuities, and communication disruptions) to reconstruct candidate attack narratives.
Attack hypotheses are restricted to types in
𝒜
\mathcal{A}
.
•
Evidence-grounded reporting.
To mitigate hallucinations and unsupported claims, every anomaly flag must be grounded in specific log entries, including timestamps, device identifiers, and log content.
Each proposed attack hypothesis must be supported by this cited evidence.
•
Escalation output.
The agent produces a structured report containing the supported attack hypothesis, log evidence, affected assets, and a recommended action. It returns an empty anomaly list if the logs support no candidate type.
The agent operates as a forensic filter within a human-in-the-loop (HITL) system: it surfaces potential threats with supporting evidence and recommends escalation, but cannot autonomously execute remediation actions. Final validation and control-action authority remain with human auditors. This design is intended to reduce the evidence-preparation burden and help bridge the OT-domain knowledge gap identified by Sahin et al.
4.4.
Cross-Layer Orchestration and Evidence Contract
The components described above are connected through a cross-layer data flow (as shown in Figure
1
) and a common evidence contract that records their processing decisions and downstream consequences. End devices (L1) forward telemetry to local centers (L2), where the MARL detector screens each input window. Accepted windows are used directly for forecasting, whereas rejected windows undergo DWT-based repair when permitted by the reference-confidence gate. If the gate abstains, the system uses a persistence forecast to preserve forecast continuity; the same fallback is used across all ablations. The resulting forecast is then forwarded to the VPP (L3), where it informs the net-load bid and subsequent operational decisions.
To make this process traceable, each input window is assigned an event identifier and a hash of the received values. At L2, the record is extended with the six specialist confidences, local actions, coordinator action, and decision-tree depth. Repair-related fields include the selected clean-reference identifier and distance, DWT parameters, repaired-signal hash, and before/after forecasts, where applicable. At L3, the VPP appends the forecast-derived net-load bid and any operator action. These records are serialized as timestamped entries in the VPP audit log.
All entries associated with the same telemetry window retain the same event identifier. This linkage allows operators to trace an L1 observation through its L2 detection and repair decisions to its recorded L3 consequences, without requiring access to complete raw user histories for record association. The evidence contract therefore specifies how cross-layer records are organized and linked, while the forensic-auditing workflow in §
4.3
describes how OT logs are analyzed to support human investigation and validation.

Adapting the four per-level thresholds increases the RSE reduction to 63.4% without gating, while reducing clean distortion to 1.3% and harm to 5.4%. Adding the confidence gate sacrifices only 1.6 percentage points of RSE reduction (63.4% to 61.8%) and adds 0.03 ms of latency (2.32 ms to 2.35 ms), but reduces clean distortion from 1.3% to 0.6% and harm from 5.4% to 2.1%. Thus, per-window threshold adaptation provides the principal repair gain, while confidence gating substantially limits repair-induced damage on unreliable references and false-positive detector outputs.
Reference-pool robustness.
Table
5
examines sensitivity to reference-pool size, history relation, and operating condition. For gated DWT, repair coverage is the fraction of detector rejections passing the reference-confidence gate. Under same-user, in-season matching, increasing the historical pool from 100 to 1K and 10K windows raises RSE reduction from 50.9% to 58.7% and 61.8%, while repair coverage increases from 86.0% to 91.7% and 94.2%. Over the same progression, clean distortion decreases from 1.1% to 0.8% and 0.6%, and harm falls from 4.6% to 3.0% and 2.1%. This improvement incurs a moderate latency increase from 1.71 ms to 1.98 ms and 2.35 ms. Holding the pool size at 10K isolates reference mismatch: cross-user matching reduces RSE reduction to 45.6%, increases harm to 6.8%, and lowers coverage to 78.4%; seasonal shift further reduces RSE reduction to 42.8%, with 8.4% harm and 72.6% coverage; and rare-event windows are the most difficult non-missing-history condition, yielding 29.4% RSE reduction, 9.7% harm, and 55.1% coverage. Their nearly identical latencies (2.34–2.36 ms) show that these degradations arise from reference mismatch rather than additional computation. When no historical reference is available, the gate performs no DWT repair and invokes the common fallback, producing 0.0% repair coverage, RSE reduction, clean distortion, and repair-induced harm at 0.08 ms. These results expose the method’s dependence on representative same-user history while showing that the confidence gate reduces coverage instead of forcing repair with an unreliable reference.

Conclusion
This work models energy infrastructure as three operational layers and presents a constraint-driven framework combining cooperative MARL detection and DWT-based signal repair at local centers with evidence-grounded LLM forensic auditing at VPPs. A common cross-layer record supports traceability, while the framework targets forecast continuity under distributional data drift and assists human forensic investigation. Across three energy datasets under DGA and GenIAS, the best MARL configuration achieves F1 above 0.91 on every dataset–attack pair and repair reduces adversarial-to-clean input RSE by up to 83%; the few-shot auditor detects 77.78% of attack windows in the separate Sherlock evaluation, including all seven evaluated Industroyer-class incidents. On the evaluated hardware, detection and repair take 7.95 ms on average (14.50 ms worst observed), below the local-center budget.
