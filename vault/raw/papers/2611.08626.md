Abstract
E-commerce supply chains generate large volumes of customer feedback and operational data that remain underutilized in inventory management. Traditional inventory classification techniques such as ABC analysis rely primarily on annual dollar usage, while multi-criteria inventory classification (MCIC) methods extend this but still depend on structured quantitative data and do not utilize unstructured information available in modern supply chains. This paper proposes an LLM-based feature extraction approach that transforms unstructured sources such as customer reviews into quantitative features using vector embeddings and LLM-generated evaluation criteria. The enriched feature set is incorporated into an MCIC framework based on the Hadi-Vencheh model. Experimental results show measurable reclassification effects when customer sentiment signals are included, demonstrating that integrating unstructured data into MCIC can enhance inventory prioritization.
1
Introduction
Inventory classification is a central task in supply chain management, particularly in e-commerce contexts where large product catalogs must be prioritized under uncertain demand, variable delivery performance, and high customer sensitivity to service quality. Traditional ABC analysis remains widely used because of its simplicity and interpretability. It classifies inventory items into categories A, B, and C according to annual dollar usage, thereby allowing firms to concentrate resources on high-value items
[
1
,
2
]
. However, reliance on annual dollar usage alone can overlook operational and market-related signals such as lead time, commonality, criticality, durability, and customer-perceived service quality.
Multi-criteria inventory classification (MCIC) addresses this limitation by incorporating additional attributes into the classification process. Prior work has proposed several approaches, including cross-tabulation, clustering, analytic hierarchy process, genetic algorithms, and artificial-intelligence-based classification methods
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. These methods improve upon traditional ABC analysis by considering multiple structured attributes. Nevertheless, most MCIC approaches still depend on quantitative data that are already available in structured enterprise systems.
At the same time, modern supply chains generate large volumes of unstructured data, including customer reviews, supplier reports, service notes, quality records, messages, and, increasingly, multimodal evidence such as images or videos. Such data may contain signals about customer satisfaction, delivery reliability, product quality, and perceived risk. Recent advances in LLMs and embedding models provide a practical way to
transform unstructured evidence into machine-readable representations
[
14
]
. This creates an opportunity to extend MCIC models with qualitative features derived from unstructured data.
This paper contributes an LLM-based feature extraction framework for AI-augmented MCIC, integrating LLM-derived features into the Hadi-Vencheh model
[
13
]
and evaluating the approach on a public e-commerce dataset with customer reviews, order, and delivery data.

4
Experimental Evaluation
The empirical evaluation uses the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist
[
15
]
, containing over 100,000 anonymized orders with product, order, delivery, and review information.
Three classification scenarios are evaluated. First, traditional ABC analysis is performed using annual dollar usage as the sole criterion. This produces a baseline distribution of 16.0% Class A items, 28.6% Class B items, and 55.4% Class C items, consistent with the Pareto principle. Second, a conventional MCIC model is applied using annual dollar usage (ADU), average unit cost (AUC), and lead time (LT). Third, an AI-augmented MCIC model (AMCIC) is evaluated by adding a perceived delivery experience (PDE) feature extracted from customer reviews.
The PDE feature is operationalized as a scalar criterion derived from review retrieval and criterion-based scoring. It complements objective lead time by capturing the subjective customer impact of delivery performance. Negative delivery experiences receive higher scores because they indicate greater potential need for managerial attention, while consistently positive experiences receive lower scores.
To quantify the effect of reclassification, transition matrices were generated comparing class changes between traditional ABC and MCIC, between MCIC and AMCIC, and between traditional ABC and AMCIC. The matrices indicate whether items are retained in the same class, moved to a higher-priority class, or moved to a lower-priority class.

The results show that LLM-derived features provide interpretable refinements to inventory classification. Items promoted under AMCIC may represent cases where structured metrics understate service risk, while downward movements may indicate low perceived delivery risk despite strong quantitative profiles. The approach therefore introduces an additional signal for differentiated monitoring, replenishment review, and managerial prioritization. These results demonstrate reclassification effects rather than validated improvements in downstream operational performance; whether shifts translate to better service levels or lower holding costs requires validation against operational ground truth and is a direction for future work.
5
Conclusion and Future Research
This paper presented an LLM-based feature extraction framework for enriching multi-criteria inventory classification with information derived from unstructured operational data. We identified three limitations of existing approaches: the reliance of traditional ABC analysis on annual dollar usage as a sole criterion, the dependence of conventional MCIC methods on structured quantitative attributes, and the underutilization of unstructured data available in modern supply chains. The proposed framework addresses all three. It moves beyond single-criterion ABC analysis by incorporating multiple criteria into a weighted classification model. It extends conventional MCIC by introducing LLM-derived qualitative features alongside structured attributes and it operationalizes unstructured customer feedback as a quantitative inventory signal, demonstrating that such data can meaningfully influence item prioritization.
Further work should validate whether reclassification translates into measurable operational gains by testing against datasets with observable ground-truth outcomes such as service level, stockout rate, and holding cost. A natural extension is to couple classification with demand forecasting models, where LLM-derived sentiment features may improve accuracy for high-risk items. Robustness checks and bias detection for LLM-generated scores remain important requirements for practical deployment.
