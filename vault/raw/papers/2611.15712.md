Abstract
The rapid growth of cloud computing, artificial intelligence (AI), and high-performance computing is driving the deployment of data centers with electrical demands ranging from tens of megawatts to multi-gigawatt campuses, creating significant challenges for utility interconnection and power system planning. This paper presents scalable, vendor-neutral reference electrical architectures for colocation, hyperscale, and AI data centers. The proposed framework integrates modular Power PODs, hierarchical HV/MV/LV distribution, standby generation, uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), battery energy storage systems (BESS), and scalable utility interfaces into a unified planning methodology. Three representative Power POD configurations are developed to support systematic capacity expansion while maintaining consistent electrical topology and equipment sizing. The proposed architectures facilitate utility interconnection studies, equipment demand forecasting, transmission planning, and grid infrastructure assessment, providing an open and reproducible benchmark for evaluating the impacts of large-scale data center deployment on future power systems.

I
Introduction
Modern data centers have evolved from enterprise facilities with electrical capacities of only a few megawatts into hyperscale and AI campuses requiring hundreds of megawatts, with multiple announced developments approaching or exceeding the gigawatt scale
[
1
]
. This unprecedented growth has made
data centers one of the fastest-growing classes of large electrical loads,
introducing significant challenges for electric utilities, transmission owners,
system operators, regulators, and equipment manufacturers
[
2
]
. This has also intensified concerns
regarding the availability of critical grid-interconnection equipment
[
3
]
. Long
manufacturing lead times for large power transformers, gas-insulated
switchgear, medium-voltage switchgear, standby generators, and other electrical
equipment have become significant constraints on both utility expansion and
data-center deployment
[
4
]
. Consequently, planning
studies increasingly require not only accurate forecasts of electrical demand
but also reproducible methodologies for estimating the associated electrical
infrastructure required to support projected data-center growth
[
5
]
.
Electrical infrastructure architectures (reference designs) published by industrial organizations have
become widely adopted during the conceptual and detailed design of modern data
centers. Guidance documents from Schneider Electric
[
6
]
, Vertiv
[
7
]
, NVIDIA
[
8
]
, the
Uptime Institute
[
9
]
, and the ISO/IEC 22237 series
[
10
]
provide valuable recommendations
regarding power distribution topologies, redundancy philosophies, modular
deployment strategies, and facility reliability. These
references have significantly improved engineering practice by standardizing
many aspects of facility design. However, these documents are primarily intended for engineering implementation
and frequently emphasize vendor-specific solutions or reference installations.
They generally do not provide an open, technology-neutral methodology that can
be directly incorporated into transmission planning studies, utility
interconnection analyses, infrastructure forecasting, or reproducible academic
research.
From the power-system perspective, the rapid growth of hyperscale and AI data centers has emerged as a major planning challenge, driving extensive research on transmission expansion, resource adequacy, voltage stability, congestion management, dynamic performance, and large-load interconnection studies
[
2
,
4
]
. Utilities, independent system operators, and regional transmission organizations have recognized that future planning requires more detailed representations of data-center electrical infrastructure to accurately assess grid impacts, equipment requirements, and utility interconnection strategies. However, most existing power-system studies represent data centers as aggregated constant-power loads or simplified demand profiles for steady-state and planning analyses, without explicitly modeling the underlying electrical infrastructure
[
11
]
. Such simplified representations are often inadequate for capturing the dynamic behavior and grid interactions of modern data centers, motivating the development of physics-based electrical models for planning and stability studies
[
12
,
13
]
. Furthermore, available reference electrical architectures are largely proprietary or vendor-specific, limiting transparency, reproducibility, and their applicability to academic research and utility planning
[
6
,
7
]
. Consequently, there remains no standardized, scalable, and vendor-neutral electrical architecture that bridges facility-level design with grid level studies.
To address these challenges, this paper presents a scalable, vendor-neutral reference electrical architecture for modern data centers that bridges facility-level electrical design with utility, transmission, and distribution planning. In this paper, ‘vendor-neutral’ denotes a modular, technology-agnostic planning methodology that uses functional categories and standardized equipment sizing and topology, ensuring direct applicability across different manufacturers and open compatibility with industry standards. The proposed framework integrates utility substations, HV/MV/LV distribution, modular power POD, standby generation, UPS, BESS, and IT infrastructure into a unified architecture suitable for colocation, hyperscale, and AI data centers. Based on established industry standards and reference designs
[
10
,
14
,
6
,
9
]
, the framework provides a reproducible benchmark for power-system planning, utility interconnection studies, and infrastructure expansion. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
•
A scalable and vendor-neutral reference electrical architecture for colocation, hyperscale, and AI data centers that supports both facility design and grid planning.
•
A modular power POD methodology with standardized HV, MV, and LV electrical topologies enables consistent capacity expansion and utility interconnection studies.
•
A reproducible planning framework for evaluating electrical infrastructure requirements, equipment demand, and future transmission and distribution planning associated with large-scale data center deployment.
The remainder of this paper describes the proposed reference architectures, the design methodology, representative planning applications, and the concluding remarks.

II-B
HV, MV, and LV Distribution Layers
At the MV layer, switchgear, bus sections, feeders, and protection devices
distribute power to individual PODs. Dedicated POD feeders allow selective
isolation and phased construction, while sectionalized buses and alternate
sources can provide maintenance flexibility. The specific MV voltage and feeder
topology are selected based on developer practice, facility capacity, voltage drop, and equipment availability.
The MV system distributes power through dedicated feeders to independent power
PODs. Dual-corded IT equipment can be supplied from independent
paths where the required reliability objective warrants it. However, redundancy
must be assessed for the entire end-to-end topology rather than inferred from a
single duplicated component
[

10
]
.
II-C
Modular Power-POD Architecture
The power POD serves as the fundamental modular and repeatable building block of modern data center electrical architectures. Major infrastructure vendors, including Schneider Electric and Vertiv, adopt POD-based electrical designs that enable standardized deployment, modular expansion, and scalable capacity growth, while hyperscale operators similarly organize facilities into repeatable capacity blocks for incremental expansion
[
17
,
18
]
. It supplies a defined portion of IT capacity together with the
corresponding cooling and auxiliary demand. A representative POD shown in Fig.
2
includes an
MV/LV transformer, LV switchboard, ATS, standby generator, UPS system, battery
bank, PDU or busway system, and downstream rack distribution.
During normal operation, the preferred utility source supplies the POD through
the MV/LV transformer and LV switchboard. The UPS conditions power to critical
IT loads and provides ride-through using its battery system during short-duration
voltage disturbances or source interruptions. If the disturbance persists, the
standby generator starts and the transfer system connects the supported bus to
the emergency source. The topology therefore distinguishes instantaneous
ride-through from longer-duration standby generation, consistent with common
high-availability data-center practice
[
9
,
19
,
20
]
.

The reference architecture separates IT, cooling, and auxiliary loads, enabling explicit PUE modeling and flexibility in cooling technologies. This is particularly important for AI/HPC facilities, where high-density computing tightly integrates power and thermal infrastructure
[

. Cooling, LV distribution, and auxiliary requirements are therefore tailored to colocation, hyperscale, and AI data centers. Detailed component design and assumptions are discussed in the following section.

V
Conclusions and Future Work
This paper presented a scalable, vendor-neutral reference electrical architecture for modern colocation, hyperscale, and AI data centers. The proposed framework combines a hierarchical electrical distribution architecture with modular power PODs and capacity-based planning rules to estimate facility electrical infrastructure and utility interconnection requirements. By explicitly representing substations, transformers, switchgear, standby generation, battery energy storage, and reactive power support, the methodology provides a reproducible benchmark for equipment-demand estimation, utility interconnection studies, and long-term transmission and distribution planning. The modular and hierarchical architecture enables consistent representation of facilities ranging from tens of megawatts to multi-gigawatt campuses while remaining adaptable to different redundancy philosophies, utility standards, and regional design practices.
Future work will extend the benchmark by incorporating time-varying workload models, detailed dynamic models of on-site energy resources and microgrid controllers, and integrated transmission–distribution representations for evaluating large-load interconnections and grid impacts.
