Abstract
Rural last-mile logistics remains challenging because of sparse demand, limited road infrastructure, and long travel distances. We study a Truck-as-Hub Vehicle Routing Problem with Drones in Rural Areas, in which multiple trucks each carry a dedicated fleet of drones. Drone launch and retrieval are restricted to predesignated passive synchronization pads, where a visiting truck temporarily acts as a mobile hub, may dispatch multiple drones simultaneously, and must wait until all launched drones return before continuing its route. We formulate a mixed-integer quadratic programming model that jointly accounts for customer delivery delays, truck return times, and operating costs. To solve the problem exactly, we transform the original truck–drone network so that each feasible route can be represented as a single path and develop a branch-price-and-cut algorithm with tailored pricing, cutting, branching, and parallel-computing procedures. Computational experiments show that the proposed algorithm proves optimality for most large-scale test cases and achieves optimality gaps of no more than 4% for the remaining cases, whereas Gurobi reaches the 1,800-second time limit on all large-scale instances when applied directly to the compact formulation. Sensitivity analyses show that drone operating costs and managerial priorities substantially affect drone utilization and customer delays, providing practical guidance for balancing service quality and operating cost in rural delivery systems.
Keywords:
rural last-mile delivery; vehicle routing problem; drone logistics; branch-price-and-cut; parallel computing.

Introduction
Rural last-mile delivery is particularly challenging due to sparse demand, limited road accessibility, and long travel distances. Trucks offer substantial range and payload capacity, while drones can extend service coverage to hard-to-reach locations but are constrained by limited payload and battery endurance. Hybrid truck–drone systems leverage these complementary strengths: trucks provide long-haul transportation and serve as mobile drone carriers, while drones handle selected short-range deliveries
(
Murray and Chu 2015
,
Agatz et al. 2018
)
.
One key challenge in truck–drone delivery lies in coordinating the two vehicle types. In many formulations, a drone departs from its companion truck, flies to a customer to deposit a package, and then returns to the truck for battery replacement or recharging before its next flight. Meanwhile, the truck continues ground deliveries, proceeding to the next scheduled rendezvous point where the drone will rejoin it
(
Ha et al. 2018
,
Carlsson and Song 2018
)
.
This hybrid system has been formalized in two related problems: the
Flying Sidekick Traveling Salesman Problem
(FSTSP)
(
Murray and Chu 2015
)
, which models drone missions as sorties, and the
Traveling Salesman Problem with Drone
(TSP-D)
(
Agatz et al. 2018
)
, which treats each drone trip as an operation.
In the FSTSP, a drone may launch from and rejoin the truck at different locations
(
Murray and Chu 2015
)
, with subsequent work considering alternative objectives such as operating cost
(
Ha et al. 2018
)
. In the TSP-D, drone trips are represented as operations associated with the truck route
(
Agatz et al. 2018
)
.
The FSTSP and TSP-D literature shows that simultaneous drone and truck operation boosts routing efficiency and cuts idle time. In practice, however, traffic variability and unpredictable road conditions complicate precise rendezvous timing, introducing significant uncertainty. To keep the problem tractable, most studies therefore focus on a single truck–single drone pairing, sidestepping the added complexity of larger fleets.
A natural extension considers multiple drones carried by one truck. Some approaches require the truck to wait at a launch location until all dispatched drones return
(
Moshref-Javadi and Lee 2017
)
, whereas others extend TSP- or FSTSP-based formulations to multiple drones
(
Cavani et al. 2021
,
Murray and Raj 2020
)
.
With multiple trucks and drones, solving the resulting problem to optimality becomes substantially more challenging. Recent exact methods for related truck–drone variants exploit route-space formulations within branch-price-and-cut or branch-and-price frameworks
(
Xia et al. 2023
,
Li and Wang 2023
,
Yin et al. 2023
,
Joo and Lee 2025
,
Schmidt et al. 2025
)
. Depending on the problem setting, truck–drone synchronization may occur at customer locations
(
Schmidt et al. 2025
)
or along truck arcs
(
Li et al. 2022
)
.
An alternative architecture that reduces synchronization requirements uses fixed service hubs. Trucks transport package-equipped drones to intermediate hubs, from which the drones are dispatched to customers
(
Kim and Moon 2018
,
Pinto and Lagorio 2022
,
Xia et al. 2023
)
.
Wang and Sheu (2019)
introduced drone stations at locations distinct from customer delivery sites. These stations support landing, recharging, and transfer operations while maintaining both active and backup drones. If a truck arrives before its assigned drone, it can continue with a backup unit, while the delayed drone becomes the reserve for a later sortie. Although this arrangement increases operational flexibility, it requires capital investment and ongoing maintenance. High delivery volumes and concentrated urban demand can help amortize these costs, whereas permanent facilities are harder to justify in rural regions with sparse demand, dispersed customers, and limited access to suitable sites with power and utilities.
Kang and Lee (2021)
considered a “wait-and-return” strategy in which a truck carrying multiple drones parks at a waiting node, dispatches drones to nearby customers, and remains there until all drones return. This arrangement avoids moving truck–drone rendezvous and does not require permanent drone stations. Instead, visited truck stops act as temporary hubs for drone launch and retrieval. Such an arrangement is well suited to rural delivery because it can serve dispersed customers without requiring fixed drone infrastructure.
We propose the Truck-as-Hub Vehicle Routing Problem with Drones in Rural Areas (TH-VRPD), in which trucks act as temporary mobile hubs at a set of predesignated synchronization pads.
These pads are passive stopping locations, such as rural parking spaces, roadside pull-outs, farm lay-bys, or community stopping locations, and contain no permanent drone resources.
When a truck visits a pad, it may launch multiple onboard drones simultaneously to serve nearby customers and remains at the pad until all dispatched drones return before continuing its route.
The TH-VRPD is related to the wait-and-return setting of
Kang and Lee (2021)
, but differs in that drone launch and retrieval are restricted to exogenous non-customer synchronization pads instead of being permitted at arbitrary truck stops. This restriction captures rural settings in which drone operations require suitable stopping locations without relying on permanent drone facilities.

Another important distinction concerns the objective function. Common criteria include minimization of tour completion time or makespan
(
Wang et al. 2017
,
Poikonen et al. 2017
,
Agatz et al. 2018
)
and total operating cost
(
Murray and Chu 2015
,
Wang and Sheu 2019
,
Nguyen et al. 2022
,
Tamke and Buscher 2023
)
.
Subsequent studies have broadened this scope by introducing specialized performance metrics, such as energy consumption minimization
(
Kuo et al. 2023
)
, reduction of customer service latency
(
Moshref-Javadi and Lee 2017
)
, and maximization of customer coverage
(
Ulmer and Thomas 2018
)
. However, these objectives do not guarantee minimal disparities in customer service levels (e.g., waiting time). Nevertheless, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no existing work explicitly addresses service-level disparities, e.g., variability in customer waiting times, within the truck–drone last-mile delivery framework.
This shortfall is particularly acute in sparsely populated rural areas, where the long distances between hubs or depots and remote customers tend to bias service quality in favor of locations nearer the depot. A U.S. Postal Service report on low-density delivery zones notes that “carriers may spend a great deal of time driving down country roads to deliver to the most remote locales,” thereby imposing longer waits on those at the periphery
(
United States Postal Service Office of Inspector
General 2020
)
. To address these service disparities, we formulate the TH-VRPD as a mixed-integer quadratic program (MIQP) with a normalized weighted objective that combines squared customer delivery delay, total truck return time, and operating cost.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
•
We introduce the Truck-as-Hub Vehicle Routing Problem with Drones in Rural Areas (TH-VRPD), in which trucks act as temporary mobile hubs at passive, predesignated synchronization pads. We formulate the problem as an arc-based mixed-integer quadratic program that jointly considers customer-level delivery delays, truck return times, and operating costs. In particular, the quadratic delay term discourages disproportionately poor service to remote customers and promotes more balanced service levels across rural regions.
•
We construct a canonical transformed network that provides a cost-preserving elementary-path representation of each feasible truck–drone route and yields an equivalent route-space set-partitioning formulation.
•
We develop an exact branch-price-and-cut algorithm for the route-space formulation. The algorithm combines tailored forward label-setting pricing with dominance and safe reduced-cost pruning, subset-row inequalities, and branching rules that preserve the pricing structure; parallel pricing with dynamic route-space repartitioning is used to accelerate the exact search.

A forward label records its partial path, covered customers, visited physical nodes, payload, timing, active pad, drone count, SR counters, branch state, and reduced cost.

4.3.1
Farkas pricing.
For an infeasible RMP, the same feasible-extension machinery searches for a route violating its Farkas certificate. A violating route is inserted; the full node master is declared infeasible only when no such route exists. Farkas reduced costs contain extreme rays, requiring the separate dominance and pruning conditions in Appendix
B
.
4.3.2
Parallel and primal procedures.
Pricing initially partitions routes by their first physical node and assigns the subsets to different parallel processes using a workload score. Dynamic splitting transfers complete open-label states to idle processes; all processes use the same dual solution, cuts, and branching restrictions. Closure requires all tasks to be exhausted or safely closed, with no workload transfer pending. For the primal procedures, a time-limited solve of the compact formulation supplies initial route columns when available. During the BPC search, the integer set-partitioning model is also solved over the accumulated branch-feasible route pool to obtain improved incumbent solutions. These scheduling and primal procedures are specified in Appendix
C
.

6
Conclusions
This paper introduces the Truck-as-Hub Vehicle Routing Problem with Drones in Rural Areas (TH-VRPD), in which trucks act as temporary mobile hubs at passive, predesignated synchronization pads. At each visited pad, a truck may dispatch multiple onboard drones simultaneously and must wait until all return before continuing its route. We formulate an arc-based mixed-integer quadratic program that jointly considers customer delivery delay, truck return time, and operating cost, with the quadratic delay term discouraging disproportionately poor service to remote customers.
To solve the problem exactly, we construct a canonical transformed network that provides a cost-preserving elementary-path representation of feasible truck–drone routes and an equivalent route-space set-partitioning formulation. Based on this representation, we develop an exact branch-price-and-cut (BPC) algorithm with tailored forward label-setting pricing, dominance and safe reduced-cost pruning, valid inequalities, and parallel pricing with dynamic route-space repartitioning.
Computational experiments show that BPC’s advantage grows with problem size. BPC proves optimality for all medium-scale and six of eight large-scale instances, with gaps of 1.81% and 3.58% on the remaining two, whereas the compact MIQP reaches the 1,800-second limit on all large instances. Sensitivity results show that higher drone costs shift service toward trucks and increase delays, particularly for spatially dispersed customers. They also show that customer delivery delay and truck return time induce distinct operating decisions, supporting explicit consideration of customer-level delay.
Several extensions merit further study. Jointly optimizing synchronization-pad locations and truck–drone routes would extend the framework toward integrated network design. Incorporating uncertain travel times and heterogeneous drone fleets would further improve the applicability of the TH-VRPD to realistic rural delivery operations.
