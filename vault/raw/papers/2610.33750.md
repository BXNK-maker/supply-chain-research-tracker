Abstract
Supply chain resilience research has traditionally viewed defensive actions such as safety stock, backup suppliers, expedited transportation, and emergency sourcing as beneficial. However, disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2021 semiconductor shortage, and the Suez Canal blockage highlight how synchronized hoarding, duplicate ordering, and capacity reservation can amplify congestion and operational costs, sometimes worsening the original disruption.
Motivated by this observation, we develop a decision-theoretic framework in which Protective Response Intensity (PRI) is an endogenous decision variable. We introduce Response-Induced Loss (RIL) to capture costs generated by defensive actions and characterize a unique optimal response that balances disruption mitigation against self-induced losses. We derive a closed-form critical response threshold beyond which RIL exceeds the original disruption intensity, a phenomenon termed a *Supply Chain Cytokine Storm*. We further propose the Cytokine Storm Index (CSI), measuring response-induced loss relative to the original disruption, and discuss its potential calibration using operational data.
Extending the framework to interconnected supply networks, we derive a spectral stability condition distinguishing bounded decentralized responses from network-wide propagation. Numerical experiments show that optimal responses remain below the storm threshold, whereas proportional response heuristics can exceed it. Network responses converge or diverge according to the derived stability condition. Overall, our findings highlight that supply chain resilience depends not only on mitigating disruptions but also on keeping defensive responses proportionate to the shocks they are intended to address.
Keywords:
Supply chain resilience; behavioral overreaction; response-induced loss; network cascades; convex optimization.

Introduction
Supply chain resilience is traditionally viewed through a simple principle: greater preparedness leads to lower disruption losses. Consequently, firms invest in safety stock, backup suppliers, flexible capacity, expedited transportation, and emergency sourcing to improve their ability to withstand unexpected disruptions
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Implicit in this perspective is the assumption that defensive actions are inherently beneficial and that increasing preparedness can only improve resilience.
Recent large-scale disruptions suggest that this assumption is incomplete. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the global semiconductor shortage, and the Suez Canal blockage, firms responded to uncertainty with synchronized hoarding, duplicate ordering, capacity reservation, and aggressive expediting. Although these actions were individually rational, their collective effect amplified congestion, prolonged shortages, and increased operational costs well beyond those caused by the original disruption itself
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
15
]
. These episodes indicate that supply chains may suffer not only from external shocks, but also from excessive defensive responses.
This phenomenon closely resembles a
cytokine storm
in immunology, where the immune system’s response to a pathogen becomes so aggressive that it causes more damage than the infection itself
[
8
,
9
]
. Motivated by this analogy, we investigate an analogous question for supply chains:
Can protective actions themselves become the dominant source of operational loss?
. This analogy is used for motivating the intuition behind the proposed framework.
To address this question, we model a firm’s
Protective Response Intensity
(PRI) as a decision variable rather than a fixed parameter and explicitly account for the costs generated by defensive actions through the notion of
Response-Induced Loss
(RIL). Building on this formulation, we introduce the
Cytokine Storm Index
(CSI), which quantifies the proportion of loss attributable to the response itself, derive a closed-form threshold distinguishing beneficial defensive actions from excessive ones, and extend the framework to interconnected supply networks by establishing a spectral condition governing whether defensive responses remain localized or propagate throughout the network.
Our work differs from existing resilience and behavioral supply chain literature in three important respects. First, unlike traditional resilience models that treat preparedness as uniformly beneficial
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, we explicitly model protective actions as optimization decisions that balance mitigation benefits against self-induced operational costs. Second, whereas the bullwhip-effect literature demonstrates how ordering variability propagates through supply chains
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, we derive a closed-form analytical condition identifying when defensive responses themselves become counterproductive. Third, by extending the analysis to interconnected supply networks, we show that excessive responses are not solely firm-level phenomena but are fundamentally shaped by network interactions, linking resilience analysis with spectral methods commonly used in network science
[
10
,
11
]
.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows. We formulate a decision-theoretic framework that models Protective Response Intensity (PRI) as an endogenous decision variable and introduces Response-Induced Loss (RIL) to explicitly capture the operational costs generated by defensive actions. Building on this framework, we establish the existence and uniqueness of the optimal response, derive its comparative statics, characterize a closed-form threshold separating beneficial protection from excessive defensive behavior, and propose the Cytokine Storm Index (CSI) as a quantitative measure of self-induced operational loss. Finally, we extend the analysis to interconnected supply networks by deriving a spectral stability condition governing the propagation of defensive responses and demonstrate the resulting threshold and cascade phenomena through numerical experiments.

Related Work
Resilience research typically models a firm’s or network’s ability to absorb, adapt to, and recover from disruptions, with defensive investment entering as a parameter that improves absorption or shortens recovery time
[
1
,
2
,
3

[

These models describe a single firm acting against a well-defined risk reasonably well, but they share an implicit assumption that resilience-enhancing investment is weakly beneficial, leaving no room for the response itself to become a source of loss.
A second stream shows that overreaction can emerge even without any individual decision-maker behaving irrationally. The bullwhip effect literature shows that order variance amplifies upstream, driven by demand-signal processing, order batching, price fluctuation, and rationing, even under individually optimal ordering policies
[
4
]
. Laboratory studies using the beer distribution game show that human subjects systematically over-order relative to the optimal benchmark, especially just after a shock
[
5
,
6
]
. This literature establishes overreaction as real and measurable, but characterizes it indirectly, through order-variance decompositions under a fixed ordering policy, rather than by asking when a
chosen
level of responsiveness becomes counterproductive; no closed-form threshold analogous to ours appears in this line of work. Nor does this literature offer a metric comparable to CSI: standard resilience diagnostics such as fill rate, time-to-recovery, or bullwhip order-variance ratios summarize how well a system absorbs or reacts to a shock, but to the best of our knowledge, these studies do not isolate the fraction of loss that the response itself creates. CSI is intended to fill exactly that gap, and comparing it empirically against these established metrics is a natural next step we flag in Section
6
.
A third stream draws on immune-inspired methods and network cascades. Artificial Immune Systems have long served as metaheuristics for supplier selection, scheduling, and routing
[
7
]
, and terms like “organizational immunity” occasionally appear in the practitioner literature as informal metaphors for adaptive capacity, stopping short of formalizing an
overactive
response. Separately, network science and epidemiology use spectral conditions to determine whether a linear process on a network converges or diverges: a threshold of the form

Taken together, these streams motivate the need for a unified decision-theoretic perspective. While existing studies have substantially advanced our understanding of disruption propagation, resilience strategies, and behavioral amplification, relatively less attention has been devoted to characterizing when protective actions themselves become counterproductive. To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of an analytical framework that explicitly identifies a critical threshold between beneficial protection and self-induced operational loss, while also examining how such behavior propagates over supply networks. Network Extension
The analysis in the previous sections considers a single decision maker that chooses its protective response independently. In real supply chains, however, firms rarely operate in isolation. A firm’s defensive actions—such as inventory hoarding, emergency sourcing, or aggressive capacity reservation can alter the operating environment faced by its suppliers and customers, often inducing them to increase their own defensive responses. Consequently, overreaction is not merely an individual optimization problem but also a network phenomenon in which local decisions may propagate throughout the supply chain.
To capture this mechanism, we extend the proposed framework from a single firm to a network of interacting firms. Rather than developing a full multi-agent game in which firms jointly optimize their responses, which will be a part of future work, we isolate the propagation mechanism through which protective responses spread across supply chain relationships. The network model is therefore a reduced-form behavioral propagation model rather than a strategic equilibrium. The complete multi-agent game analysis of multiple firms is an interesting future direction.

In interconnected supply chains, resilience depends not only on how individual firms respond to disruptions but also on how those responses propagate through the network. Even a well-intentioned local reaction can escalate into a system-wide overreaction when amplified by highly connected partners. Consequently, managers should promote firm-level resilience initiatives with mechanisms that limit behavioral contagion, such as information sharing, coordinated decision-making, and greater supply chain visibility.
Taken together, the numerical results are consistent with the theoretical analysis. The optimal response remains below the cytokine storm threshold across the tested parameter range, whereas simple proportional response heuristics readily enter the storm region. At the network level, defensive responses either remain contained or propagate into system-wide overreaction depending on the spectral stability condition of Theorem

This observation is consistent with events following the Suez Canal blockage, where widespread inventory building, rerouting, and capacity reservation contributed to congestion and prolonged recovery across global supply chains

The framework yields four managerial implications: (i) the firms should optimize rather than maximize defensive actions, since marginal response costs eventually dominate mitigation benefits; (ii) monitor

6.5
Limitations
There are several limitations. First, the reduced-form parameters

\mu

Third, the network extension is a reduced-form behavioral propagation model rather than a strategic equilibrium, and its numerical illustration uses a single representative network realization rather than a systematic sweep across topologies. Finally, the network extension assumes static topology and exogenous disruption characteristics; dynamic supply networks with jointly optimized defensive responses is an interesting direction for future research.
7
Conclusion
We develop a decision-theoretic framework for analyzing when protective actions in supply chains become counterproductive. By introducing the concepts of
Protective Response Intensity
(PRI),
Response-Induced Loss
(RIL), and the
Cytokine Storm Index
(CSI), the proposed framework explicitly distinguishes losses caused by external disruptions from those generated by excessive defensive responses. The analysis establishes a unique optimal response, derives a closed-form threshold separating proportionate and excessive defensive actions, and extends the framework to interconnected supply networks through a spectral stability condition governing the propagation of behavioral responses.
The numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical results. The optimal response remains below the cytokine storm threshold, whereas simple proportional response heuristics readily enter the storm region. The network experiments further illustrate how behavioral responses either remain localized or propagate into system-wide overreaction depending on the underlying network interactions.
Overall, the proposed framework suggests that effective supply chain resilience depends not only on mitigating external disruptions but also on regulating the intensity of defensive responses.
